Property insurance is built on a single distinction: it pays for things that **happen** and excludes things that **wear out**. On a roof that distinction is often obvious. On masonry it is not — which is why so many masonry claims turn into an argument, and why the argument is usually settled by physical evidence rather than opinion.

The useful news for owners, managers and adjusters is that **masonry keeps a fairly honest record.** A wall that failed in an hour looks materially different from a wall that failed over thirty winters, and the differences are readable if somebody gets close enough to read them.

## The Physical Tells

### Fracture faces

The single most useful indicator. A brick or stone broken by sudden force leaves a **clean, sharp, unweathered fracture face** — often a different colour from the exposed surface, because it has never seen weather. A unit that failed through freeze-thaw shows **rounded, dirty, weathered** break edges that have been exposed for seasons.

### Mortar condition at the failure

Impact and wind break the **bond** while frequently leaving the mortar itself hard. Deterioration leaves mortar **sandy, powdery and receded** — you can rake it out with a key. Hard mortar with a broken bond points to an event; soft mortar points to time.

### Pattern and direction

Storm damage is **directional**. It concentrates on the elevation and roof slope facing the wind, aligns with a debris path, and often shows a strike point. Weathering is **exposure-driven** — worst on south and west faces, at parapets, chimneys and base courses, distributed across the whole building rather than concentrated along a line.

### Displacement vs. erosion

Sudden events **move** masonry: units displaced, coping shifted, a stack toppled or rotated. Deterioration **removes** material gradually — joints recede, faces spall, profiles round off. A displaced coping stone with sound bedding mortar underneath is a very different finding from one sitting on crumbled mortar.

### Efflorescence and staining

White salt bloom and established staining tracks take **time** to develop. Their presence at a failure point indicates water has been moving through that masonry well before the event in question. Their absence at a fresh break supports a sudden cause.

### Biological growth and dirt

Moss, algae and accumulated soiling in a crack or on a fracture face mean the opening predates the storm by a long way.

### Debris and where it landed

Fresh debris in a distinct pattern — on a roof slope, in a valley, across a walkway — is event evidence. Mortar crumbs accumulated in a gutter over years are not.

## Side by Side

| Evidence | Points to a sudden event | Points to deterioration |
|---|---|---|
| **Fracture face** | Clean, sharp, unweathered | Rounded, dirty, weathered |
| **Mortar at the break** | Hard, bond broken | Sandy, receded, crumbling |
| **Pattern** | Directional, localised, follows a strike or wind path | Exposure-driven, distributed, worst at parapet/base |
| **Masonry position** | Displaced, rotated, missing | In place but eroded and spalled |
| **Efflorescence** | Absent at the break | Present, established |
| **Growth/soiling in cracks** | Absent | Present |
| **Debris** | Fresh, patterned, dated to the event | Accumulated over seasons |
| **Interior symptoms** | New, sudden onset | Recurring, historically reported |

## Most Buildings Show Both

This is the part that gets flattened in adversarial claims, and it should not be. The common real-world case is a building with **long-standing deterioration** on which a **storm caused specific, identifiable damage** — a weakened chimney that a wind event finally brought down, or a parapet with eroded joints that lost three courses in a gust.

A competent assessment separates them **by location and mechanism** rather than characterising an entire elevation one way. That produces an accurate scope, which serves the owner, the adjuster and the eventual repair better than an all-or-nothing characterisation that neither side can defend.

It is also why maintenance matters to claims. An insurer examining whether pre-existing condition contributed has a much harder argument against a building with documented upkeep than one with a decade of visible neglect.

## Element by Element: What the Evidence Usually Looks Like

### Chimneys and stacks

The most common masonry claim in this region. A wind-toppled stack shows **displacement or rotation**, clean breaks at the failure course, and debris in a directional pattern on the roof or ground. A stack that failed by deterioration shows **sandy joints throughout**, spalled faces, a cracked crown that has been feeding water down the flue for years, and material loss without displacement. Many real cases show a deteriorated stack that a storm finished — and the assessment should say exactly that.

### Parapets and coping

Storm evidence is **shifted or missing coping units** with sound bedding mortar underneath, or a section pushed out of plane along the wind path. Deterioration evidence is **open coping joints, eroded bedding mortar, efflorescence** on the back face, and a wall that has gone gradually out of plumb.

### Brick faces

Hail and impact leave **fresh, localised, directional** damage on exposed elevations, with clean fracture faces and dust rather than weathered residue. Freeze-thaw spalling is **distributed by exposure**, concentrated in the base courses and on wet elevations, with faces that have clearly been off for some time.

### Lintels and openings

Almost always deterioration. **Rust jacking** — corroding steel expanding and lifting the masonry above it — is a multi-year process, and the orange staining, opened horizontal joint and lifted course all develop slowly. A storm rarely damages a lintel; it may expose one that had already failed.

### Interior symptoms

**New and sudden** water intrusion after a documented event supports a claim. **Recurring** intrusion, historic stains, and previously reported complaints indicate an existing path — and property management records frequently settle this question faster than the wall does.

Separate from coverage, the repair itself is priced on the usual masonry variables:

## What to Do in the First 48 Hours

1. **Make it safe.** Cordon anything that could fall — over a sidewalk, entrance, patio or parking stall. This precedes everything.
2. **Photograph before anything moves.** Multiple distances and angles, debris where it landed, the direction of fall, interior damage, timestamps. Cleanup destroys evidence permanently.
3. **Protect against further damage.** Temporary tarping and covering of open masonry. Most policies expect reasonable steps to prevent additional loss, and in this climate an open wall deteriorates fast.
4. **Keep receipts** for emergency work and materials.
5. **Notify the insurer promptly** — policies contain notice requirements.
6. **Get a masonry assessment**, from staging or a lift rather than the ground.
7. **Do not start permanent repairs** before the claim position is established, beyond what safety and mitigation require.
8. **Read anything before signing it**, particularly documents that assign claim benefits.

## Build the Baseline Before You Need It

The cheapest thing an owner or board can do for a future claim costs nothing but an afternoon:

- **Dated photographs** of every elevation, the parapet and the chimneys, every year or two
- **Written inspection reports** kept with the property records
- **Invoices and scopes** from prior masonry work, including the mortar specification
- **A record of maintenance** — tuckpointing cycles, sealant renewal, gutter clearing

That file is what turns "the wall was fine before the storm" from an assertion into evidence. For associations it also supports the reserve conversation, as covered in [HOA masonry maintenance budgets](/blog/hoa-masonry-maintenance-budgets).

## Chicagoland Context

The region produces both halves of this problem generously. **Straight-line wind events, derechos, spring and summer hail, and severe thunderstorm gusts** supply the sudden causes, and they hit chimneys and parapets hardest because those are the most exposed, least supported masonry on any building.

Meanwhile the climate supplies the gradual cause continuously: dozens of **freeze-thaw cycles** each winter, water entering a joint, freezing, expanding roughly nine percent, and forcing the masonry apart — the mechanism set out in [freeze-thaw masonry damage in Chicago](/blog/freeze-thaw-masonry-damage-chicago). Add a century of soft common brick and lime mortar across the older stock, plus heavy de-icing salt at grade, and most Chicagoland buildings carry real deterioration before any storm arrives.

Which is precisely why the separation has to be done building by building, on evidence.

## The Short Version

Insurance separates events from wearing out, and masonry records the difference honestly if you look closely enough. **Clean fracture faces, hard mortar with broken bond, directional patterns and displaced units** point to an event. **Rounded erosion, sandy mortar, efflorescence, growth in cracks and exposure-driven distribution** point to time.

Most buildings show both. The job of an assessment is not to pick a side — it is to say precisely which is where, and let the policy and the adjuster do the rest.
