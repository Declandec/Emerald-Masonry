## How Long Does Masonry Work Actually Take?

**Most residential masonry repairs involve days rather than weeks of actual work** — spot repointing one to three days, a full-house repoint roughly three days to two weeks, a chimney rebuild a few days, lintel replacement about a day per opening. **But the calendar is always longer than the labor**, because setup, access, joint preparation, material sourcing, weather windows and mortar cure all consume time without looking like progress. These are working days on site, not calendar weeks. The gap between the two is the part worth understanding.

## Where the Calendar Actually Goes

### Setup and access
On a two-story house, staging goes up before any mortar is touched. On a commercial building, scaffolding or a swing stage can take days to erect and permit — and on downtown work, sidewalk protection and street permitting come first. **Access is frequently the largest single line item in the estimate, and a proportional share of the schedule.**

### Protection
Landscaping, windows, walkways, the neighbor's property on a city lot. Done before work starts, restored after.

### Joint preparation — the invisible half
This is where homeowners think nothing is happening. Grinding or raking failed mortar out to a **sound depth — generally two to two and a half times the joint width** — then cleaning the joints so new mortar bonds to clean masonry, takes as long or longer than packing the new mortar in.

**On soft historic brick it takes longer still**, because joints are hand-raked rather than power-ground to avoid widening them and chewing the brick edges.

Skip this and the job goes twice as fast and fails in three years.

### Material sourcing
Matched **salvaged brick** for a pre-1940 wall depends on what the yards have, and a difficult match can add real lead time. Custom-blended lime mortar takes formulation and test batches. Replication of ornamental stone or terra cotta is manufactured to order.

### Cure and protection
Mortar sets in hours but keeps gaining strength for days and weeks — and **lime-based mortars cure more slowly than Portland-based**. What matters practically is protecting fresh joints from freezing, driving rain and direct hot sun during the early period.

### Discovery
Masonry is the trade where opening a wall reveals more than the estimate assumed. A rusting lintel found behind the brick above a window, or spalling worse than it looked from the ground, legitimately extends scope and schedule.

## What Each Day Actually Looks Like

A full-house repointing job, so you can see where the time goes rather than wondering why nobody appears to be laying mortar.

**Day one — arrival and setup.** Crews arrive, walk the elevations with the homeowner, confirm the scope, and begin protection: landscaping covered, windows masked, walkways cleared, and the neighbor's side protected on a tight lot. Staging or ladders go up on the first elevation. Very little mortar is touched. This looks like the slowest day and it is setting up everything after it.

**Days two through four — grinding and raking.** The loud, dusty, apparently destructive phase, and the one homeowners find most alarming. Failed mortar is cut out to sound depth across the elevation. The wall looks worse than when the crew arrived, because it is temporarily an open-jointed wall. **This is the step that determines whether the repair lasts**, and it is the first thing a rushed bid shortens.

**Ongoing — cleaning the joints.** Dust and loose material blown or brushed out so fresh mortar bonds to clean masonry rather than to debris. Fast, easy to skip, and skipping it undermines everything.

**Days three onward, overlapping — mixing and matching.** Mortar batched to the matched formulation, with test areas checked against the existing wall once cured, since mortar dries lighter than it looks wet.

**Days four through eight — repointing.** Mortar packed into the joints in lifts and compacted so no voids remain behind the face. This is the part that looks like masonry work, and it is a minority of the schedule.

**Trailing each section — tooling.** Joints struck to match the original profile at the right firmness. Timing matters: too soon and the mortar smears, too late and it will not tool cleanly. Crews work back across sections as each reaches the right state.

**Final days — cleaning and cure protection.** The wall is brushed down, mortar smears cleaned off the brick faces, protection installed if weather demands it, staging struck, site restored, and finished work photographed.

**After the crew leaves — cure.** Nothing visible happens and the job is still finishing. Fresh joints keep gaining strength for weeks and need protecting from freezing, driving rain and direct hot sun during the early period.

Add a rain day or two, and a two-week calendar for eight working days is completely normal.

## Weather Runs the Calendar

More than any trade except roofing.

**Cold.** Mortar must cure above freezing. Cold-weather work is possible with **tenting, heat and mix adjustments**, but it costs more, moves slower, and a hard freeze on green mortar can ruin the work outright. Most Chicagoland masonry is scheduled for milder months for that reason.

**Rain.** Wet masonry cannot be repointed, and fresh joints need protection from driving rain. A wet week simply stops the job.

**Heat.** Extreme heat and direct sun flash-dry mortar before it cures properly, which weakens the bond. Hot-weather work means adjusted mixes, damp curing and sometimes shifted hours.

**And the season is why lead times exist.** Late spring through fall is both the best working window and the busiest booking period. Homeowners who wait until they see a problem in October are competing for the last slots before cold weather.

## Estimate to Start: The Other Half of the Wait

The schedule people ask about is the work. The one that surprises them is **getting on the calendar**.

Peak-season lead times run longer than most homeowners expect, and the honest advice is to **get an assessment before you have an urgent problem**. An assessment costs nothing, tells you what is genuinely urgent versus what can wait a season, and lets you book a slot rather than take whatever is left.

## Permits Add Calendar, Not Labor

Requirements vary by municipality and by scope. Routine repointing frequently needs no permit at all, while **structural work, chimney rebuilds, foundation work and some facade repairs** generally do — and **landmark or historic-district properties add a review step** on top of the permit.

None of that is labor, but all of it is calendar. Review timelines are set by the village or city, not by your contractor, and a historic commission that meets monthly can add weeks by itself. On downtown commercial work, **street-side permitting for sidewalk protection or a lift** is a separate approval again.

The practical advice: ask at the estimate stage whether your scope needs a permit and whether the property carries any designation. A contractor working in your town should know immediately, and the answer belongs in the proposal rather than surfacing after you have signed.

## Residential vs. Commercial

Commercial and association work runs longer for reasons that have little to do with masonry:

- **Access** is more involved — swing stage, scaffold, lifts, permits.
- **Occupancy** means phasing around tenants, business hours, congregations or classes.
- **Public protection** — walkway canopies, netting, pedestrian routing.
- **Coordination** with roofing and other trades on shared details like parapet and coping work.
- **Approvals** — boards, property managers and reserve budgets add decision time before mobilization.

A phased facade project on an occupied building is often deliberately spread across weeks or split across budget years. That is not slowness; it is how you keep a building operating.

## What a Fast Bid Is Usually Skipping

If one contractor quotes half the time everyone else does, the difference is almost always one of these:

- **Shallow joint preparation** — skimming mortar over old joints instead of cutting to sound depth.
- **Power grinding soft historic brick** instead of hand-raking, which is faster and permanently damages the wall.
- **A generic bagged mortar** instead of a matched mix.
- **No water-source correction** — repointing while the failed sill or chimney crown keeps feeding the wall.
- **Skipping protection and cure management.**
- **Less scope than you think** — one elevation priced, not four.

Speed in masonry is almost never efficiency. It is omission.

## How to Plan Your Project

1. **Get the assessment early**, ideally the season before you intend to do the work.
2. **Ask for the scope itemized by element** so you can phase if you want to.
3. **Ask what the access plan is** and how long setup takes — it is a real part of the schedule.
4. **Ask about material lead time**, especially if brick matching is involved.
5. **Book the season, not the week.** Weather will move exact dates.
6. **Expect discovery** and ask what would trigger a change order.
7. **Plan around cure**, not just around work — fresh masonry needs protection after the crew leaves.

## The Bottom Line

The work itself is usually shorter than people fear. The calendar around it — setup, sourcing, weather, cure — is longer than people expect, and most of that time is not padding. It is the difference between a repair that disappears into your wall for decades and one that shows up as a band of spalling in five years.

Plan a season ahead, ask what the access and preparation actually involve, and be suspicious of anyone promising to be finished by Friday.
