# Why Your Chimney Started Crumbling After You Got a New Furnace

If your brick chimney started shedding mortar, flaking brick or white streaks a few winters after you put in a **high-efficiency furnace**, the furnace is very likely part of the reason. Modern high-efficiency furnaces vent through plastic pipe out the side of the house, so the old chimney is left venting only a **gas water heater — an "orphaned" water heater — or nothing at all**. The small amount of exhaust no longer warms the oversized flue, the water vapor in it **condenses inside the chimney**, and that wet, slightly acidic moisture breaks down mortar, flue tiles and brick through every Chicago freeze-thaw cycle. > A chimney that's suddenly crumbling after a furnace replacement usually isn't a coincidence. The chimney was built to be warm, and now it runs cold and wet.

This guide explains what changed, how to spot the damage, who fixes which part, and what the masonry repair looks like.

## How a chimney used to work — and what changed

For most of the last century, a typical Chicago-area house had a gas furnace and a gas water heater both venting into the same brick chimney. Their hot exhaust warmed the flue quickly, created a steady upward draft and carried the water vapor that burning gas produces up and out before it could condense.

Then came **high-efficiency (condensing) furnaces**. They pull so much heat out of the exhaust that what's left is cool and wet, so it's vented through PVC pipe out a side wall rather than up the chimney. That's a good upgrade for the heating bill. But the chimney stays — and now it has one of two jobs:

| Situation | What's happening in the chimney | Typical result |
|---|---|---|
| **Furnace and water heater both vent up the chimney** (older setup) | Plenty of hot exhaust, warm flue | Normal wear |
| **Only the water heater vents up the chimney** (orphaned) | Small exhaust volume in a flue sized for two appliances; flue stays cool | Condensation inside the flue, wet mortar and brick |
| **Nothing vents up the chimney** (fully abandoned) | No heat at all; chimney is just a brick tower in the weather | Freeze-thaw damage, often uncapped and neglected |

### Why condensation does so much damage

Burning natural gas produces water vapor. When that vapor hits a cold flue wall, it condenses — just like breath on a cold window. That condensate is mildly acidic. It soaks into the mortar joints and flue tiles, and on cold days it can freeze inside the masonry. Over a few winters, the mortar softens and washes out, the clay liner cracks and flakes, and moisture works outward through the brick until it shows up on the outside of the chimney.

The damage is usually worst at the **top of the chimney**, where the flue is coldest and the brick is most exposed. See [why the top of your chimney fails first](/blog/why-the-top-of-your-chimney-fails-first).

## Warning signs of condensation damage in a chimney

### White streaks or efflorescence on the chimney

White, chalky staining on the outside of the chimney — especially near the top or on the side facing away from the sun — is salts carried out by moisture moving through the brick. On a chimney that used to look clean, it's one of the earliest signs. Read [why efflorescence keeps coming back](/blog/why-efflorescence-keeps-coming-back).

### Crumbling or missing mortar joints

Joints that are soft, sandy, recessed or missing altogether, particularly in the top few feet of the chimney.

### Spalling or flaking brick

Brick faces popping off or flaking in layers. Once the brick itself is breaking down, the repair grows from repointing to brick replacement or a partial rebuild. See [what causes brick to spall](/blog/what-causes-brick-to-spall-illinois).

### Debris at the cleanout or the appliance connection

Pieces of clay tile, mortar or rust collecting at the chimney's cleanout door in the basement, or around the vent connector of the water heater.

### Stains inside the house

Water stains, peeling paint or damp drywall on ceilings and walls around the chimney, or in the attic where the chimney passes through.

### A crumbling chimney base in the basement

Wet, salty, crumbling brick and mortar at the base of the chimney in the basement often points to moisture running down inside the flue. Our guide to [a crumbling chimney base in the basement](/blog/crumbling-chimney-base-in-basement-causes-repair-chicago) covers this in detail.

### A leaning chimney or loose top courses

Loose bricks at the top, a cracked crown or a chimney that has started to lean need attention right away. See [why brick chimneys lean](/blog/why-brick-chimneys-lean-pull-away-from-house).

## Is this a safety problem?

It can be. A deteriorating liner, debris that partly blocks the flue, or poor draft from an oversized flue can all affect how well the water heater vents. **That part is not a masonry question.** If you have an orphaned water heater, have a qualified **chimney professional or HVAC contractor** inspect the flue and the venting, and make sure you have working carbon monoxide detectors. A mason's job is the brick, mortar, crown and structure — and to tell you when we see signs that the venting needs a look. ## Fix the cause first: liner, venting or retirement

Repairing the masonry without dealing with the cause means the damage comes back. There are three common paths, and the right one depends on your appliances:

1. **The water heater still uses the chimney.** The usual fix is a **properly sized liner** for the water heater, installed by a chimney or HVAC professional, so the exhaust stays warm enough to vent without soaking the masonry.
2. **The water heater is being replaced with a power-vent or tankless unit that vents through the wall.** The chimney then has no job, and you can decide whether to keep it, cap it or take it down.
3. **The chimney is already unused.** It still needs a sound crown and a cap to keep rain out — or it can be lowered below the roofline or removed. See [should I remove my chimney instead of repairing it?](/blog/should-i-remove-my-chimney-instead-of-repairing)

## How we repair condensation-damaged chimney masonry

Once the venting question is settled, the masonry repair follows a standard sequence:

1. **Inspect from the roof and the basement** — mortar, brick, crown, flashing, cap, and the base of the chimney.
2. **Decide repair vs rebuild** for the section above the roof. Sound brick with failed joints gets repointed; badly spalled or loose brick gets replaced or rebuilt.
3. **Grind out the mortar joints** to a sufficient depth for the new mortar to bond properly and **tuckpoint** with matched mortar.
4. **Replace spalled brick** with matched units, or **rebuild** the top of the chimney course by course where needed.
5. **Build a new crown** with a proper overhang and drip edge so water sheds clear of the brick, and install or refit the **cap**.
6. **Reflash or reseal** where the chimney meets the roof if the flashing is part of the leak. See [chimney flashing leaks](/blog/chimney-flashing-leaks-chicago).
7. **Repair the base** in the basement if it has been damaged by moisture.

## Materials and techniques

| Item | What we use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Mortar** | Type N for most chimney repointing; Type S only where the exposure calls for it | Mortar should be softer than the brick so the joints wear, not the brick |
| **Brick** | Matched salvaged or new brick graded for severe weather | Chimney brick takes the hardest exposure on the house |
| **Crown** | Sloped crown with an overhang and drip edge | Throws water clear of the brick instead of down its face |
| **Cap** | Metal cap sized to the flue | Keeps rain, snow and animals out |
| **Sealer** | Breathable water repellent only where appropriate | A film sealer traps moisture in a chimney that's already wet inside |

## DIY vs hiring a pro

Chimney work is roof work, so the first issue is safety. Beyond that, the common DIY fixes make this particular problem worse: smearing new mortar over old joints doesn't bond, patching cracks with caulk traps moisture, and coating a wet chimney with a film-forming sealer keeps the condensation inside the brick. A mason grinds the joints out properly, matches the mortar to the brick, and builds a crown that actually sheds water. And because the cause is in the flue, you need someone who will tell you when the venting needs a chimney or HVAC professional rather than just patching the outside.

If your house fits that pattern and your chimney has started to change, it's worth a look before another winter.

## Maintenance and prevention

- **Look at your chimney every spring and fall** — top courses, crown, cap and the base in the basement.
- **Check the cleanout** for tile and mortar debris once a year.
- **Have the flue inspected** by a chimney or HVAC professional whenever you replace a furnace or water heater.
- **Keep a cap on it**, whether the chimney is used or not.
- **Repair the crown early** — a cracked crown lets water straight into the top of the chimney.
- **Don't seal a wet chimney** with a film-forming product.
- **Decide what to do with an unused chimney** instead of letting it deteriorate.
