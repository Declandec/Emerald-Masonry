## Why You Can't Get a Masonry Price Over the Phone

**Because the three things that determine the cost are all invisible from a description: how deep the damage actually goes, what mortar and brick the wall requires, and how crews will physically reach the work.** A contractor who commits to a firm number without seeing those has either padded it heavily to protect themselves, or intends to revise it once work is underway. This frustrates people, understandably. You can get a ballpark on a roof, a driveway, a furnace. Masonry is genuinely different, and it is worth understanding why rather than assuming the trade is being cagey.

## The Three Invisible Variables

### 1. How deep the damage goes

From the street, two walls can look identical. One needs **surface repointing**; the other needs joints ground to full depth, thirty spalled units cut out and replaced, and a corroding lintel addressed.

Nothing in a phone description distinguishes them. "The mortar looks bad" covers a modest maintenance job and a small restoration project equally well.

And masonry hides its worst news. **Rust jacking** — a steel lintel corroding and expanding inside a rigid wall — is invisible until the crack appears. Failed **ties** behind veneer cannot be seen at all. Deteriorated inner wythes in solid masonry show nothing from outside.

### 2. What the wall actually needs

**The governing rule in masonry is that mortar must be softer than the brick it holds.** Mortar is the sacrificial layer — it absorbs movement and moisture stress and wears out first, because replacing mortar is cheap and replacing century-old brick is not.

Satisfying that rule means knowing how soft **your** brick is. A 1958 ranch and an 1890s greystone need different mixes — **Type N** on one, **Type O** or a custom lime blend on the other — and getting it backwards spalls the brick faces within a few winters.

Then there is the brick itself. If units need replacing, is there a current-production match, or does it mean **salvaged brick** sourced from a yard? That is a real difference in both cost and lead time, and it cannot be determined over the phone.

### 3. How anyone reaches the work

**Access is frequently the largest single line item in a masonry estimate**, and it is entirely site-specific: ladders versus staging versus a lift versus a swing stage; a tight city gangway; landscaping and decks in the way; roof pitch on a chimney; sidewalk protection and permitting downtown.

Two identical chimneys, one on a walkable roof and one on a steep three-story pitch above a narrow side yard, are not the same job.

## What a Phone Quote Actually Means

If someone gives you a firm number without looking, one of three things is true.

**It is padded.** They have priced in the worst case to protect themselves, which means you are paying a premium for their uncertainty.

**It is going to change.** The number gets you to sign, then the scope "grows" once crews are on site. This is the more common version, and it is why change-order disputes are so frequent in this trade.

**It is for less work than you think.** Cheap because they priced one elevation, or surface repointing rather than proper preparation. This is the version that looks fine for a year and fails in four.

Occasionally a genuinely simple, well-described job can be ballparked honestly — a chimney cap, a single ground-floor sill. **The tell is not whether they give a number; it is whether they will commit to it in writing without having seen the wall.**

## What a Real Estimate Visit Covers

A serious assessment is not a five-minute glance from the driveway.

- **Every elevation**, not just the one you called about. Damage concentrates where water goes, and the wall you noticed is often downstream of a problem on another side.
- **The screwdriver test on the joints.** If nobody touches your mortar, nobody assessed it.
- **Eyes on the chimney**, from the roof where feasible. Crown cracking, flashing condition and top-course deterioration are invisible from below.
- **Gutters, downspouts and grade.** Not masonry work, but usually the water source driving everything else.
- **Sills, lintels and openings**, looking for rust staining and cracking.
- **Photographs**, so you can see the roof and the north elevation you cannot reach.
- **A ranking of what is urgent versus what can wait a season.**

**An assessment that finds everything urgent is a sales call.** A real one tells you what to defer.

## What You Should Get Afterward

A written scope that states:

| Item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Elevations and elements included** — and excluded | "Tuckpointing as needed" is an argument, not a scope |
| **Mortar type and how it was matched** | The single most consequential technical decision |
| **Joint preparation depth and method** | Where cheap bids hide — grind or hand-rake, and how deep |
| **Joint profile** | Concave, weathered, raked — tooling makes a repair invisible |
| **Brick sourcing and matching** | Salvaged or current production |
| **What is being done about the water** | Repairs that ignore the source have a countdown on them |
| **Access method** | Frequently the largest cost, and the most common change order |
| **Insurance, warranty, payment schedule** | Standard, and revealing when missing |

**Bids that differ by a factor of three usually differ in scope, not price.** Line those columns up before comparing totals.

## What a Contractor *Can* Honestly Tell You by Phone

Declining to quote is not the same as declining to help. A good contractor can give you real, useful information on that first call — it just is not a number.

**Which category your problem is in.** Describe rust staining below a window and any competent mason will tell you it is likely a **lintel**, that lintel work is priced per opening, and that it is not something to sit on for three years. That is genuinely actionable.

**What drives your cost up or down.** Ask directly: *what would put my job at the expensive end?* Expect answers like height and staging, how hard the existing mortar is to grind out, whether brick needs matching from salvage, and whether the chimney is involved. Now you know what to look at.

**Relative scale.** Not a price, but a shape. Repointing one elevation, replacing a dozen brick, and rebuilding a chimney above the roofline occupy three clearly different tiers, and a contractor can place your likely job among them without committing to a figure.

**Whether it is urgent.** A leaning chimney, a bulging wall or fresh debris after a freeze should change how the call is handled. Receded joints should not. That triage is free and immediate.

**Whether they are the right trade at all.** Sometimes the honest phone answer is *that sounds like your gutters* or *that is a roofer*. We would rather say so than book a visit.

**What to have ready for the visit**, and roughly when they could schedule it — which in peak season is genuinely useful planning information.

### A reasonable way to ask

Rather than *what does tuckpointing cost*, try: **"What are the main things that would make my job expensive versus cheap, and which category do you think I'm in from what I've described?"**

That question gets you the substance without asking anyone to commit to a number they cannot stand behind — and how a contractor handles it tells you a great deal about how they will handle the estimate.

## Red Flags Worth Naming

- **A firm price quoted by phone** on a wall nobody has seen.
- **Square-foot pricing applied sight-unseen** — it prices area, not condition.
- **Pressure to sign today**, or a discount expiring this afternoon.
- **Door-knocking with "leftover materials"** from a nearby job. Established contractors schedule work in advance and do not have surplus mortar and brick to unload.
- **A large cash deposit** demanded before anything is on site.
- **No written scope** — just a number.
- **Sealing proposed instead of repointing.** Sealing a wall with open joints seals water in.
- **Sandblasting or high-pressure washing** proposed on older brick. Both strip the fired face and accelerate spalling.
- **Reluctance to provide a certificate of insurance.**
- **The same mortar mix for every house.** A contractor who never varies it is not matching anything.

## Getting Value From the Visit

1. **Give access to all four elevations**, including the side nobody walks down.
2. **Say when prior masonry work was done**, and by roughly whom — old repairs are diagnostic.
3. **Report where you have seen water inside**, even if it seems unrelated.
4. **Show photographs of seasonal conditions** the visit will miss — ice damming, spring seepage, summer algae.
5. **Know your build era**, roughly. It drives the mortar decision.
6. **Ask what is not included**, and what would trigger a change order.
7. **Ask what can wait.** The answer tells you a great deal about who you are dealing with.

## Why Online Cost Calculators Mislead

Search for masonry pricing and you will find per-square-foot calculators and national cost guides. They are not useless — they establish rough magnitude — but they systematically miss the things that actually move a Chicagoland number.

They assume **average condition**, which is precisely the variable in question. They rarely account for **access**, frequently the largest single line item. They almost never distinguish **mortar type**, so a soft-brick greystone and a modern veneer wall price identically when they should not. They do not know whether your brick can be matched from current production or requires **salvage sourcing**. And they cannot see the rusting lintel that turns a repointing job into a structural one.

Use them to sanity-check whether a bid is wildly out of range. Do not use them to decide a real proposal is overpriced.

## Chicagoland Context

This matters more here than in milder climates. The region delivers **dozens of freeze-thaw crossings each winter**, and the housing stock — **brick bungalows, two-flats, greystones, worker cottages**, post-war ranches, modern veneer — spans a century and a half of wildly different materials.

A contractor quoting your 1912 two-flat by phone is quoting a wall whose brick hardness, mortar composition, wythe construction and prior repair history they know nothing about. Those four facts determine most of the job.

## The Bottom Line

The refusal to quote by phone is not gatekeeping. It is the same reason a doctor will not prescribe from a description of symptoms: the thing that determines the answer has not been examined yet.

What you should insist on is that the visit produces something real — a **written scope naming the mortar, the preparation depth, the access method and the water source**. That document is what makes competing bids comparable, and it is the actual product of an estimate.

A number without it is just a guess with a signature line.
