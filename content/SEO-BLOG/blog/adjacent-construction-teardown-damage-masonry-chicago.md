## A Century of Standing Still, Then a Backhoe

Masonry is patient. A Chicago two-flat, a greystone, a corner storefront, a brick bungalow — these buildings sit on their footings and do essentially nothing for a hundred years. Their mortar erodes slowly, their brick weathers slowly, and if you leave them alone they behave predictably.

Then somebody buys the lot next door.

Within a few weeks the adjacent building is demolished, an excavator is working eight feet from your foundation, and a hole appears where a basement used to be. And on the third floor of your building, a crack you have never seen before opens above a window.

## Three Mechanisms, Not One

People describe this as "the vibration cracked my wall." Vibration is real, but it is usually the least of the three.

### 1. Vibration

Demolition, breaking out old foundations and slabs, pile driving, sheet piling, compaction equipment and heavy truck traffic all put energy into the ground, and the ground puts it into your building. Older masonry with eroded mortar joints has very little tensile capacity and it relieves that energy at its weakest points — mortar joints, the corners of openings, and anywhere the wall is already stressed.

Vibration alone rarely destroys a sound building, but it very efficiently opens up masonry that was already marginal.

### 2. Soil movement and loss of lateral support

**This is the big one, and it is the one nobody expects.**

Your foundation is not just resting on soil. It is confined by it. Soil beside and beneath a footing provides lateral restraint and distributes load. When a contractor excavates a deep basement immediately alongside, several things can happen:

- **Lateral support is removed** from the side of your foundation, and the soil under your footing can move sideways into the open cut.
- **Soil relaxes and moves** toward the excavation, taking your footing with it slightly.
- **Dewatering** — pumping groundwater out to keep the excavation dry — lowers the water table and causes fine-grained soils to consolidate, which settles everything around it, sometimes for a considerable distance.
- **Chicago's heavy clay soils** amplify this. Clay changes volume with moisture content, so anything that alters the moisture regime beside your building alters what your building is sitting on.
- **Heavy equipment and material stockpiles** placed close to your foundation surcharge the soil and load it in ways it has not seen before.

The result is **differential settlement** — one part of your building moving relative to another. That is what produces stepped diagonal cracking, out-of-square openings and doors that suddenly stick.

### 3. Removal of a building your wall was leaning on

On Chicago's narrow lots, buildings are frequently built to the lot line, sometimes sharing a **party wall** and often standing as two separate walls a few inches apart. When the neighbouring building comes down:

- A wall that had been sheltered on one side for a century is **suddenly a weather-facing exterior wall** — with no flashing, no weather-struck joints, sometimes unfinished common brick, and often **open joist pockets** where the neighbour's floor framing was embedded.
- If it was a true shared party wall, **structural elements were removed from one side of it**.
- Whatever lateral bracing the adjoining structure provided is gone.

That wall now takes wind-driven rain directly on masonry never detailed for it, and in Chicagoland that means saturation followed by dozens of freeze-thaw cycles a winter.

## What to Do Before Anything Starts

If you learn a teardown or major excavation is coming, do this. It takes an afternoon and it is worth more than everything else in this article.

**Photograph everything, with dates.**

- Every exterior elevation, wide and close
- **Every existing crack**, with a ruler or coin in frame for scale
- Existing settlement, bowing, spalling and prior repairs
- The foundation and basement walls from inside
- Interior plaster and drywall cracks, especially at door and window corners
- Doors and windows — note any that already stick or are out of square
- Floors — a level or a marble tells you something about existing slope

**Store the images somewhere with reliable metadata** — the automatic date stamp on the file matters. Email them to yourself if that is easiest.

**Consider a formal preconstruction survey.** On a valuable or already-distressed building, a documented survey by an engineer is money well spent. Developers doing this properly often offer one; if one is offered, accept it and get a copy.

**Ask what is planned.** Excavation depth, whether sheet piling or pile driving is involved, whether dewatering is planned, and whether they intend to underpin adjacent foundations. Ask for the contractor's insurance information in writing before work starts, not after damage appears.

## Warning Signs During and After the Work

### Outside

- **New stepped diagonal cracking** through mortar joints, especially near corners and openings
- **Vertical cracks** through brick and joint alike
- **Horizontal cracking** in a foundation wall, which suggests lateral pressure
- Masonry **bulging or out of plane**
- Openings visibly **out of square**
- Cracks above windows and doors, or a **sagging brick arch** — see our guide to [sagging brick arches](/blog/brick-arch-repair-sagging-jack-arch-chicago)
- A **gap opening** between a chimney, porch or addition and the main structure
- Fresh, clean, unweathered crack edges — the tell that a crack is new

### Inside

- New plaster or drywall cracking, typically at the corners of openings
- Doors and windows that suddenly stick or will not latch
- Floors developing a slope, or gaps opening at baseboards
- New water entry at the foundation or through a wall that was previously dry

### On the exposed party wall

- Unfinished, weather-struck-free joints now facing the sky
- **Open joist pockets** where the neighbour's framing was
- Common brick — never intended to be exposed — taking direct weather
- Water staining appearing on the corresponding interior wall

## Documenting Damage as It Happens

When cracking appears during adjacent work, the record you build determines what happens next.

1. **Photograph immediately**, with scale and date, from the same positions as your preconstruction shots.
2. **Mark the crack ends** with a pencil line and date them. When the crack extends past the mark, you have proof of progression.
3. **Install crack monitors.** A simple crack gauge or telltale mounted across the crack, photographed weekly and dated, distinguishes active movement from a crack that has stabilized. This distinction drives the entire repair strategy.
4. **Notify in writing.** The property owner, the general contractor and, where appropriate, the city. Verbal notice does not exist later.
5. **Keep a dated log** of what was happening on site when new damage appeared — demolition day, pile driving, dewatering start.
6. **Get professional assessments.** A structural engineer for stability and cause; a masonry contractor for scope and cost.
7. **Do not accept a quick cosmetic patch** from the contractor before the extent is known. A crack filled in week two hides the evidence you need in month six.

## Repair or Wait?

The governing question is whether **movement has stopped.**

| Situation | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Anything unstable or a falling hazard | Address immediately, regardless of schedule |
| Active water entry | Temporary weatherproofing now, permanent repair later |
| Cracking that is still progressing | Monitor; do not do permanent repairs yet |
| Movement stopped, construction ongoing | Usually wait until the adjacent work is complete |
| Movement stopped, construction complete | Proceed with permanent repair |
| Exposed party wall | Weatherproof promptly — it deteriorates every week it is exposed |

Repairing cracks while a building is still moving means paying for the same repair twice, and it destroys the visual record of what happened.

## What a Real Repair Involves

Depending on what occurred:

1. **Structural assessment first**, where movement has been significant.
2. **Stabilize the cause.** Underpinning, soil stabilization or foundation repair where support was lost — this is engineering-led work and it precedes any masonry cosmetics.
3. **Rebuild displaced masonry.** Sections that have moved out of plane are taken down and rebuilt rather than patched.
4. **Repoint cracked joints** properly — grind back to sound mortar, repack in lifts with matched mortar, tool to the original profile. Not caulk, and not a smear over the surface.
5. **Replace cracked and spalled brick** with matched units, blended through the field.
6. **Reset or rebuild arches and lintels** over openings that have moved.
7. **Treat the newly exposed party wall** — repoint the full elevation, close and properly fill open joist pockets, address the parapet and coping above it, and apply an appropriate breathable treatment where warranted. Never a film-forming coating that traps moisture in the wall.
8. **Restore drainage and flashing** disturbed by the adjacent work.
9. **Document the completed repair** with photographs and a written scope, for your records and any claim.

## Materials Notes

Mortar matching matters more than usual here, because repairs are often scattered across an otherwise untouched elevation and any mismatch is conspicuous. **Mortar must be softer than the brick it sits against** — on pre-1920 Chicago common brick that means lime-rich or Type O mixes, not a hard Portland blend.

On a newly exposed party wall, the temptation is to seal it. Resist it. That wall has to dry to at least one side, and a film-forming coating on saturated century-old brick produces spalling within a few winters. Repointing with appropriate mortar, closing the joist pockets correctly, and getting the top of the wall detailed to shed water do far more.

## The Chicago Context

This is a very Chicago problem, for structural and market reasons both.

The city's **lot-line construction** means buildings frequently sit within inches of each other, so an adjacent excavation is genuinely adjacent. Its **heavy clay soils** move with moisture and respond strongly to dewatering. Its **housing stock** is largely pre-1940 masonry on shallow footings by modern standards, built with soft brick and lime mortar that has limited tensile capacity.

And the **teardown-and-rebuild market** across the city and the inner suburbs means a large number of century-old masonry buildings are having deep new basements dug beside them by contractors working to a schedule.

None of that makes the damage acceptable. It makes it predictable — which is exactly why the preconstruction photographs matter so much.

## The Bottom Line

If a teardown is coming next door, spend an afternoon photographing your building before the first machine arrives. Date the images. Keep them.

If damage appears, document it in the same frames, mark and monitor the cracks, notify in writing, and get both an engineer and a mason to look at it. Then wait for movement to stop before doing permanent repairs — unless something is unsafe or letting water in, which gets handled now.

And if a party wall has just been exposed to the sky for the first time in a century, treat that as urgent. It is taking weather it was never built to take, and Chicago winters are unforgiving of masonry that cannot dry.
