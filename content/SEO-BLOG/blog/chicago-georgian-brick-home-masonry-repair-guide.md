# Chicago Georgian Brick Homes: A Masonry Repair Guide

**Chicago Georgian brick home masonry repair** comes down to one principle: respect how the house was built. Most Georgians on Chicago's south side and in the near suburbs went up between the 1920s and the 1950s with solid brick walls and soft, lime-rich mortar. They need repointing with a compatible mortar that is softer than the brick, careful repair of limestone sills and lintels, and regular chimney maintenance. If you live in **Beverly, Mount Greenwood, Evergreen Park or Oak Lawn**, you know the house: square, symmetrical, two storeys of brick under a hip roof, a centred front door with a limestone or brick surround, limestone sills under every window, a chimney on one end wall, and glass block in the basement. It is one of the best-built house types in the region. It is also the one most often damaged by well-meaning repairs.

> A Chicago Georgian rarely fails from age alone. It fails from fifty years of repairs made with materials harder than the house.

## How a Chicago Georgian brick home is built

Understanding the wall is the first step in repairing it properly.

### Solid masonry, not veneer (usually)

Most pre-war Georgians are **solid masonry**. The outer layer is **face brick** — hard, dense, fired for appearance — and behind it sits a second wythe of **Chicago common brick** or **structural clay tile**. Every few courses a row of bricks laid end-on (a header course), or metal ties, binds the two layers together. The wall itself carries the floors and roof.

That matters because a solid wall handles water differently from modern brick veneer. There is no air cavity, no flashing system and often no weep holes. The wall absorbs rain, holds it, and dries it back out through the brick and — critically — through the mortar joints. Some later 1950s Georgians are brick veneer over block or frame; they look nearly identical from the sidewalk, so the wall type should be confirmed before anything is specified.

### Lime-rich mortar was the design, not a weakness

The original mortar in most of these homes is **lime-rich**: softer and more porous than the brick. That is deliberate. Mortar is supposed to be the sacrificial, replaceable part of the wall. It flexes slightly as the house moves, lets moisture escape, and wears away over decades so the brick does not have to. For a deeper explanation, see our guide to [lime vs Portland mortar](/blog/lime-vs-portland-mortar).

### Face brick in front, common brick out of sight

Face brick on the street and side elevations is durable. The softer **common brick** that shows up on rear walls, chimney interiors, garages and the back wythe is far more vulnerable once exposed — we cover it in detail in [why Chicago common brick crumbles](/blog/chicago-common-brick-why-it-crumbles).

## Common problems on Georgian brick homes

| Component | Common failure | Proper fix |
|---|---|---|
| Mortar joints | Receding, sandy or cracked joints; earlier hard-mortar patches | Grind or rake joints to depth, repoint with compatible lime-rich or Type N mortar matched for colour and texture |
| Face brick | Spalling at the edges next to hard-mortar repairs | Replace spalled units with matched brick; correct the mortar so it does not recur |
| Limestone sills | Cracks, spalling, open end joints, lost slope | Patch with restoration mortar, pin or reset; replace only when broken through |
| Lintels over windows | Rusted steel lintels jacking brick; cracked limestone lintels | Replace or treat steel and add flashing; repair or replace stone lintel |
| Brick arches | Sagging or dropped keystone; cracked joints | Reset and repoint arch; rebuild when units have shifted — see [brick arch repair](/blog/brick-arch-repair-sagging-jack-arch-chicago) |
| End-wall chimney | Cracked crown, eroded joints at the top, failed flashing, leaning | Rebuild crown, repoint or rebuild top courses, new flashing and cap |
| Glass block basement windows | Cracked block, failed mortar, leaks | Reset or replace panels; repair surrounding brick and sill |
| Front stoop | Settled or cracked brick steps, loose limestone treads | Reset treads, repoint or rebuild the stoop on a sound base |

### Warning signs to look for

- **Receding mortar joints** you can scratch out with a key or screwdriver.
- **Spalling or flaking brick faces**, especially beside grey, glossy, obviously newer mortar.
- **Stair-step cracks** running diagonally from window corners.
- **Rust stains or bulging brick** above windows — a sign of rusting steel lintels.
- **Cracked or tilted limestone sills**, or open joints where the sill meets the brick.
- **White efflorescence** below sills, at the chimney or low on the foundation.
- **Loose bricks or eroded joints at the top of the chimney**, or a crumbling crown.
- **Water stains on interior plaster** along exterior walls or around the chimney breast.

## Why hard Portland repairs damage these homes

This is the single most important point in this guide. From the 1960s onward, many Georgian homes were patched with straight Portland cement or a hard Type S mix. Hard mortar is stronger and less permeable than the original brick. When the wall gets wet and freezes, the stress and moisture no longer escape through the joint — they move into the brick. The edges of the bricks crack and pop off while the hard mortar stays intact. The result is a wall of rounded, shrinking bricks framed by proud grey joints.

> If the mortar is harder than the brick, the brick becomes the sacrificial part of the wall. Every proper repair on a Georgian brick home puts that relationship back the right way round.

## Tuckpointing vs repointing on an older home

In everyday use the words overlap. Strictly, **repointing** means removing deteriorated mortar to a consistent depth and replacing it; **tuckpointing** traditionally adds a fine contrasting line for appearance. On a Georgian home what matters is the method:

1. **Assess** the wall, mortar hardness, brick condition and earlier repairs.
2. **Match the mortar** — test colour, sand and texture against cleaned original joints, and choose a strength softer than the brick.
3. **Remove mortar** to roughly two and a half times the joint width with grinders and hand tools, without widening the joints or chipping brick arrises.
4. **Clean and dampen** the joints.
5. **Repoint in lifts**, compacting each layer.
6. **Tool the joints** to match the original profile.
7. **Cure** the work slowly and protect it from sun, wind and frost.
8. **Replace damaged units** with matched or salvaged brick.

Not every Georgian needs the whole house repointed at once — compare [spot repointing vs full tuckpointing](/blog/spot-repointing-vs-full-tuckpointing) before you commit.

## Materials and techniques that suit Georgian homes

- **Lime-rich or Type N mortar**, matched for colour, sand and joint profile. Type S is reserved for below-grade or very exposed applications where the brick can take it.
- **Matched or salvaged face brick** for replacements; new brick is chosen for size, colour and texture.
- **Restoration mortars** for limestone sills, lintels and trim, compatible with the stone.
- **Galvanized or properly primed steel lintels** with flashing where old lintels have failed.
- **Breathable, vapour-permeable sealers** only where a specific exposure calls for them. Film-forming sealers on a solid brick wall trap moisture and accelerate spalling.

## DIY vs hiring a professional mason

Most homeowners can clean out a gutter or reseal a hose bib. Repointing a solid brick wall is different. The two most common DIY outcomes — hard bagged mortar from the hardware store, and joints widened by an angle grinder — both cause permanent damage to face brick that cannot be replaced like-for-like. Limestone work, lintel replacement and chimney rebuilds also involve heights, structural loads and flashing details best left to experienced masons.

## The Chicago and near-suburban context

Chicago's **freeze-thaw cycles** are the main driver of masonry deterioration: water enters a joint, freezes, expands, and opens the joint a little further each winter. Georgian homes in **Beverly** and **Mount Greenwood** often sit on narrow lots with gangways only a few feet wide, which slows drying on side walls and complicates scaffolding. Many in **Evergreen Park** and **Oak Lawn** have end-wall chimneys fully exposed to west and north winds. ## Buying or inheriting a Georgian: what to check first

Many Georgians change hands only once a generation, so a new owner often inherits several decades of deferred and mismatched repairs at once. Before you plan a kitchen or a roof, spend an hour on the masonry:

- **Look at the mortar colour across each elevation.** Patches of grey, glossy, smooth mortar among softer buff or tan joints mark earlier hard repairs — and the bricks beside them are usually the ones already spalling.
- **Check the chimney from the street with binoculars.** Missing joints in the top few courses, a cracked crown or a slight lean are the most common urgent items on these houses.
- **Look up under every window head.** A horizontal crack, a bulge or rust staining above a window means a steel lintel is expanding as it rusts.
- **Run a hand along the limestone sills.** Cracks, flaking and open end joints let water straight into the wall below.
- **Go to the basement.** Damp patches, white powder and loose mortar on the inside face of the foundation, or leaking glass block, point to water management problems outside.
- **Check the front stoop.** Settled steps and loose limestone treads are a safety issue as well as a masonry one.

Home inspectors note many of these items in passing, but they rarely distinguish a cosmetic patch from an active failure. A mason's assessment ranks them, so the first year's budget goes to water entry and anything that could fall, and the cosmetic items wait.

## Maintenance that keeps a Georgian sound

- **Walk the house every spring and fall.** Look at joints, sills, lintels and the chimney top with binoculars.
- **Keep gutters and downspouts working.** Overflowing gutters soak the top of the wall and the sill line below.
- **Seal sill ends and window perimeters** with compatible sealant where they meet the brick.
- **Cap the chimney** and keep the crown sound.
- **Avoid power washing and sandblasting**, which strip the brick's fired face.
- **Fix small areas promptly** rather than waiting for a whole-house repointing.
