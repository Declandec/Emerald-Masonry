A general contractor does not really buy masonry. They buy a **duration, a set of dependencies, and a risk position** — and then they buy the masonry that comes with it.

Which is why the lowest masonry number is so often the most expensive one on a job. A proposal that prices the work but says nothing about lead times, crew size, what it needs from the trades before it, or what happens when the wall opens up cannot be scheduled. It can only be hoped over.

## 1. A Scope With Stated Assumptions and Exclusions

The proposal should be legible as a schedule input, not just a price. That means:

- **Quantities**, by area or elevation, with the assumption behind each
- **Cut-out depth and mortar specification** where repair work is involved
- **Unit sources** — matched, reclaimed, or current production
- **Exclusions, stated plainly** rather than implied by silence

The exclusions list is where bid spread actually lives. The usual suspects:

| Commonly excluded | Why it matters to a GC |
|---|---|
| Access, staging, lifts | Can rival the masonry cost on upper elevations |
| Overhead protection | Required wherever work sits above a path of travel |
| Shoring at openings | Non-optional for lintel and arch work |
| Temporary heat and cold-weather protection | Governs whether winter work is possible at all |
| Flashing supplied by others | Classic gap between masonry and roofing scopes |
| Hoisting, dumpsters, debris removal | Small line items that surface late |
| Final cleaning | Often assumed by both sides to be the other's |

## 2. Durations With Crew Size, Not Just Dates

"Three weeks" is not schedulable. **"Three weeks with a crew of four, assuming one elevation at a time and no more than two rain days"** is. A GC needs manpower loading to sequence other trades, plan hoisting and lay-down, and know what a delay actually costs.

It also exposes optimism. A sub who commits to a duration without naming a crew size has not built the schedule backwards from the work.

## 3. Material Lead Times — The Real Schedule Risk

Labour rarely blows a masonry milestone. **Material availability does.**

Standard block and common brick are generally quick. **Matched, reclaimed or discontinued brick, custom coping, specific stone and long-lead steel** can run weeks or considerably longer, and on restoration work the match has to be approved before it can be ordered — which adds a submittal cycle in front of the lead time.

The question a GC should ask at bid, not at buyout: *what is the longest-lead item in this scope, and when do you need approval to hit the date?*

## 4. What Masonry Needs From the Trades Before It

A masonry sub who cannot answer this has not thought about the sequence. Typically:

- **An accurate, ready substrate** — correct plane, correct dimensions
- **Openings set correctly.** Masonry cannot cheaply absorb a dimensional error once coursing starts
- **Embedded items in place** — anchors, ties, sleeves, lintels or flashing where supplied by others
- **Structural and shoring conditions resolved** where a wall has moved or bears on something being altered
- **Access and a stable working surface**, with staging able to be positioned
- **Temporary protection and heat** where the season requires it

Sequencing against other trades is a recurring source of rework — the general problem is covered in [masonry sequencing with other trades](/blog/masonry-sequencing-with-other-trades).

## 5. Submittals and a Mockup

On anything visible, an approved **sample panel** settles mortar colour, sand, texture, joint profile and unit blend before an elevation is built. It costs a day and prevents the worst conversation on a project — the one about a finished wall that the owner does not accept.

Submittals should cover mortar type and mix, units and their source, flashing and accessories, and sealant where it is in scope.

## 6. The Documentation Package

Routine, not requested repeatedly:

- **COI** naming the GC and owner as required, at the right limits
- **W-9**
- **Lien waivers** with each payment application, including lower tiers where applicable
- **Schedule** with durations and manpower
- **Safety documentation** appropriate to the site
- **Change-order procedure**, agreed before it is needed

## 7. Unit Prices and a Change-Order Mechanism

Masonry has genuine hidden conditions — deterioration depth is not visible until the wall is opened, and corroded steel is buried by design. A sub who acknowledges that and prices **unit rates** for the uncertain quantities is easier to manage than one who promises no surprises.

Agreed unit prices — per unit replaced, per linear foot of additional repointing, per lintel — convert a mid-job discovery into arithmetic instead of a negotiation while the wall is open. The mechanics are covered in [hidden conditions and change orders in masonry repair](/blog/hidden-conditions-change-orders-masonry-repair).

## New Construction vs. Restoration: Different Subcontracts

GCs sometimes treat these as one trade with one bid format. They are not.

| | New construction masonry | Restoration / remediation masonry |
|---|---|---|
| **Quantity certainty** | High — it is on the drawings | Low — deterioration extent is inside the wall |
| **Primary schedule risk** | Material lead time and crew loading | Hidden conditions and match approval |
| **Contract mechanism needed** | Fixed scope, clear submittals | Fixed scope **plus unit prices** for discovery |
| **Substrate dependency** | Structure and layout accuracy | Existing conditions, which nobody fully knows |
| **Mockup purpose** | Establish workmanship standard | Establish *match* to existing work |
| **Typical dispute** | Dimensional coordination | Whether extra work was foreseeable |

The practical consequence: a restoration scope bid like new construction will generate change orders, and a GC who has not built a mechanism for them will absorb the friction. The difference in mindset is covered in [restoration mason vs. new construction mason](/blog/restoration-mason-vs-new-construction-mason).

## Prequalifying a Masonry Sub: Questions Worth Asking

Ten minutes on the phone separates subs who have run this work from subs who have only priced it.

- **"What is the longest-lead item in this scope?"** — a sub who has not identified one has not looked at the units.
- **"What crew size are you carrying, and for how long?"** — the answer is either specific or it is guesswork.
- **"What do you need from us before you mobilise?"** — good subs have a list ready.
- **"What is excluded?"** — the shortest route to comparing bids honestly.
- **"How do you handle a condition worse than the scope assumed?"** — you want to hear unit prices and a documented procedure, not "we'll work it out."
- **"Who is on site daily, and who do we call?"** — a named foreman beats an office number.
- **"Have you done this building type before?"** — occupied multi-tenant, institutional, historic and industrial masonry are genuinely different disciplines.
- **"What happens if we hit November?"** — the answer reveals whether cold-weather protection was priced or ignored.

## Where Masonry Subs Most Often Cause GC Pain

Named plainly, because they are avoidable:

**Underestimated cut-out depth on repair scopes.** A sub who priced a shallow pass either does the wrong work or returns for a change order. Requiring depth in the scope prevents both.

**Match approval left until mobilisation.** Unit approval sits in front of the lead time, so a match submitted late pushes the milestone twice over.

**Access assumed rather than surveyed.** A lift that cannot reach or cannot be positioned becomes staging, and staging becomes days.

**No shoring plan for openings.** Lintel and arch work without shoring is both a safety issue and a schedule risk if anything moves.

**Silence on flashing responsibility.** The masonry-to-roofing boundary is the most common scope gap on envelope work, and it surfaces as a leak after everyone has demobilised.

**Closeout drift.** Punch, cleaning and final waivers trickling in for weeks holds up the GC's own closeout and retention.

## 8. Weather Realism

In Chicagoland this is a scheduling constraint, not a caveat. Mortar needs sustained above-freezing conditions to cure; cold-weather work requires enclosures, protection and heat, all of which cost time and money. A sub who quietly accepts a November milestone without pricing protection is transferring risk to the GC.

## 9. Closeout That Does Not Drag

- **Punch walked promptly** with the GC, not weeks later
- **Cleaning completed** to the standard in the scope
- **Warranty terms** stated in writing
- **As-built notes** on what was actually found and done — particularly the mortar specification and unit source, which the owner will need for the next phase
- **Final lien waivers** delivered without chasing

## What Drives the Cost of a Masonry Subcontract

Scope quantities and the true extent of deterioration on repair work · access method and who provides it · overhead protection and shoring · match difficulty and long-lead materials · crew size and duration against the master schedule · phasing and occupancy constraints · cold-weather protection where the season demands it · documentation and submittal requirements. A bid that omits several of these is not cheaper — it is incomplete.

## Chicagoland Context

Two regional realities shape masonry subcontracts here. **The stock is old and mixed** — hard face brick on street elevations and softer common brick on sides and rear across much of the city's pre-war inventory, which means one mortar specification across a building is wrong somewhere, and a sub who does not raise that has not looked closely. And **the working season is finite**: the practical window runs roughly spring through autumn, so masonry milestones pushed late either carry protection costs or slip.

For remediation and restoration work specifically, the hidden-condition risk is higher than on new construction, which makes stated assumptions and unit prices more valuable, not less.
