For a long time, masonry entered an insurance conversation in exactly one circumstance: after something fell, when somebody filed a claim.

That has been shifting. On older commercial and multi-family buildings across Chicagoland, façade condition now shows up **at renewal** — in a loss-control inspector's photographs, in an underwriting questionnaire about deferred maintenance, or in a recommendation letter with a date on it.

The practical consequence for owners, boards and managers is worth stating plainly: **the most useful masonry document you can hold is not a repair invoice. It is a dated condition assessment with a written plan that you actually followed.**

## Why a property carrier cares about brick

Two separate exposures, and they are often confused.

**Property.** Water getting into a building through a failed façade produces interior damage, mould remediation, tenant disruption, and business interruption. From an underwriting standpoint, a wall that is actively admitting water is a predictable future claim.

**Liability.** Masonry over a sidewalk, an entry, or a parking stall that is spalling, loose, or displaced is a bodily-injury exposure. This is the one that moves underwriters most, because the severity is open-ended in a way water damage is not.

Which is why the conditions that draw attention are disproportionately **overhead**: parapets, coping, cornices, lintels over entrances, balcony edges, and decorative units held by corroding anchors.

## Where condition surfaces in the process

### Loss-control inspections

Many commercial and larger multi-family policies include a physical inspection. The inspector is not a mason, and the report is not an engineering assessment — but they photograph elevations, and visible spalling, open joints, displaced units and staining go into a file.

### Underwriting questionnaires

Renewal submissions increasingly ask about building age, roof and façade condition, deferred maintenance, capital improvement plans, and whether recent inspections identified conditions. How those questions are answered matters, and an honest "assessed in 2026, three-phase plan underway, phase one complete" is a far stronger answer than a blank.

### Recommendation letters

The formal version: a written finding with a condition, sometimes a deadline, occasionally a consequence. Masonry recommendations usually concern loose material over pedestrian areas, parapet deterioration, or active water infiltration.

The correct response is not to argue and not to ignore. It is to **get it assessed and respond in writing with a scope and a schedule** — even when the work itself must wait for curing temperatures. Carriers respond well to documented plans with dates and badly to silence.

### After a loss

Where the argument is always the same one.

## Sudden loss versus accumulated deterioration

Policies cover sudden and accidental loss. They do not cover wear.

So when masonry fails and a claim follows, the whole thing turns on **causation** — did a storm, impact or event cause this, or did years of freeze-thaw on a saturated wall finally let go on a windy night?

In Chicagoland, honestly, it is usually the second with the first as a trigger. A wind event dislodges a unit that lost its bond three winters ago.

That reality makes one preparation disproportionately valuable: **dated baseline photographs of every elevation, refreshed annually, from repeatable positions.** They are what let you demonstrate that a condition was not present last spring, and they cost nothing but an hour. Without them, a causation argument is assertion against assertion. ## What Chicagoland does to façades

Worth understanding because it explains why this region draws underwriting attention at all.

**Freeze-thaw cycling.** A Chicago winter does not freeze once and hold — it thaws and refreezes dozens of times a season. Every cycle is an expansion event inside wet masonry, and it is the dominant deterioration driver here.

**Lake-effect moisture.** More wetting, slower drying, particularly on shaded elevations.

**De-icing salt** at grade level, attacking the bottom courses of every wall, step and dock.

**Age of the stock.** Chicago brick bungalows, greystones, two-flats, courtyard buildings, and mid-century commercial construction — much of it now at or past the point where original mortar and original unprotected steel lintels are genuinely due.

**Parapets everywhere.** Flat-roofed commercial and multi-family construction means an enormous quantity of the region's masonry is roof-edge masonry, which is the most exposed and least inspected material on any building. See [parapet wall failure signs](/blog/parapet-wall-failure-signs-commercial-buildings).

## The conditions that draw the most attention

From what actually gets photographed and written up, the list is short and consistent.

**Loose, displaced or spalling material above a pedestrian area.** Top of the list every time, because the severity is unbounded. A single displaced brick over an entry canopy outweighs a whole elevation of tired joints on a rear wall.

**Parapet deterioration visible from the street or the roof.** Open coping joints, a parapet visibly out of plane, vegetation growing from the top of a wall — that last one is a particularly bad sign, because it means water has been standing in the masonry long enough to support a root system.

**Cornices, terra cotta and cast-stone ornament** showing cracks, displacement or previous patch repairs. Anchors corrode invisibly, so displacement matters more than surface condition here.

**Active water infiltration** — interior staining, efflorescence blooming on an exterior wall, saturated masonry that never dries on one elevation.

**Lintel rust-jacking above entrances and storefronts**, which reads as a straight horizontal crack with rust staining below it. See [lintel rust stains and orange streaks](/blog/lintel-rust-stains-orange-streaks-brick).

**Balcony and walkway edges** on multi-family, where reinforcement corrosion pushes the concrete surface off from within.

**Prior repairs that look wrong** — hard grey mortar smeared over soft historic joints, painted-over spalling, sealant used as a structural fix. These signal a maintenance history rather than a single condition, and they are read that way.

## What it costs to do nothing

Worth setting out, because the deferral decision is usually framed as saving money.

Masonry deterioration is not linear. The mechanism that caused it — an open joint, a cracked coping, a corroding lintel — keeps operating every rain and every freeze-thaw cycle. So the progression runs from a repointing scope, to repointing plus unit replacement, to a saturated wall with interior damage, to rebuilding. Each step is a multiple of the last.

Layer the insurance consequence on top: terms that tighten, deductibles that move, a recommendation that escalates, and — after a loss — a causation argument you are poorly positioned to make because there is no baseline documentation.

The building was always going to need the work. Deferral mostly determines how much of it, and under what conditions, and whether you were choosing the timing or something else was.

## What a carrier-ready file contains

None of this is exotic. Most of it is an afternoon.

**A dated condition assessment**, elevation by elevation, with photographs. Emerald provides this free, in writing.

**A written repair plan with a priority order** — overhead and structural first, active water entry second, deterioration third, appearance last — and dates against each phase.

**Evidence of work completed** against that plan: scopes, invoices, before-and-after photographs.

**Baseline elevation photographs**, dated and refreshed annually.

**Documentation of conscious deferral.** This one is underrated. Deferring a cosmetic item with a stated reason and a revisit date is defensible planning. Deferring an overhead item silently is not. A plan that shows judgement reads better than one that shows everything was done at once, because nobody believes the latter anyway.

**Contractor documentation** — COI, W-9, lien waivers on file, which Emerald provides on request.

## Phasing, and what it signals

A common worry: does phasing look like deferral to an underwriter?

Done properly, no — it looks like management. What distinguishes them is **sequence and evidence**.

Phasing by priority, with the overhead and water-entry items in phase one and completed on schedule, signals a building that is being run. Phasing that defers the parapet because the street elevation is more visible signals the opposite, and an inspector's photographs will show which one you did.

Two practical rules carry over from any capital plan: **group by access**, since the expensive part of upper-elevation work is getting up there, and **sequence by priority**, never by convenience. ## For condo and townhome associations specifically

Associations carry a particular version of this problem, because the insurance conversation and the reserve conversation happen in different meetings with different documents.

The reserve study carries a masonry line estimated from building age and square footage. The insurance renewal asks about condition. Neither is informed by an actual survey of the walls unless somebody commissions one.

Connecting them is straightforward and high-value: one condition assessment feeds both. It gives the board a defensible scope and sequence for the reserve plan, and it gives the manager something substantive for the renewal submission. ## Timing the work against the renewal date

A small piece of sequencing that repeatedly pays for itself.

Find out when your renewal submission is actually prepared — usually weeks before the policy date, not on it — and work backwards. An assessment completed and a first phase underway before that submission changes what can honestly be reported. The same work finished a month after it counts for nothing until the following year.

In Chicagoland that collides with the construction season. A policy renewing in late winter means the useful window for completing visible work closes in autumn, because mortar needs workable temperatures to cure. Buildings that handle this well commission the assessment in summer, complete phase one in early autumn, and go into the submission with photographs of finished work and dates against the remaining phases.

If the timing has already slipped, a written scope with a scheduled start is still a materially better answer than nothing — it is evidence of a decision rather than evidence of drift.
