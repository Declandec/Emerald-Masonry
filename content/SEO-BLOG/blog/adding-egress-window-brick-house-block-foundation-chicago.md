# Adding an Egress Window to a Brick House or Block Foundation

**Adding an egress window to a brick house** means cutting into masonry: a code-sized opening in the foundation and, often, in the brick above it, supported by a properly sized **steel lintel**, rebuilt cleanly at the edges, and finished with a window and a drained **window well**. Done right, it is safe, dry and looks like it was always there. Finishing a basement is one of the most common projects on Chicago-area homes, and turning it into a bedroom almost always means adding an **egress window**: an opening large enough for someone to get out in an emergency and for firefighters to get in. On a frame house, that is mostly carpentry. On a brick bungalow, Georgian, raised ranch or two-flat, it is masonry and structural work. This guide explains what is involved.

> An egress window is a hole in the part of the house that holds everything else up. Treat it like structural work, because it is.

## What an egress window is, and why it is required

Building codes require every sleeping room, including a basement bedroom, to have an **emergency escape and rescue opening**. Most Illinois communities adopt the **International Residential Code (IRC)** with local changes. Under the IRC, the opening generally has to meet these minimums:

| Requirement | Typical IRC minimum |
|---|---|
| Net clear opening | 5.7 sq ft (5.0 sq ft for some grade-floor or below-grade openings) |
| Clear opening height | 24 inches |
| Clear opening width | 20 inches |
| Sill height above floor | No more than 44 inches |
| Window well area | At least 9 sq ft, with at least 36 inches of projection and width |
| Ladder or steps | Required when the well is deeper than 44 inches |

These are general figures, and local codes and amendments vary. Always confirm the current requirements with your building department before designing the opening.

Most existing basement windows on older Chicago-area homes are much smaller than this, so meeting the code usually means **enlarging an existing opening** or **cutting a new one**.

## Know your foundation and wall

How the opening is cut depends on what it is cut through.

### Concrete block foundations

Common on many mid-century homes. Cutting block is straightforward, but the cut edges leave hollow cores that have to be filled and reinforced, and block walls carry the load in a way that makes the lintel essential.

### Poured concrete foundations

Common on newer homes. Poured walls are typically cut with a large wet saw. They are strong, but often have reinforcement in them, and a clean, square cut is key to a good window fit.

### Brick above grade

On many homes, especially raised ranches and bungalows, the basement window sits partly above grade, so the opening extends up into the brick. That brick has to be cut cleanly, a **lintel** set to carry it, and the brick rebuilt around the new window. On **solid masonry** walls that means every layer; on **brick veneer** it means the veneer plus the backup wall, with flashing and weep holes.

### Older stone or brick foundations

On some older homes, the foundation itself is stone or brick laid in soft mortar. Openings in these walls need extra care and often an engineer's input.

## Enlarge an existing window or cut a new opening?

Most homeowners start by asking whether the old basement window can simply be made bigger. Often it can, and it is usually the first option we look at.

### Enlarging an existing opening

An existing window already has a lintel, a finished head and, on many homes, a well. Making it taller (by lowering the sill) is the simplest version, because the width and the lintel can sometimes stay. Making it wider almost always means a new, longer lintel, because the old one was sized for the smaller span. On older homes, the existing lintel is often rusted anyway and due for replacement.

### Cutting a new opening

A new location makes sense when the existing windows are in the wrong room, sit under a deck or driveway, or are too close to a corner or a beam pocket to enlarge safely. It is more work: a full new lintel, new edges, and a new well, but it puts the window where the bedroom needs it.

| Factor | Enlarge existing | Cut new |
|---|---|---|
| Lintel | Often replaced with a longer one | Always new |
| Room layout | Fixed to the old location | Choose the best location |
| Outside space | Existing well may be reused or enlarged | New well and excavation |
| Brick above grade | Rebuild at the widened edges | Cut and rebuild a full new opening |

## Before any cutting: the planning checklist

- **Permit and plans**: most municipalities require a permit, and some require an engineer's design for the opening and lintel.
- **Location**: avoid gas, water, electrical and sewer lines, plus load points such as beams resting on the wall.
- **Grade and well**: confirm there is room outside for a code-compliant well, especially on tight city lots and gangways.
- **Drainage**: plan how the well will drain, usually to the drain tile or a gravel base, before the hole is dug.
- **Existing condition**: walls that are already cracked, bowed or leaking should be assessed and addressed first.
- **Matching**: plan the brick and mortar match for any above-grade rebuilding.

## How an egress opening is cut and built

Every project differs, but a careful job generally follows this order.

1. **Lay out the opening** to the required size, plus allowances for the window frame.
2. **Excavate outside** down to the depth of the new well and below the sill.
3. **Support the wall** above the opening where needed, with temporary shoring.
4. **Set the lintel**: on brick and block walls, the lintel is often installed before or as the opening is cut, so the wall is carried the whole time.
5. **Cut the opening**: block or brick is removed and edges cut square; poured concrete is sawn with a wet saw.
6. **Finish the edges**: block cores are filled solid and reinforced as specified; brick is rebuilt and toothed in.
7. **Install the window** in a properly sized buck or frame, sealed to the masonry.
8. **Flash and seal**: install flashing at the head on above-grade brick, keep weep holes open, and seal the window perimeter.
9. **Build the well**: set the well liner, a drain at the bottom tied into drainage, and a ladder or steps if required.
10. **Backfill and grade** so surface water runs away from the well.
11. **Rebuild above-grade brick** in matched brick and mortar and clean up.

## Materials and techniques

| Item | What is typically used | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Lintel | Steel angle or engineered lintel sized to the span and load | Carries the wall above the opening |
| Block edges | Grout-filled cores, reinforcement as specified | Restores strength at the cut |
| Brick rebuild | Matched face brick, toothed in | Keeps the wall bonded and blended |
| Mortar | Type S below grade; Type N or matched mix above grade | Durability where wet; compatibility with existing brick |
| Flashing | Flexible flashing over the head on brick walls | Sends water out, not in |
| Window buck | Treated or composite frame sealed to masonry | Keeps the window and seal stable |
| Well | Galvanized or composite liner, drain, gravel | Escape room and drainage |

## Common mistakes

- **No lintel, or one that is too small**, leading to cracks above the opening.
- **Cutting before shoring** on a loaded wall.
- **Hollow block edges left open**, weakening the wall and letting water in.
- **Wells without drainage**, which turn into basins and leak through the window.
- **Gray mortar and mismatched brick** on the above-grade rebuild.
- **No flashing above the window** on brick walls.
- **Opening too small** to pass inspection once the window frame is in.

Egress windows are a popular DIY topic online, but cutting a foundation is not a weekend project. ## Chicagoland context

Chicago-area basements were not built to be bedrooms. **Bungalows** from the 1910s to 1930s often have small, high basement windows in brick or block; post-war **raised ranches** and split-levels have larger windows partly above grade, with brick veneer above; and newer homes have poured foundations with small sliders. Clay-heavy soils hold water around foundations after heavy rains, so the well and its drainage matter as much as the opening. Narrow gangways on city lots can make wells tight. ## Maintenance after installation

- **Keep the well drain clear** of leaves and debris, especially in fall.
- **Use a well cover** that lifts easily from inside, to keep out snow and leaves without blocking escape.
- **Check the window seal and the lintel** each spring for gaps or rust.
- **Keep downspouts away** from the well.
- **Watch the brick above** the opening for new cracks in the first year.
