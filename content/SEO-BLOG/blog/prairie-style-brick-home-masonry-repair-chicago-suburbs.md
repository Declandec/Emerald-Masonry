**Prairie-style brick home masonry repair** comes down to protecting the details that make the house look long and low. That means repointing **Roman brick** with **raked horizontal joints** and **flush, color-matched vertical joints**, repairing **continuous limestone or cast stone sills and water tables**, rebuilding **broad, low chimneys**, and restoring the **urns, planters and terrace walls** that tie the house to the ground. The Prairie style was born in and around Chicago in the early 1900s, and the region still has more of these houses than anywhere else, from the best-known examples in Oak Park and River Forest to quieter brick Prairie homes and "Prairie box" Foursquares in La Grange, Hinsdale, Riverside, Downers Grove and Chicago neighborhoods. Most are now more than a century old. This guide explains what fails on a Prairie-style brick home, why, and how to repair it without losing what makes it a Prairie house.

> On a Prairie house, the mortar joints are part of the design. Repoint them the wrong way and the house stops looking horizontal.

## What makes a Prairie-style home's masonry different

Most brick houses use masonry to keep the weather out. Prairie houses also use it to draw lines. The architects behind the style wanted homes that sat low on the flat Midwest landscape, so nearly every masonry detail stretches the house sideways.

### Roman brick

Many Prairie homes are built in **Roman brick**: a long, thin brick, flatter and longer than the standard modular brick on a post-war bungalow. More bed joints and fewer head joints per foot of wall give a streaky, horizontal look. Roman brick was often made in tans, buffs, browns and iron-spot blends rather than plain red.

### Raked bed joints, flush head joints

The signature detail on many Prairie walls is the joint work. The **horizontal (bed) joints** are raked back deeply so they throw a shadow line. The **vertical (head) joints** are struck flush and colored close to the brick so they nearly vanish. The eye reads a wall of long horizontal stripes. Not every Prairie home has this, but when yours does, it has to be reproduced.

### Continuous sills, bands and water tables

Instead of a separate stone sill under each window, Prairie homes often run one **continuous limestone or cast stone sill** across a whole row of windows, plus stone or stucco **bands** and a **water table** near the base. These long courses are made of several pieces joined end to end.

### Broad chimneys, piers, urns and planters

Chimneys tend to be **wide and low**, rising through hipped roofs with deep overhangs. Porches and terraces sit on **brick piers and low walls** with flat stone caps, and many houses have built-in **urns and planter boxes** in brick, stone or cast concrete.

| Detail | Typical Prairie version | Typical post-war version | Why it matters for repair |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brick | Long, thin Roman brick | Standard modular brick | Replacements must match size, not just color |
| Bed joints | Deeply raked | Concave tooled | Profile must be reproduced |
| Head joints | Flush, tinted to brick | Same as bed joints | Two mortar colors may be needed |
| Sills | Continuous stone course | One sill per window | Piece-by-piece repair is possible |
| Chimney | Broad, low, through a hipped roof | Narrower, taller | Wider crown, more flues, more exposure |
| Garden masonry | Urns, planters, terrace walls | Rare | Highest freeze-thaw exposure on the house |

## Why Prairie home masonry fails

The good news: the **wide overhanging eaves** on most Prairie homes keep a lot of rain off the main walls, so the brick under the eaves is often in better shape than you would expect for its age. The trouble concentrates where the eaves cannot protect the masonry.

### Chimneys above the roof

The chimney is exposed on all sides above the roof, gets no protection from the eaves, and on a broad chimney the crown is large and flat. A cracked or worn **chimney crown** lets water straight into the masonry, and the top courses start to spall and loosen. Chimneys that also vent a furnace can be soaked from inside by condensing exhaust.

### Urns, planters and terrace walls

These are the hardest-working masonry on the house. They hold wet soil, are exposed on every face and the top, and freeze through every winter. Cracked caps, open joints and blocked drainage lead to **spalling**, bulging and pieces breaking away.

### Continuous sills and water tables

A long sill has many joints, and every joint is a place for water to get in. Over time joints open, individual pieces crack or settle, and some begin to pitch back toward the wall. At the base, the water table takes splash-back, snow and **de-icing salt**.

### Porch piers and steps

Brick piers and low porch walls carry roof loads and take splash and salt at grade. Open joints and failed caps let water in, and the bottom courses often deteriorate first.

### Past repairs

On many century-old Prairie homes, the biggest damage comes from earlier repairs: **hard gray portland mortar** smeared over soft original joints, every joint tooled the same way, standard-size brick patched into Roman brick walls, or **waterproof coatings** that trap moisture. Hard mortar can make the brick edges spall, because the brick ends up as the weakest part of the wall.

## Warning signs to look for

- **Mortar recessed or crumbling**, especially at the chimney top, planters and the base of the walls.
- **Brick faces flaking off** (spalling) near the ground, at the chimney or on garden masonry.
- **Cracked or shifted pieces** in a continuous sill or water table.
- **Cracked crowns** and caps on the chimney, piers and planters.
- **White efflorescence** or dark wet patches on walls that should be dry.
- **Mismatched patches**: wrong-size brick, gray joints, or joints that no longer match the original profile.
- **Rust staining** above openings, which can point to rusting steel lintels.
- **Bulging or leaning** planter walls or porch piers.

## How a professional repairs a Prairie-style brick home

Every house is assessed on its own, but a careful repair follows the same order.

1. **Document the original details.** Photograph and measure the brick size, joint widths, bed and head joint profiles and mortar colors before anything is removed.
2. **Take a mortar sample.** Match the original for color, sand and, as closely as practical, strength. On older homes this is often a lime-rich mix softer than modern Type S.
3. **Prepare test panels.** Repoint a small area so you can approve color and profile before the full job, especially where bed and head joints differ.
4. **Remove deteriorated mortar carefully.** Joints are cut or raked out to a sufficient depth for the new mortar to bond, without chipping the edges of thin Roman brick.
5. **Repoint in two passes where needed.** Raked bed joints and flush tinted head joints are tooled separately so the horizontal effect returns.
6. **Replace damaged brick** with a matched Roman brick, salvaged or specialty, set in matching mortar.
7. **Repair sills, bands and water tables piece by piece.** Pin and patch, piece in with a dutchman, or replace individual units; reset any that pitch toward the wall.
8. **Restore the chimney.** Tuckpoint or rebuild the top, form a new crown that overhangs and sheds water, and add a cap.
9. **Rebuild or repair garden masonry.** Reset caps, repoint, rebuild unsound sections, and give urns and planters drainage so water cannot sit inside.
10. **Clean gently.** Low-pressure washing and suitable cleaners only. Never sandblasting.

## DIY vs hiring a pro

Small jobs like clearing planter drains or keeping gutters clean are good homeowner tasks. Repointing a Prairie wall is not. The joint profiles are unusual, thin brick chips easily, and a mismatched mortar color or hard mix is very difficult to undo. On a house where the masonry carries the design, a bad repair is visible from the street for decades. If the home is a landmark or in a historic district, poor work can also cause problems with the preservation commission.

## Chicagoland context: where Prairie homes are and what they face

Prairie houses cluster in the older railroad suburbs and city neighborhoods built up in the early 1900s: Oak Park and River Forest, Riverside, La Grange, Hinsdale, Downers Grove, Evanston and parts of Chicago. Many simpler "Prairie box" Foursquares sit on ordinary blocks and are not always recognized as Prairie at all.

They all face Chicago's climate: dozens of freeze-thaw cycles a winter, wet springs, and heavy salt use on walks and drives. That combination is hard on exposed masonry like chimneys, planters, piers and water tables. Local rules also matter. Several of these communities have historic districts or landmark programs, so check with your municipality before exterior work on a designated property.

## Maintenance and prevention

- **Look up every spring and fall.** Check the chimney top, crown and cap with binoculars.
- **Keep gutters and downspouts clear** so the wide eaves do their job, and send downspouts away from the foundation.
- **Drain urns and planters.** Make sure drain holes are open; empty or protect them for winter if they hold water.
- **Check sill and water table joints** for gaps and pieces that have tipped.
- **Go easy on salt** near porch piers, steps and the water table.
- **Repoint small areas early** with the correct mortar before damage spreads.
- **Avoid sealers that form a film** and never paint the brick.
