## The Short Answer

**There is no per-brick price for brick repair in Illinois, and any figure quoted without seeing the wall is describing someone else's job.** What actually sets the number is how many units have failed, whether they are concentrated in one area or scattered across an elevation, how hard the brick is to match, how high the work is, how much surrounding repointing is needed, and whether the water source that caused the damage still has to be corrected.

## Why "Cost Per Brick" Is the Wrong Unit

The internet is full of per-brick and per-square-foot masonry figures. They are convenient for an article and close to useless for a real quote, for one structural reason:

**Almost all the cost of a small masonry repair is in getting to it, not in doing it.**

Consider two jobs. In the first, forty spalled bricks are clustered in one area, low on a rear wall, reachable from the ground. In the second, forty spalled bricks are scattered across a three-story front elevation, five here, three there, in eleven separate locations.

Same brick count. The second job can cost several times the first, because it needs scaffolding, it needs that scaffolding moved, and each cluster needs its own setup, cutting, matching and cleanup.

**Distribution matters as much as quantity.** That is the first thing an estimator is actually reading when they look at your wall.

## The Cost Drivers, In Order of How Much They Move the Number

### 1. Access and height

This is the largest single variable on most jobs, and the one homeowners consistently underestimate.

| Working height | Typical access | Effect on cost |
|---|---|---|
| Ground level, reachable | Ladders, minimal setup | Baseline |
| Second story | Ladder jacks or light staging | Moderate increase |
| Third story and above | Full scaffolding or lift | Substantial increase |
| Tight gangway or alley | Custom staging, neighbor protection | Substantial increase |
| Over a public sidewalk | Overhead protection, sometimes permits | Substantial increase |

A repair that takes two hours at ground level can take a day at the third floor, and most of that day is scaffolding.

### 2. How much has failed, and where

- **Concentrated damage** — one area, one setup, one matching exercise. Most efficient.
- **Scattered damage** — many small setups. Cost per unit rises sharply.
- **Full elevation** — most efficient per unit, because everything is done once.

This is why we itemize by area rather than quoting a wall at its worst spot. A 90-foot wall might need 20 feet rebuilt, 30 feet of unit replacement and 40 feet of straightforward repointing, and pricing all 90 at the worst condition would be both wrong and expensive.

### 3. Repair versus replacement versus rebuild

These are three different price tiers, and knowing which one you are in explains most of the range you have been quoted.

| Scope | When it applies | Relative cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Repointing** | Brick faces sound, mortar joints eroded | Lowest — this is maintenance |
| **Unit replacement** | Faces spalled off, units cracked through or crumbling | Moderate |
| **Section rebuild** | Wall has bowed, displaced, or lost cohesion | Highest |

The honest rule: **once a brick has lost its fired outer skin, it cannot be restored.** That skin is what makes it weather-resistant. A spalled unit will keep absorbing water no matter what is painted or troweled over it, so it needs replacing. Brick with sound faces surrounded by failed joints needs repointing, not replacement — and any contractor recommending wholesale replacement of sound units should be asked to justify it.

### 4. Brick matching difficulty

| Brick era | Matching approach | Effect on cost |
|---|---|---|
| 1990s–present | Often still in production or close current equivalent | Minimal |
| 1950s–1980s | Close production match, or salvage | Modest |
| 1900–1940 (Chicago common, face brick) | Salvage yards, era-matched reclaimed units | Meaningful |
| Pre-1900 hand-formed | Scarce salvage; sometimes harvesting from a hidden elevation | Significant |

That last technique is worth knowing about: on a historic building, we frequently take sound original brick from a rear or concealed elevation to repair the visible face, and put the new or near-match units in the concealed location. It costs more labor and delivers a far better result on the wall people actually see. See [brick matching and replacement material](/blog/brick-matching-replacement-material) and [salvaged brick sourcing in Chicago](/blog/salvaged-brick-sourcing-matching-chicago).

### 5. Mortar matching

Matching mortar means matching **hardness first**, then color, sand and texture.

Hardness is not a preference — it is a rule. **Mortar must be softer than the brick it holds**, so that seasonal movement and freeze-thaw stress land in the replaceable joint rather than in the brick face. Century-old Chicago brick is soft; a bagged Portland-heavy mix is hard. Using the wrong one produces a repair that looks flawless for two years and then spalls the brick around it.

A custom-blended mortar — sand sourced to match, pigment adjusted, test panels laid — costs more than opening a bag. It is also the difference between a repair that disappears into the wall and one you see from the street forever. See [Type N vs. Type S mortar](/blog/type-n-vs-type-s-mortar-chicago) and [mortar color matching](/blog/mortar-color-matching-tuckpointing).

### 6. The water source

This is the cost driver that nobody quotes and everybody pays.

Brick does not spall for no reason. Something kept it wet: a downspout discharging at the foundation, mulch and grade covering the weep line, a sprinkler head clipping the wall, a failed sill, an open coping joint, a rusting lintel, a gutter that overflows every storm.

**Replacing brick without correcting that means replacing it again.** Sometimes the correction is free — rotating a sprinkler head, pulling a mulch bed back down. Sometimes it is real work. Either way it belongs in the conversation before the masonry is priced, and an estimate that never mentions it is an estimate that has not diagnosed anything.

### 7. Removing someone else's repair

On older Chicagoland buildings, a surprising share of the scope is undoing previous work: hard mortar patches on soft brick, mortar smeared over failing joints instead of cut back, sealant packed into mortar joints, coatings applied over spalling, mortar filling the weep holes.

Removing a hard patch from soft brick without damaging the brick is slow, careful, hand-tool work — often slower than the original repair took to install. It is legitimate scope, and a good estimate names it. See [why masonry repairs fail: contractor shortcuts](/blog/why-masonry-repairs-fail-contractor-shortcuts).

### 8. Everything found once the wall is open

With units removed, you can see things that were invisible from outside: corroded veneer ties, missing or damaged flashing, blocked weeps, a lintel end that has lost section, wet sheathing behind a veneer.

Good estimates handle this with a clear allowance and a written process for changes, not with a surprise. Ask how it will be handled before work starts.

## Why Two Bids on the Same Wall Look Nothing Alike

Because they are frequently not describing the same job. The most common divergences:

- **Joint preparation depth.** Cutting back to sound mortar — generally two to two and a half times joint width — versus skimming over failed joints. Invisible at handover, decisive within five winters.
- **Mortar specification.** Custom-matched hardness and color versus whatever is in the truck.
- **Brick sourcing.** Era-matched salvage versus the closest thing available today.
- **Access method.** Proper staging versus working off ladders at heights where that is neither safe nor thorough.
- **What is included.** Water-source correction, weep clearing, cleaning down, protection of landscaping and neighboring property.
- **Insurance and licensing.** A genuinely insured, licensed crew costs more per hour than one that is not, and that difference is real, not padding.

Read the scopes side by side. If one bid is dramatically lower, the useful question is not "why are you cheaper" but **"what are you doing differently."** Related: [why commercial masonry bids vary](/blog/why-commercial-masonry-bids-vary) and [how to evaluate a masonry contractor's bid](/blog/how-to-evaluate-masonry-contractor-bid).

## The Illinois Context

**Freeze-thaw is the engine.** Chicagoland crosses the freezing point dozens of times each winter. Water inside a saturated brick expands roughly nine percent when it freezes, and each cycle takes a little more of the face. This is why identical brick in a milder climate lasts noticeably longer, and why deferring a repair here is more expensive than deferring one elsewhere. See [Chicago freeze-thaw vs. national averages](/blog/chicago-freeze-thaw-vs-national-averages).

**Housing stock sets the matching difficulty.** Chicago bungalows, greystones, two-flats and worker cottages mean soft brick and lime-rich mortar; post-war ranches mean denser units and early Portland mortars; 1990s-onward suburban homes mean brick veneer over a drainage cavity. Each carries different matching, different mortar and different cost.

**Salt adds a second mechanism.** De-icer splash crystallizes inside brick and damages it independently of frost, which is why the lowest courses so often fail first.

**Season affects scheduling, not just comfort.** Mortar needs temperature to cure properly. Cold-weather work is possible with precautions but requires protection and heat, which is a real cost. See [cold-weather masonry repair in Illinois](/blog/cold-weather-masonry-repair-illinois).

## How to Get a Number You Can Trust

1. **Insist on an on-site look.** Nobody can price masonry from a photo, and anyone who offers to is guessing or building in margin for guessing.
2. **Ask for the scope in writing, itemized by area** — units to be replaced, footage to be repointed, sections to be rebuilt.
3. **Ask what mortar, and why.** A contractor who cannot explain hardness matching in plain language is telling you something.
4. **Ask about the water.** What caused this, and what is being done about it.
5. **Ask how access is being handled**, and whether staging is included.
6. **Ask what happens if something is found once the wall is open.**
7. **Compare scopes before totals.**

## The Bottom Line

Brick repair pricing is not mysterious, it is just specific. The number is set by access, distribution, matching difficulty, scope tier and whether the underlying water problem is being solved — and every one of those requires someone standing in front of your wall.

The one generalization that does hold: **the cheapest moment to address masonry is while the problem is still joints.** Repointing is maintenance. Unit replacement is repair. Section rebuilding is construction. Each tier is a large step up from the last, and Chicagoland winters move a wall from one tier to the next faster than most owners expect.
