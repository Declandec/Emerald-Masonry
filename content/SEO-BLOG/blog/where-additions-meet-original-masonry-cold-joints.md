## Why That Crack Is Exactly Where the Addition Starts

**Because it is a cold joint — the boundary between two structures built at different times, on separate foundations, settling and moving at different rates.** The wall relieves that difference at the weakest available line, which is the junction. Repointing it with rigid mortar sends it straight back within a season or two; the correct treatment is usually a **properly detailed flexible sealant joint**, after ruling out active structural movement. Nearly every homeowner who calls about this assumes somebody did the work badly. Usually nobody did. **Two buildings are behaving like two buildings.**

## What a Cold Joint Actually Is

When an addition goes on an existing house, you now have:

- **Two foundations**, poured or laid decades apart, bearing on soil that was disturbed at different times.
- **Two ages of masonry**, with different absorption, different hardness and different remaining shrinkage.
- **Two thermal masses** that expand and contract at slightly different rates.
- **Two settlement curves** — a new addition settles most in its first years while a hundred-year-old house has long since finished.

The seam between them is a **cold joint**: a line where fresh work met cured work with no monolithic bond. It is not a defect. It is a boundary, and boundaries move.

**In Chicagoland the movement is amplified**, because heavy clay soils swell when saturated and shrink when dry, seasonally, in both directions. Two foundations of different age and depth ride that differently.

## Why Repointing It Keeps Failing

Here is the core mistake, and it is made constantly — sometimes by contractors.

**Mortar is rigid.** It is engineered to be the sacrificial layer in a masonry wall, softer than the brick so it absorbs stress and wears out first, but it does not *flex*. Pack rigid mortar into a joint that opens and closes seasonally and it cracks on the first thermal cycle.

So the homeowner sees the crack return, concludes the mason did poor work, and hires somebody else to repoint it again.

**The joint is not failing. The material is wrong for the joint.**

| | Rigid mortar | Flexible sealant |
|---|---|---|
| **Accommodates movement** | No | Yes |
| **Right for** | Bed and head joints between units | Joints designed to move |
| **At a cold joint** | Cracks within a season or two | Works, if detailed correctly |
| **Needs backer rod** | No | **Yes** |

## Reading Your Crack

Not every crack at a junction is the benign kind. The pattern tells you.

**Probably a normal cold joint:**
- Runs **straight and vertical**, floor to roof, right at the junction line.
- Roughly **consistent width** top to bottom.
- **Opens and closes seasonally** — wider in winter, narrower in summer.
- No displacement; the two faces stay in the same plane.

**Warrants structural evaluation:**
- **Widening measurably over time** in one direction.
- **Wider at one end** than the other — rotational movement.
- **Displacement** — one side sitting proud of the other.
- **Stair-step cracking** spreading away from the junction into the field of the wall.
- **Bowing** visible when sighting along either wall.
- **Doors and windows sticking** on the addition side.
- **Accompanying foundation cracks**, especially horizontal ones.

### How to tell if it is still moving

**Mark both ends with a dated pencil line and photograph it.** Check again after a full year that includes a winter and a wet spring. Seasonal opening and closing is normal. **Steady one-direction widening is not.** A crack monitor gauge does the same thing with numbers.

## What Went Wrong at Construction — and Why It Barely Matters Now

Three common origins, all producing the same crack:

**Toothed in with no movement provision.** The builder cut into the original brickwork and interlocked the new courses. It looks integrated, and it produces a ragged crack **through the brick** rather than a clean line — harder to repair well.

**Butted with no joint.** New wall built tight against old with mortar in between. Clean crack line, easiest to convert to a proper joint.

**Tied but not jointed.** Metal ties connecting the two, which resists separation but does not make two foundations settle as one. The crack appears anyway.

**None of these is fixable retroactively in the way homeowners hope.** You are not going to make two structures into one. The realistic goal is a **deliberate, well-detailed joint** that moves without letting water in.

## The Repair That Actually Works

1. **Assess first.** Confirm it is a movement joint rather than active structural settlement. If there is displacement or progressive widening, that gets evaluated before any repair.
2. **Address the water sources** feeding the area — gutters, grade, a downspout discharging at the junction.
3. **Cut out the failed mortar** the full depth of the crack, cleaning both faces to sound material.
4. **Repair damaged brick** at the junction where units have cracked or spalled.
5. **Install backer rod.** This is the step that decides the outcome. Backer rod sets the depth and creates the **hourglass profile** that lets the sealant bead stretch. Without it, sealant bonds three-sided and tears on the first cold snap.
6. **Prime** where the substrate requires it.
7. **Install a high-performance flexible sealant** — polyurethane or hybrid, chosen for movement capability and paintability, tooled to wet both faces fully.
8. **Match the appearance** as far as practical — a color-matched sealant in a clean vertical line reads as a deliberate detail rather than a repair.

**A well-executed joint at a junction looks intentional.** That is the realistic best outcome, and it is genuinely better than a crack that reopens.

## What About Matching the Brick?

Worth setting expectations. Addition brick was manufactured decades apart from the original and has weathered for a different length of time — different absorption, different hardness, different patina. **An exact match at the junction is rarely achievable**, and chasing it is usually the wrong use of the budget.

Where brick at the junction genuinely needs replacing, salvaged units matched for size, texture and absorption give the best result. But most homeowners are better served by a **clean, deliberate joint line** than by a failed attempt to make the seam disappear.

## Water Is the Real Urgency

Set aside appearance. **A vertical crack running the height of a wall is a direct path into the assembly.**

Water entering there reaches sheathing and framing on the addition side, saturates masonry that then spalls through freeze-thaw, and finds any embedded steel — **lintels, ties, shelf angles** — that happens to be in the vicinity. On the interior it shows up as staining, damp drywall or musty air at the junction wall.

That is the argument for sealing it properly this year rather than watching it another season.

## The Matching Crack on the Inside

Homeowners often mention this second, almost as an afterthought: there is also a crack inside, at the same line.

**That is the same joint, seen from the other side**, and it is worth understanding because it changes who you call.

Interior cracking at an addition junction typically shows up as a **vertical line in the drywall or plaster** at the corner where old meets new, sometimes with **nail pops or taping-seam cracks** running alongside it. Doors in that area can stick seasonally. On a plaster wall it may appear as a hairline that opens and closes with the weather.

**The interior crack is drywall responding to structural movement, not a masonry defect.** Sealing the exterior joint correctly reduces water intrusion and stops the masonry deterioration, but **it does not stop the two structures moving** — so the interior crack will keep reappearing if it is simply mudded and painted each time.

Two realistic approaches inside. **Accept and maintain it**: patch on a cycle and treat it as cosmetic, which is what most people do. Or **detail it deliberately**, using a control joint, a trim detail or a corner bead designed to accommodate movement, so the movement has somewhere to go that is not the middle of a painted wall.

What matters diagnostically is the same as outside: **is it stable and seasonal, or progressively widening?** An interior crack that opens further every year, alongside sticking doors and out-of-square openings, is telling you something different from one that breathes with the seasons.

**Fix the exterior joint for the water. Decide about the interior separately, for appearance.** Conflating the two leads people to spend masonry money hoping to solve a drywall symptom.

## Chicagoland Context

This is a very common local condition, and the housing stock explains it.

The region is full of **pre-1940 brick bungalows, two-flats and worker cottages** that acquired rear additions, sunrooms, expanded kitchens, attached garages and second-floor dormers across the following eighty years. Each of those is a separate structure on a separate footing, meeting century-old masonry.

Add **heavy clay soils** that swell and shrink seasonally, **dozens of freeze-thaw crossings each winter**, and original brick that is softer and more absorbent than whatever the addition was built from — and the junction becomes the most predictable crack location on the entire building.

## If You Are Planning an Addition Now

Worth a paragraph for anyone at the design stage rather than the repair stage.

**Ask the builder how the junction will be detailed**, and expect a real answer. A deliberate movement joint designed in at the start — proper backer rod and sealant, with the brick coursing terminated cleanly rather than toothed in — gives a joint that works from day one instead of a crack that appears in year three.

It also gives you the best available brick match, since **sourcing happens while somebody is actively buying material** rather than a decade later from a salvage yard.

**Nobody regrets a well-detailed junction.** Plenty of people inherit a badly detailed one.

## Maintenance

- **Inspect the junction annually**, on both the exterior and the interior side.
- **Mark and date any crack** so you know whether it is moving.
- **Keep water off it** — check for a downspout or gutter seam discharging near the junction.
- **Re-seal on a cycle.** Sealant is a wear item; a joint sealed once is not sealed forever.
- **Do not let anybody repoint it with rigid mortar** and tell you that will hold.
- **Watch the interior** for staining or damp at the junction wall.

The crack at your addition is not evidence that someone botched the job. It is two buildings, two foundations and two ages of masonry doing what they were always going to do at the line where they meet.
