A board or owner gets three masonry bids. The high one is more than double the low one. The reasonable conclusion is that somebody is overcharging — and the usual reality is less satisfying: **all three are honest prices for three different jobs**, because nobody told them what job to price.

Masonry is unusually prone to this. The work is quantity-driven, the quantities are partly hidden inside the wall, and the specification choices that matter most — cut-out depth, mortar hardness, how much of an elevation — are invisible in a finished job. A vague scope does not produce a competitive market. It produces noise.

## Why Masonry Bids Diverge

**Quantity is a judgement call.** "Repoint deteriorated joints" invites each bidder to decide what counts as deteriorated. One says 20% of an elevation; one says 60%. Both are defensible; the prices are not comparable.

**Depth is invisible in the finished work.** A joint cut to a proper depth and a joint skimmed a quarter inch look identical on day one. Only one is still there in six years.

**Mortar specification is a real cost.** Matching colour, sand and texture — and specifying hardness appropriate to the units — takes time and sometimes custom mixing. A bidder assuming bagged grey mortar prices a different job.

**Access is a large, separable number.** Pipe staging versus a lift versus rope access, plus overhead protection and permits, can rival the masonry cost on a tight or tall site.

**Documentation has a price.** COI, lien waivers, submittals, phasing and resident notice all take administrative time. Bidders who assume none of it is required come in lower.

None of that is fixed by more bidders. It is fixed by one scope.

## The Scope Skeleton

### 1. The building and the condition record

Address, building count, construction type, and a **condition record mapped by elevation** with photographs tied to locations. Bidders who can see what you saw price closer to it.

### 2. Quantities, stated by location

The single highest-value section. For each elevation:

- Approximate **percentage or square footage** of joints to be repointed
- **Count** of units to be replaced
- **Linear feet** of sealant, coping joint or control joint
- **Count** of lintels to be treated or replaced
- Parapet **linear feet**, and whether repointed or rebuilt

Approximations are fine and expected. Silence is not.

### 3. Cut-out depth and method

State the depth — commonly to sound mortar at roughly two to two and a half times the joint width — and the method, including where hand-raking is required instead of grinding to protect soft or historic units.

### 4. Mortar specification

Type and mix, colour and sand match requirement, joint profile to be tooled, and the governing rule that **the mortar must not be harder than the units**. On older Chicago masonry that means a lime-rich, low-Portland mix; the reasoning is set out in [lime vs. Portland mortar](/blog/lime-vs-portland-mortar).

### 5. Unit sources and matching

Whether replacement brick is to be reclaimed, current production, or salvaged from a concealed elevation, and who approves the match.

### 6. Access, protection and site logistics

Access method and who provides it; overhead protection over walkways and entrances; parking and drive-aisle management; permits; working hours; resident or tenant notice; daily cleanup. Say who is responsible for each.

### 7. Sequencing and phasing

Phase boundaries, the order of elevations, and any fixed constraints — school hours, service times, tenant trading hours, seasonal cutoffs.

### 8. Unit prices for uncertain quantities

Per brick replaced beyond the base quantity, per linear foot of additional repointing, per lintel, per square foot of additional parapet rebuild. This is the mechanism that makes discovery survivable — see [hidden conditions and change orders](/blog/hidden-conditions-change-orders-masonry-repair).

### 9. Submittals and mockups

Mortar samples, a small approved **test panel** for colour and tooling on visible elevations, and unit samples for approval before ordering.

### 10. Documentation and administrative requirements

COI naming the owner or association, W-9, lien waivers, written schedule, change-order procedure, warranty terms, and closeout photographs.

### 11. Bid form

Require the base scope priced as a lump sum, phases priced separately, unit prices listed, and exclusions stated explicitly. A bid form is what converts three proposals into one comparison.

## The Same Line Item, Written Two Ways

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Tuckpoint building as needed" | "Repoint approximately 45% of joints on the north and west elevations, locations mapped in the attached condition record; cut to sound mortar, minimum 3/4 inch or 2.5× joint width, whichever is greater" |
| "Replace damaged brick" | "Replace approximately 120 units, base quantity; unit price stated for units beyond base; reclaimed common brick matched to existing, sample approved before installation" |
| "Repair lintels as required" | "Treat or replace 6 identified lintels (locations mapped); scope includes shoring, through-wall flashing at head, and rebuild of masonry above; unit price stated for additional lintels found" |
| "Match existing mortar" | "Type N, colour and sand matched to existing; concave tooled profile; 2 ft × 2 ft test panel required for approval before general work proceeds" |
| "Contractor to provide access" | "Pipe staging on north and west elevations, provided by contractor; overhead protected walkway at main entrance for the duration; permits by contractor; parking staging per attached site plan" |
| "Repair parapet" | "Rebuild 90 linear feet of parapet to 4 courses below coping; reset existing limestone coping; install through-wall flashing under coping; repoint remaining 140 linear feet" |
| "Work to be completed promptly" | "Phase 1 north/west elevations, substantial completion by 15 October; no repointing below 40°F sustained without approved cold-weather protection" |

## What Drives the Cost of the Work You're Scoping

Understanding these helps you write realistic quantities and read the bids you get:

- **Elevation area** and the genuine percentage of joints and units that have failed
- **Cut-out depth**, and how hard the existing mortar is to remove
- **Access method** — a lift on an open site versus pipe staging on a zero-lot-line building
- **Overhead protection** wherever work sits above a walkway or entrance
- **Parapet, lintel and sill work** on the same elevations
- **Match difficulty** for brick, stone and mortar, especially on historic or discontinued units
- **Phasing, off-hours or occupancy constraints**
- **Season**, since curing sets the calendar as much as labour does

A scope that ignores these is not cheaper to build — it just moves the cost into a change order later.

## Levelling the Bids You Get Back

1. **Read the exclusions first.** That is where the difference usually lives.
2. **Check quantities against yours.** A bidder who re-took the quantities and disagrees is telling you something useful — ask why.
3. **Compare cut-out depth and mortar spec**, not the total.
4. **Confirm access is included** and by what method.
5. **Compare unit prices**, which reveal how a bidder will behave on discovery.
6. **Check the schedule** against your constraints and the season.
7. **Verify insurance and references** for work of the same type and scale — not just any masonry.
8. **Ask each bidder what they would add to the scope.** The best answer is often worth more than the lowest price.

## Prequalify Before You Send It Out

A perfect scope sent to the wrong list still produces an unusable comparison. Before issuing, confirm each bidder:

- **Does this type and scale of work** — association multi-building, occupied commercial, institutional, historic — not just "masonry"
- **Carries insurance at the limits your owner or association requires**, and will name the right entity on the certificate
- **Has references on comparable buildings**, ideally ones you can drive past
- **Will provide lien waivers** as a matter of course
- **Has capacity in the season you need**, which in Chicagoland is a real constraint by late summer

Three prequalified bidders on one scope beats six unqualified ones every time.

## What to Avoid Writing

- **"Repair as needed"** — unpriceable, and an invitation to price low
- **"Match existing"** with no approval mechanism or sample requirement
- **"Contractor to verify all quantities"** with no base quantity given — this shifts the entire scope risk into the bid, and honest bidders pad while optimistic ones do not
- **Naming a proprietary product** with no equivalent allowed, which narrows the field without improving the wall
- **Silence on access**, the most common source of apparent bid variance
- **Silence on what happens if more is found**, which guarantees the conversation happens at the worst possible moment

## Chicagoland Context

Two regional realities should shape any scope written here.

**The masonry is old and mixed.** Chicago's bungalows, two-flats, courtyard buildings, greystones and pre-war commercial stock are frequently **hard face brick on the street elevation and softer common brick on the sides and rear** — which means one mortar specification across a whole building is wrong somewhere. A scope should specify by elevation. The behaviour of the local material is covered in [Chicago common brick and why it crumbles](/blog/chicago-common-brick-why-it-crumbles).

**The season is short and the freeze-thaw is severe.** Mortar needs sustained above-freezing conditions to cure, so a scope with an unrealistic schedule either gets rushed or slips into cold weather. Stating seasonal limits protects the work and stops a bidder from winning on a schedule they cannot honour.

## Do You Need a Design Professional?

For repointing, unit replacement and typical envelope work, a well-documented contractor assessment converted into a scope is often sufficient — and considerably faster. Bring in an engineer or architect when a wall has **moved**, when a **structural element** is involved, when the project is **large or publicly bid**, or when a board needs an independent specifier separate from the bidders. Those cases are the minority, but they are exactly the ones where getting it wrong is expensive.

## The Short Version

You cannot buy masonry work competitively without telling every bidder the same thing. State the quantities by elevation, the cut-out depth, the mortar specification, the unit sources, the access method, the phasing, the unit prices for what cannot be known, and the paperwork you require.

Do that and a wide bid spread becomes genuinely informative — it means somebody disagrees with your quantities or your method, and that is a conversation worth having. Skip it and the spread tells you nothing at all.
