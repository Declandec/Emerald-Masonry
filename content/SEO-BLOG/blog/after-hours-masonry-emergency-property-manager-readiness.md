## What actually falls off buildings around here

Masonry emergencies in Chicagoland are not random. They cluster.

**Parapet and coping material.** The most exposed masonry on any flat-roofed building, weathered on three surfaces with no heated space behind it, standing directly over sidewalks and entries. This is the most common source of falling material. See [parapet wall failure signs](/blog/parapet-wall-failure-signs-commercial-buildings).

**Brick above an opening.** A corroded lintel expands, lifts the masonry it carries, and eventually a displaced unit lets go — over a door people use every day.

**Spalled face material** from a wall that has been saturating and freezing for several winters, typically after a hard freeze-thaw swing.

**Cornices, terra cotta and cast stone ornament**, held by internal anchors that corrode invisibly. A unit can look sound and be held by nothing. See [terra cotta and cast stone façade repair](/blog/terra-cotta-cast-stone-facade-repair-chicago).

**Balcony slab edges** on multi-family buildings, where embedded reinforcement corrodes and pushes the surface off from within.

**Impact damage** — a vehicle into a dock corner, a bollard that was never there, or a delivery truck against a storefront pier.

Three of those six are **overhead by definition**. That is why overhead masonry outranks everything cosmetic in any maintenance plan.

## The first twenty minutes

In order, and the order matters.

### 1. Secure the drop zone

Before photographs, before phone calls. Barricade or cordon the area beneath the failure — sidewalk, entry, walkway, parking stalls — and make it wide enough. Material that falls from height does not land directly below; it bounces and skitters.

If an entrance is under a compromised parapet or lintel, **close it and reroute**, with clear signage. Cones do not stop masonry. A partially restricted entry is an entry people will still use.

### 2. Get people away and keep them away

Residents and tenants will come to look. That is human and it is dangerous. A physical barrier plus a person, if you have one, beats tape alone.

### 3. Photograph, if it is safe

Before anything is moved or swept up:

- The **failed material where it landed**, with something for scale
- The **wall it came from**, from ground level and from any safe vantage
- **Comparable undamaged elevations** on the same building
- Any **weather evidence** — ice, standing water, a blocked scupper
- A **wide shot** establishing the relationship between the failure and what is below it

This is the evidence a carrier will want and the single hardest thing to reconstruct a week later.

### 4. Call a mason who is already in your file

Which is the entire point of this article.

## The file, and why it has to exist beforehand

Everything below takes an hour to assemble on a quiet Tuesday and is impossible to assemble on a Saturday evening.

**A named masonry contractor, already onboarded.** Not a search result. A firm whose COI and W-9 are in your vendor file, whose after-hours number you have, and who has seen the property before. Vendor onboarding is the step that turns a two-hour response into a two-day one.

**A current certificate of insurance.** Emerald provides COI, W-9 and lien waivers on request, and they should sit in the file before anything is needed. See [masonry contractor insurance and what to verify](/blog/masonry-contractor-insurance-what-to-verify-chicago).

**Documented emergency spending authority.** What can you authorise, up to what amount, without a board or owner sign-off? Most management agreements and association bylaws grant hazard-mitigation authority. Know your number before you need it, and know who to call above it.

**Baseline photographs of every elevation, dated.** This is the one people skip and regret. A set of dated photographs from repeatable positions, refreshed annually, is what lets you demonstrate that a condition was *not* present last spring. That is a causation argument, and causation is what an insurance claim turns on.

**A copy of the most recent condition assessment**, if one exists, and any façade inspection documentation the building is subject to.

**A resident and tenant notification template.** Drafted calmly in advance, not written at 9pm.

## Stabilisation is a different job than repair

This distinction matters contractually and it matters on a claim.

**Stabilisation** is making the hazard safe: removing loose material, netting or screening a face, shoring an opening, erecting overhead protection, barricading. It cannot be scoped in advance, so it is reasonably billed by the day or as time and materials — capped, with daily documentation.

**Permanent repair** is a defined scope: rebuild this section of parapet, replace these lintels, repoint this elevation. It should be scoped, priced, approved and scheduled through the normal process. For contract structure on work whose quantity is genuinely unknowable until the wall is opened, see [unit price versus lump sum](/blog/unit-price-vs-lump-sum-masonry-contract).

Blending the two into one open-ended number is bad for the owner and invites a dispute with the carrier over both.

## What to say to residents and tenants

The construction part of an emergency is usually shorter than the communication part, and the communication part is where reputations are made or lost.

Three messages, in this order.

**Immediately — safety and scope of restriction.** What area is closed, what route to use instead, whether parking is affected, and that it is being addressed. Short. No speculation about cause, and no estimate of duration you cannot stand behind.

**Within a day — what happened and what is next.** Plain language: a section of masonry failed, the area is secured, a mason has assessed it, and here is the next step and the expected timeline. People tolerate disruption well when they understand it and badly when they are guessing.

**When the plan is set — the permanent repair.** Dates, duration, what will be noisy, what access is affected, and a named contact. See [masonry work in occupied buildings and tenant communication](/blog/masonry-work-in-occupied-buildings-tenant-communication).

Two things to avoid. Do not assign cause in writing before it is established — "the storm damaged the parapet" is a claims position, not an observation, and it can be contradicted later by your own photographs. And do not promise a completion date that depends on curing temperatures in February.

## The liability question nobody enjoys

It has to be said plainly, because it changes how urgently overhead conditions get treated.

Masonry that falls from a building onto a place where people walk or park is a foreseeable-risk problem, and foreseeability is established by what the owner knew or should have known. A condition assessment that identified a deteriorating parapet two years ago, filed and not acted on, is a different situation from one that was identified and scheduled.

That cuts both ways, and the practical conclusion is not to avoid assessments — it is that **an assessment should produce a dated plan, and the plan should be followed or consciously re-prioritised in writing.** Documented deferral with a stated reason is defensible. Silence is not.

This is also why the survey that follows an emergency matters more than the repair itself: it converts an unknown into a managed item with dates against it.

## The Illinois weather problem

A February failure creates a specific difficulty: **mortar needs workable temperatures to cure properly.**

That does not mean nothing happens until spring. It means the sequence becomes:

1. **Secure immediately** — remove the hazard, protect below.
2. **Assess** the full extent, from the roof where relevant.
3. **Protect through the winter** — netting, screening, temporary weather protection, maintained and inspected.
4. **Scope and approve** during the winter, so the work is ready to go rather than starting the procurement in April.
5. **Execute** when conditions allow.

Buildings that handle winter failures badly are the ones that wait until March to start scoping. See [cold-weather masonry repair in Illinois](/blog/cold-weather-masonry-repair-illinois).

## When an engineer belongs in it

Call one where:

- A **wall or parapet is out of plane** — leaning, bowing, or bulging
- A **section has separated** from the structure behind it
- **Reinforcement is exposed** in a slab, balcony or lintel condition
- The **extent of the failure is unclear** from a visual assessment
- **Movement appears ongoing** rather than historic
- The building is subject to a **façade inspection regime** and the failure is reportable

A contractor who says "you need an engineer for this" before quoting is giving you a better answer than one who quotes around it.

## What the aftermath should produce

The most valuable output of an emergency is not the repair. It is the **survey that follows it**.

A parapet that dropped material almost never dropped it from the only bad spot. A lintel that failed over one opening has siblings on the same elevation, installed the same year, from the same unprotected steel, in the same exposure. The emergency is a symptom; the elevation is the condition.

So after stabilisation, the right next step is a full assessment of that elevation and its counterparts, with a priority order and a phasing plan — which is what turns an unbudgeted crisis into a managed capital item. ## Two failures that look alike and are not

Worth separating, because the response differs and the claim differs.

**A sudden event.** An impact, a wind event, an ice storm that loaded a parapet, a nearby excavation or teardown that shook a party wall. The evidence is usually localised, recent, and inconsistent with the condition of comparable elevations — which is exactly why photographing undamaged walls matters. See [adjacent construction and teardown damage](/blog/adjacent-construction-teardown-damage-masonry-chicago).

**An accumulated condition that finally let go.** Years of freeze-thaw on saturated masonry, a lintel that has been rust-jacking since the last decade, coping joints open long enough to soak the core of a parapet. The evidence is diffuse: similar deterioration across the elevation, efflorescence, previous patch repairs, staining that predates the event.

Most real failures are the second kind with the first kind as a trigger — a windy night is what finally dislodges a unit that had lost its bond three winters ago. Say that accurately rather than reaching for whichever version is convenient, because your own photographs will eventually be read by someone else.

## The preparedness checklist

Assemble this once, review it annually:

- [ ] Masonry contractor named, onboarded, COI and W-9 on file
- [ ] After-hours contact number, tested
- [ ] Emergency spending authority documented, with escalation contact
- [ ] Baseline elevation photographs, dated, refreshed annually
- [ ] Most recent condition assessment on file
- [ ] Resident / tenant notification template drafted
- [ ] Barricade and signage materials on site or sourceable same-day
- [ ] Insurance policy and carrier contact accessible outside business hours
- [ ] Structural engineer contact, for the conditions listed above
- [ ] Known overhead conditions already catalogued, with anything urgent already scheduled

That last line is the one that prevents most of the calls.
