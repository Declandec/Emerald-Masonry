The call is always some version of the same thing: a tenant on the fourth floor has water at an exterior wall, and can somebody come and seal it.

## Why the Symptom and the Cause Are in Different Places

A masonry wall is not a solid barrier. Depending on its construction it is either a **cavity or veneer wall** — brick, an air space, flashing and weep holes, designed to let water in and drain it back out — or **solid multi-wythe masonry** that manages water by mass and sound joints.

In both, water that gets past the outer face travels. It runs down the back of the brick, along ledges and shelf angles, across flashing, and out wherever it finds a gap. That path can be several feet horizontally and several floors vertically from where it entered.

So the useful question is never "where is it wet?" It is **"what is directly above and outboard of the wet spot, and what failed up there?"**

## Step One: Interview and Document

Before anyone goes outside, get this from the tenant and from your own records:

- **Exact location** — floor, elevation, height above floor, proximity to a window or corner
- **When it started**, and whether it has happened before at the same spot
- **Weather at the time** — driving rain, steady rain, snowmelt, wind direction
- **Duration** — during the storm, or hours after
- **Prior complaints** at that location or the one above
- **Any recent work** — roofing, window replacement, sign installation, tuckpointing

That last bullet solves a surprising share of cases on its own. New leaks that begin shortly after window replacement, roof work or sign anchoring usually trace to that work.

## Step Two: Correlate With Weather

This narrows the field faster than anything else on site.

| Pattern | Likely path |
|---|---|
| Only during **wind-driven rain**, stops when wind drops | Wall face, sealant joints, window perimeters |
| During and after **steady heavy rain** | Roof, roof-to-wall transition, scuppers, drains |
| During **snowmelt** or thaw, not during rain | Coping and parapet, ice damming, roof edge |
| **Continuous or unrelated to weather** | Plumbing, HVAC condensate, not masonry |
| Appears **hours after** rain stops | Slow path through the wall assembly — established saturation |

That fourth row matters: a meaningful fraction of "masonry leaks" turn out to be mechanical, and ruling it out early saves a lift rental.

## Step Three: Inspect the Elevation Above — From the Wall

This is where most triage fails. An inspection from the sidewalk cannot see the elements that actually leak. Get a lift or staging and work the elevation **above and outboard** of the interior symptom, checking in rough order of likelihood:

### Parapet and coping

Open coping joints let water directly into the top of the wall. On flat-roofed commercial buildings this is the leading cause, and the parapet is also the most deteriorated masonry on the property — the reasoning is set out in [parapet wall failure signs on commercial buildings](/blog/parapet-wall-failure-signs-commercial-buildings).

### Through-wall flashing

If flashing is missing, damaged, or was never lapped correctly, water entering the cavity has no route out and finds the interior instead.

### Sealant joints

Window and door perimeters, panel and material transitions, control and expansion joints. Split, hardened or debonded sealant is a direct path — and one of the fastest to confirm visually.

### Lintels and shelf angles

Corroding steel opens the joint above the opening from behind. Orange staining is the tell, covered in [lintel rust stains and orange streaks on brick](/blog/lintel-rust-stains-orange-streaks-brick).

### Sills and projections

A sill that has lost its slope or drip edge stops throwing water clear and runs it back onto — and into — the wall beneath.

### Weep holes

Blocked, mortared or caulked weeps convert a drainage wall into a wall that holds water. They must stay open, as explained in [weep holes in masonry walls](/blog/weep-holes-masonry-walls).

### Mortar joints

Eroded, cracked or sandy joints across the field wall — usually a contributing factor rather than the sole path, but on badly deteriorated elevations it is enough on its own.

### Penetrations

Vents, conduit, hose bibs, light fixtures, sign anchors and abandoned fastener holes. Anchoring into brick faces rather than joints creates leaks, as covered in [anchoring into brick](/blog/anchoring-into-brick-awnings-handrails-signs).

## Step Four: Water Testing, Done Properly

Where visual inspection leaves more than one candidate, controlled testing settles it. The discipline matters more than the equipment:

1. **Start low, work upward.** Test the lowest candidate first; if you start at the parapet you have contaminated every lower test.
2. **One variable at a time.** Test a single joint, sill or opening, then stop and observe.
3. **Give it time.** Some paths take twenty minutes or more to surface; moving on too fast produces a false negative.
4. **Mask adjacent areas** so water cannot reach the element you are not testing.
5. **Have someone inside** watching the symptom location, in communication with the person outside.
6. **Record every test** — location, duration, result. That log is the evidence.

## Two Worked Cases

**Case one — the fourth-floor corner office.** Water appears at the top of an exterior wall beside a window, only during storms from the west. Sealant at the window perimeter looks intact. Inspection from a lift finds the **coping joints on the parapet directly above** open along a six-foot run, with efflorescence on the parapet's roof-side face. Water was entering the top of the wall, running down inside the assembly, and exiting at the first horizontal interruption — the window head two floors below. Repointing the field wall, which was the original proposal, would have changed nothing.

**Case two — the ground-floor retail unit.** Damp appears at the base of a demising wall after every heavy rain. The masonry above is sound. The path turns out to be **blocked weep holes** at the base course, buried under a landscaping bed that had been built up over the years, plus a downspout discharging into that same bed. The cavity had no way to drain, so it filled. The repair was clearing weeps, regrading the bed and extending the downspout — a fraction of the tuckpointing scope that had been quoted.

Both cases share the same lesson: the elevation *above and outboard* of the symptom held the answer, and neither would have been found from the sidewalk.

## What Not To Do

- **Do not seal the interior face.** It hides the symptom and traps water in the assembly.
- **Do not apply a film-forming coating** to the exterior. On a wall that is already wet it spalls the brick face from behind.
- **Do not caulk weep holes** — that is the drain.
- **Do not repoint the whole elevation** as a first response. If the path is a coping joint or a flashing failure, repointing changes nothing and costs a great deal.
- **Do not accept a proposal that skips diagnosis.** "We'll seal the wall" priced before anyone went up on a lift is a guess.

## What Drives the Cost of the Investigation and Repair

- **Access** — a lift or staging to reach the elevation, which is the main cost of investigation itself
- **Number of candidate paths** and how much controlled testing is required
- **Whether a small area must be opened** to confirm a concealed condition
- **The repair scope that follows** — coping and flashing work versus sealant renewal versus repointing an elevation
- **Height and overhead protection** where the work sits above an entrance
- **Tenant coordination** and whether work must be phased or run off-hours
- **Season**, since testing and mortar curing both need above-freezing conditions

Investigation is a small fraction of the repair, and it is the part that decides whether the repair works.

## Chicagoland Context

Two regional factors make this urgent rather than routine. **Wind-driven rain** off the lake and across open suburban sites loads west and south elevations far harder than a rain gauge suggests, so the leaking elevation is predictable. And **freeze-thaw cycling** — dozens of cycles each winter, water expanding roughly nine percent every time — means an unresolved water path does not hold steady over a season; it enlarges. A complaint logged in October and diagnosed in April is a materially bigger repair.

The region's older commercial and multi-family stock adds its own wrinkle: solid multi-wythe walls with no cavity, no flashing and no weeps, where the only defence is sound joints and sound coping. On those buildings, deferred repointing is the leak path.

## Building a Triage Habit

- **Log every complaint** with location, date and weather — patterns emerge across a season
- **Walk elevations after major storms**, using the [post-storm masonry inspection checklist](/blog/post-storm-masonry-inspection-checklist)
- **Inspect from a lift annually** on buildings over two storeys
- **Keep dated photographs** by elevation for comparison and for claims
- **Track prior repairs** and where they were, so recurrence is obvious
- **Watch for efflorescence** — it means an established path, not a new one

## The Short Version

Water shows up where the assembly lets it out, not where it came in. Document the symptom, correlate it with the weather, inspect the elevation above from a lift, and test methodically from the bottom up. Only then decide what to repair.

Sealing where it is wet is fast, cheap, and almost always wrong — and it usually costs more than the diagnosis would have.
