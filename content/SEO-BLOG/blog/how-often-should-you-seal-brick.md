# How Often Should You Seal Brick? A Straight Answer for Chicagoland Homeowners

If you're asking how often you should seal brick, here's the honest answer up front: a quality **breathable masonry water repellent** typically lasts about **7–10 years** before it needs reapplication — and some premium silane/siloxane products stretch closer to **10–15 years** on sound, well-prepped masonry. Brick is not a sponge you have to keep waterproofing, and it's not a wooden deck that needs a fresh coat every couple of years. A brick wall is engineered to absorb a little water during a storm and then dry back out. Your real protection against water damage isn't a sealer at all — it's **sound mortar joints, working gutters and downspouts, and good flashing.** Sealing has a genuine place, but it's a targeted tool for specific situations, not a default chore for every brick home. Below is exactly how often to reseal when you do seal, how to tell it's time, and — just as important — when you shouldn't seal at all.

## What sealing brick actually does

A proper **brick sealer** is a **penetrating, breathable water repellent.** It soaks into the surface of the brick and mortar and lines the pores so the wall **sheds liquid water** — rain beads up and runs off instead of soaking in — while still letting **water vapor escape** from inside the wall. That last part is the whole game. Masonry always carries some moisture, and it has to be able to breathe and dry outward. A good repellent slows water going *in* without trapping moisture *in*.

This is very different from what most people picture when they hear "seal." A breathable repellent is:

- **Not a glossy "wet look" coating.** Those are film-forming and change the appearance.
- **Not paint or elastomeric coating.** Those form a skin over the brick.
- **Not a permanent waterproof barrier.** Nothing makes brick permanently impervious, and you wouldn't want it to be.

The products that do this well on our region's brick are generally **silane/siloxane-based** penetrating repellents. They go on looking wet and dry nearly invisible, leaving the brick's natural color and texture intact.

## The most important rule: never seal over problems

Before anything else, understand the one rule that matters more than the reapplication schedule:

> **Never seal over failed mortar, cracks, or spalling brick, and never use a film-forming sealer or paint on exterior brick in a freeze-thaw climate.** Both mistakes trap water inside the wall and make the damage dramatically worse.

Here's why. If your **mortar joints** are open, crumbling, or cracked, water is already getting *behind* the brick face. Spray a repellent — or worse, a film coating — over the outside, and you've just made it harder for that trapped water to escape. Come winter, that water freezes, expands, and pushes the face of the brick off. That's **spalling**, and film-forming sealers and paint are a classic cause of it on Chicago-area masonry.

So the correct order of operations is always:

1. **Inspect and diagnose** the masonry.
2. **Tuckpoint** failed mortar joints and **repair or replace** any damaged brick.
3. **Let it cure and dry.**
4. **Then** apply a breathable repellent — but only if there's a real reason to.

Sealing is a finishing step on **sound** masonry. It is never a substitute for **[tuckpointing](/services/tuckpointing)** or **[brick repair](/services/brick-repair)**. If a contractor offers to "just seal it" over visibly failing joints, that's a red flag — you can read more in our guide on **[what repointing actually means](/blog/what-repointing-actually-means)**.

## How long does brick sealer last?

When you do seal sound brick with a quality breathable repellent, here's the realistic lifespan:

| Product / situation | Typical service life before resealing |
|---|---|
| Standard breathable water repellent, average exposure | ~7–10 years |
| Premium silane/siloxane, sound brick, good prep | ~10–15 years |
| Harsh weather elevation (south/west, wind-driven rain) | Shorter end of the range |
| Sheltered or low-exposure wall | Longer end of the range |
| Any sealer applied over damp or damaged brick | Fails early — sometimes within a year or two |

The single biggest factor is **surface preparation and brick condition at the time of sealing.** A repellent applied to clean, dry, sound brick performs to its full life. One sprayed over dirt, moss, damp masonry, or failing joints fails fast — no matter what the label promises.

**Exposure** is the next factor. In Chicagoland, south- and west-facing walls take the brunt of sun, **wind-driven rain**, and the worst of the **freeze-thaw** punishment, so those elevations wear a repellent faster than a protected north wall or a wall under a deep eave. It's completely normal for one side of a house to need resealing before another.

## How to tell it's time to reseal

You don't have to guess or track a calendar. The brick will tell you.

### The water-drop absorption test

This is the simplest, most reliable check, and you can do it yourself from the ground:

- On a dry day, **splash or mist water** onto the brick surface.
- If the water **beads up and runs off**, the repellent is still doing its job — leave it alone.
- If the water **soaks straight in and darkens the brick** within a minute or so, the repellent has worn off.

A wall that darkens and drinks water isn't an emergency — sound brick is *supposed* to absorb some water and dry out. But it does tell you the repellent layer is gone, so if you sealed for a specific reason, it may be time to reapply.

### Other signs

- **Persistent darkening when wet** that takes a long time to dry.
- **Returning [efflorescence](/blog/why-efflorescence-keeps-coming-back)** — the white chalky bloom — on brick that is otherwise sound. That's water moving through the wall and evaporating out.
- **A previously "beading" wall that stopped beading.**

If, on the other hand, you're seeing **spalling, cracked brick, or crumbling mortar**, that's not a resealing question — that's a repair question. Reach for **[brick repair](/services/brick-repair)** and tuckpointing, not a sprayer.

## When you should seal — and when you shouldn't

This is where most homeowners are steered wrong. Sealing is genuinely useful in some cases and pointless or harmful in others.

| Situation | Seal? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Sound newer brick on an exposed south/west weather elevation | Often yes | Reduces water absorption where the wall takes the most punishment |
| A sound masonry chimney | Often yes | Chimneys are exposed on all sides and above the roof; a breathable repellent helps after any needed repairs |
| Recurring efflorescence on otherwise-sound brick | Consider it | Cutting water absorption can reduce it — after confirming there's no active leak |
| High-absorption brick that drinks water and stays damp | Consider it | Some soft or older brick benefits from reduced absorption |
| Open or crumbling mortar joints | No — repair first | Sealing traps water behind the wall; tuckpoint first |
| Cracked or spalling brick | No — repair first | The defect must be fixed before any sealer |
| Already-painted brick | No | Paint already traps moisture; adding sealer compounds it |
| As a substitute for tuckpointing | Never | A repellent cannot replace sound mortar joints |
| A sheltered, sound wall with no water issues | Usually unnecessary | Brick is designed to shed and dry on its own |

Notice the pattern: **seal for a specific, identifiable reason on sound masonry — not as a blanket "protect the house" default.** If someone can't tell you *why* your particular wall needs sealing, it probably doesn't.

## The sealing process, done right

When Emerald does seal a wall, the work is more about preparation than spraying:

1. **Inspect the masonry** and identify any mortar or brick that needs repair.
2. **Tuckpoint and repair first** — open joints, cracked brick, failed sills or crowns all get addressed before anything is sealed.
3. **Clean the surface** of dirt, biological growth, and loose material so the repellent can penetrate.
4. **Let the masonry dry** thoroughly — sealing damp brick traps moisture.
5. **Apply a breathable penetrating repellent** at the correct coverage rate, usually flood-coated so it fully saturates the pores, with a second pass where the product calls for it.
6. **Avoid film-forming products and paint** entirely on exterior brick.

Done this way, the repellent disappears into the brick, keeps its natural look, and gives you years of reduced water absorption on masonry that was sound to begin with.

## Why Chicagoland brick is a special case

Our climate is exactly why homeowners here ask about sealing more than in milder regions — and exactly why the "breathable only, sound brick only" rules matter so much. Chicagoland brick endures:

- **Hard freeze-thaw cycling.** Water that gets into brick and mortar freezes, expands, and thaws dozens of times each winter. Trapped water — behind a film coating or over failed joints — is what turns that cycling into spalling.
- **Lake-effect moisture and wind-driven rain** that drive water into south and west elevations.
- **Old, soft, absorbent brick** on the region's century-old **bungalows, greystones, and two-flats**, alongside newer brick-veneer subdivisions. Different brick, different absorption, different sealing calculus.

On the older, softer brick common across Chicago and its suburbs, a **film-forming sealer or paint is especially destructive**, because that brick needs to breathe even more than modern hard-fired brick. When in doubt on historic masonry, the answer is almost always "repair the mortar, skip the film coating." ## Maintenance and prevention: what actually protects brick

If you take one thing from this article, let it be this: **sealing is near the bottom of the list of things that keep a brick wall healthy.** Far more important, and far more cost-effective:

- **Keep your mortar joints sound.** Tuckpointing failed joints is the number-one protection against water intrusion. It's the fix that a sealer can never replace.
- **Manage your water.** Clean gutters, downspouts that carry water away from the wall, and proper grading prevent more brick damage than any sealer.
- **Keep flashing, chimney crowns, caps, and sills in good repair.** Most "my brick wall is leaking" calls trace back to one of these, not to unsealed brick. Our **[chimney repair](/services/chimney-repair)** and **[caulking and joint sealant](/services/caulking)** work often solves the actual water path.
- **Inspect annually**, and after big storms, from the ground.
- **Then, if there's a real reason,** consider a breathable repellent on the sound, repaired wall — and note the water-drop test result so you know roughly when to check it again.

## The bottom line

So, how often should you seal brick? **If your brick genuinely benefits from sealing, plan on reapplying a breathable repellent roughly every 7–10 years — up to 10–15 for premium products on sound, well-prepped masonry — and let the water-drop test, not a calendar, be your guide.** But don't start from the assumption that you must seal at all. Most sound, well-maintained brick sheds and dries on its own, and your mortar joints and water management protect the wall far more than any sealer. Seal for a real reason, seal only sound masonry, repair first, and never trap water behind a film coating.
