## What a step crack actually is

A step crack (also called a stair-step or stepped crack) is a crack that travels through the **mortar joints** of a brick or block wall rather than through the bricks themselves, producing a diagonal, staircase-shaped line.

It follows the joints for a simple reason: the mortar is the weakest, most flexible path through the wall. When the wall is stressed — pushed, pulled, or settled — it fails along the joints of least resistance, stepping up and over from one brick course to the next. So a step crack isn't random. It's a map of where stress is moving through the masonry.

That's the key mental shift: **a step crack is a symptom, not a disease.** The crack is the wall reporting movement somewhere in the structure. Fix only the crack and you've silenced the messenger while the underlying problem keeps working.

## What causes step cracks in brick walls

Almost every step crack traces back to differential movement — one part of the wall or foundation moving relative to another. In the Chicago area, a handful of causes account for the great majority.

### Foundation settlement

The most common structural cause. When part of a foundation settles — sinks — more than the rest, the wall above it is pulled out of plane and cracks diagonally, usually stepping down toward the low point. Older Chicagoland homes on century-old footings are especially prone to this.

### Expansive clay soils

Much of Cook, DuPage, and Will County sits on clay-rich soil that **swells when wet and shrinks when dry.** This seasonal heave-and-shrink cycle lifts and drops portions of a foundation, and brick walls above register the movement as step cracks. A wet spring followed by a dry summer is a classic trigger.

### Water and drainage problems

Clogged gutters, downspouts dumping at the foundation, poor grading, and saturated soil all concentrate moisture against one part of the footing — causing localized settlement or heave. Water management problems are behind a surprising share of the cracks we see.

### Rusting steel lintels and shelf angles

Above windows, doors, and garage openings, a steel lintel carries the brick. As that hidden steel rusts, it expands — a process called **rust jacking** — and physically lifts and cracks the brick coursing above the opening, often producing a step crack running up from the corner of a window. This is a masonry cause, not a foundation one, and the fix is completely different.

### Thermal movement and overload

Long unrelieved brick walls expand and contract with temperature and, without proper expansion joints, can crack. Added loads or altered structures can do the same. These are less common in typical Chicago housing but real on larger and commercial buildings.

## Cosmetic vs. structural: how to read your crack

Not every step crack is an emergency. The practical question is whether the crack is **stable** (the movement happened once and stopped) or **active** (still moving). Here's how the two tend to differ:

| Indicator | Likely cosmetic / stable | Likely structural / active |
|---|---|---|
| Crack width | Hairline to ~1/8" | Wider than ~1/4", or growing |
| Change over time | Stable for years | Widening over weeks/months |
| Displacement | Faces flush, aligned | One side pushed out / offset |
| Location | Isolated, mid-wall | From corners of windows/doors, or a building corner |
| Companion signs | None | Sticking doors/windows, bowing wall, interior drywall cracks |

A reliable, low-tech test: **mark the ends of the crack and note the date**, or bridge it with a pencil line, and watch for a month or two. A crack that hasn't moved is far less urgent than one visibly opening. When in doubt, get eyes on it.

## When to worry — and when it's urgent

Call for an assessment promptly if you see any of these:

- The crack is **widening** over time, or wider than about a quarter inch.
- There's **offset** — one side of the crack has pushed out past the other.
- The **wall is bowing or bulging**, not just cracked.
- **Doors and windows are sticking**, or you see matching cracks inside.
- The crack runs diagonally **from the corner of the building** or from an opening and keeps climbing.

Any of these can indicate active foundation movement or structural distress. In those cases, a **licensed structural engineer** should evaluate the foundation, and the masonry repair follows their findings. We'll tell you plainly when a wall is past the point where masonry work alone is the answer — that honesty is part of doing the job right.

## How step cracks get repaired the right way

1. **Diagnose the cause.** Before any masonry, we determine *why* the wall moved — settlement, soil, water, a rusted lintel. This is the step cheap repairs skip.
2. **Address the cause.** That may mean correcting drainage and grading, involving a foundation contractor or engineer for active settlement, or replacing a rusted steel lintel. The wall has to stop moving before it's worth fixing the brick.
3. **Repair the masonry.** For stable cracks, that means grinding out the cracked joints and **repointing** with matched mortar, and where bricks are broken or displaced, cutting them out and **rebuilding** the section with matching brick and mortar. Larger cracks can be "stitched" with reinforcement across the joints.
4. **Seal and finish.** Restored joints are tooled to match, and where appropriate the area is sealed to keep water out.

Done this way, the repair lasts because it treats the disease, not just the symptom.

## Materials and technique

Good step-crack repair uses **mortar matched to your existing wall** — color, texture, and, crucially, hardness. On older Chicago brick, that means a softer, lime-based mortar so the joint (not the brick) remains the flexible element. Replacement bricks are sourced to match size, color, and texture, since mismatched brick advertises the repair forever. On veneer walls, we make sure the underlying cause — often water or a lintel — is resolved so the new work stays sound.

Chicago-area masonry faces a punishing combination: **expansive clay soils** that heave and shrink with the seasons, **freeze-thaw cycles** that drive water in and out of any open joint, and a huge stock of **century-old bungalows, two-flats, and greystones** on aging footings. ## Prevention and maintenance

You can't stop soil from being clay, but you can control water, which is behind most movement:

- Keep **gutters clean** and **downspouts** discharging several feet from the foundation.
- Maintain **positive grading** so soil slopes away from the house.
- Keep **mortar joints repointed** so water isn't getting into the wall in the first place.
- Have masonry **inspected periodically**, and act on small cracks before they widen.
