# Mixed-Use Building Masonry Repair: One Wall, Two Kinds of Tenant

**Masonry repair on a mixed-use building — a shop on the ground floor, apartments above — is a single brick façade serving two tenant types with opposite needs.** The retailer needs the door open, the sign visible and the sidewalk clear during trading hours. The residents need to sleep, get up the back stair and keep their windows. The masonry itself fails in a predictable order: the steel over the display window first, the parapet and cornice next, the alley elevation quietly throughout. The right repair is sequenced around all three. Emerald Masonry LLC scopes and repairs mixed-use buildings across Chicago and the Chicagoland suburbs — call **(708) 288-1696**.

Drive Western Avenue, Archer, Cermak, 95th Street, or the old main street of almost any Cook County or DuPage suburb and this building is everywhere: two or three storeys of brick, a wide plate-glass storefront at grade, a sign band above it, flats upstairs reached from a side door or a rear porch off the alley.

These buildings are commercial property with residential obligations attached. That is what makes **mixed-use building masonry repair** different from both a storefront job and an apartment job — and why scopes written for either one alone tend to go wrong.

> On a mixed-use building the masonry is rarely the hard part. The hard part is that every decision about access, timing and noise affects two groups of people who want opposite things. The scope that works is the one that plans for both before a single joint is ground.

## What a mixed-use building actually is, in masonry terms

Most Chicagoland corridor buildings were built between roughly the 1890s and the 1950s. Underneath the variety they share an anatomy:

- **The storefront opening.** A wide display window spanned by a **steel beam or built-up lintel** carrying every storey of brick above it. This is the most heavily loaded and most corrosion-prone element on the building.
- **The sign band.** The strip of brick or stone between the top of the storefront and the second-floor windows, often punched with old sign anchors, conduit and bracket holes.
- **The upper-floor façade.** Face brick, sometimes with limestone sills, lintels and a belt course — residential-scale openings on a commercial wall.
- **The parapet and cornice.** A flat roof hidden behind a parapet, frequently topped with a corbelled brick, pressed-metal or stone cornice that overhangs the sidewalk.
- **The side and rear walls.** Usually softer **Chicago common brick**, facing an alley or a gangway, carrying the rear porch, utility penetrations and the dumpster enclosure.

Each of those zones fails differently, and each one affects a different tenant when it is repaired.

## Causes and warning signs, zone by zone

### Over the storefront

The steel beam over the display window is wrapped in masonry that holds water against it. As it rusts, the corrosion product expands — **rust jacking** — and lifts the brick above. Watch for:

- A **horizontal crack** along the course just above the beam, or **step cracks** rising from the bearing ends
- **Orange rust staining** bleeding down from the beam line onto the sign band
- Brick at the beam ends pushed out of plane, or mortar spalling off in chunks
- Storefront glazing that has started to bind or crack in its frame

Our post on [lintel rust stains and orange streaks](/blog/lintel-rust-stains-orange-streaks-brick) explains what those stains mean.

### On the upper floors

- **Receding or sandy mortar joints**, deepest under window sills
- **Spalling brick** faces, especially below sills and on the north or west elevation
- Cracked or rotated **limestone sills** and loose stone lintels
- Water stains inside the apartments at window heads

### At the roofline

- Open joints in the **coping**, missing coping stones, plant growth
- A cornice that sags, has displaced units, or sheds pieces onto the sidewalk
- Top-floor ceiling stains that came back after a roof replacement

### At the rear and alley

- Soft common brick eroding at grade where salt and splashback hit it
- Cracking where the **rear porch** is anchored into the wall
- Open penetrations around old flues, vents, gas lines and conduit
- Vehicle and dumpster damage at the corners

## Why the order of failure matters

Not every defect is equally urgent, and on a mixed-use building the ranking is driven by **who is underneath it**.

| Zone | Typical failure | Who is exposed | Urgency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cornice / parapet over sidewalk | Loose units, displaced coping | Public, customers, retail staff | Highest — falling-hazard risk |
| Storefront beam / display lintel | Rust jacking, cracked brick above | Retail tenant, structure | High — gets worse every winter |
| Upper-floor façade | Failed joints, spalled brick, sills | Residential tenants (leaks) | Medium — plan within a season |
| Rear / alley elevation | Eroded common brick, porch anchors | Residents, porch users | Medium — check porch anchorage early |
| Sign band | Old anchor holes, open penetrations | Water path into beam | Low alone, but feeds the beam |

The sign band is the sleeper. Every abandoned sign bracket and conduit hole in it is a **water entry point directly above the steel beam**, which is why sealing and patching it belongs in the same scope as the beam repair.

## The process: how a mixed-use façade repair is sequenced

1. **Assessment from every elevation and the roof.** Sound the cornice and parapet, open a joint at the storefront beam bearing to see the steel, document the alley face, and note every tenant entrance, window unit and porch route.
2. **Make the sidewalk safe first.** If anything at the roofline is loose, it comes down or is secured immediately, and **overhead protection** — a covered walkway or sidewalk canopy — goes up before work starts over the public way.
3. **Storefront beam work as a defined event.** Shoring the masonry above, removing the brick around the beam, cleaning or replacing the steel, installing **through-wall flashing** with end dams and weep holes, and rebuilding. This is the step most likely to affect the retailer, so it is planned as a short, scheduled disruption with the entrance kept usable.
4. **Upper-floor repointing and brick replacement.** Grinding or raking joints to a consistent depth, **mortar color matching** to the existing, packing in lifts, tooling to the original profile, and replacing spalled units with matched brick.
5. **Parapet and cornice.** Coping reset or replacement, flashing, and cornice repair or anchorage — coordinated with the roofer if the roof is due.
6. **Rear and alley elevation.** Repointing, replacing eroded brick at grade, sealing penetrations, and checking the porch connections.
7. **Caulking and sealants** at the storefront perimeter, window frames and dissimilar-material joints, then closeout with photos and lien waivers.

> The single most useful sequencing rule on a mixed-use building: fix what is over people's heads first, fix the steel second, and only then make the façade look good.

## Keeping the store open and the residents housed

This is where mixed-use projects are won or lost.

**For the retail tenant:**

- Keep the entrance usable throughout, under a protected walkway
- Agree the noisy and dusty days — grinding and cutting — in advance, and keep them off the tenant's peak trading days where possible
- Keep the sign visible, or agree a temporary sign on the canopy
- Plan the storefront beam work as a short, dated window rather than an open-ended one

**For the residential tenants:**

- Written notice of dates, the elevation being worked, and which windows will be covered
- **Window air conditioners** on the work elevation removed or protected
- The rear stair or porch route kept open, or an alternative agreed
- Work hours that respect the fact that people live there, including those who work nights

## Permits, the public way and Chicago's façade ordinance

Mixed-use buildings sit right on the property line, so most façade work happens **over the public sidewalk**. That usually means a permit for scaffolding or a canopy in the public way, and often a building permit for beam replacement or rebuilding. Requirements differ between the City of Chicago and each suburb — confirm with the local building department before mobilization. Our guide to [masonry repair permits in the Chicago suburbs](/blog/masonry-repair-permits-chicago-suburbs) covers the common cases.

In Chicago, buildings above the ordinance height threshold are subject to periodic **façade inspection requirements**; many two- and three-storey corridor buildings fall below it, but taller mixed-use buildings do not. See [Chicago's façade inspection ordinance](/blog/chicago-facade-inspection-ordinance-masonry) for how it works. Separately, Illinois owners carry a general duty to keep their buildings from shedding onto the public — [falling masonry liability](/blog/falling-masonry-liability-owner-duty-to-inspect-il) is worth reading if your cornice overhangs a sidewalk.

## Materials and techniques

- **Mortar:** matched to the brick, not to a bag. On older soft brick, a softer mix — often **Type N** or a lime-rich mortar — protects the units; harder **Type S** belongs on sound modern brick and below-grade work. [Type N vs Type S mortar](/blog/type-n-vs-type-s-mortar-chicago) explains the choice.
- **Steel:** galvanized or coated replacement lintels and beams, sized by an engineer where the span or load calls for it.
- **Flashing:** through-wall flashing with end dams and weep holes over every replaced lintel.
- **Brick:** salvaged or matched units for the front; common brick for the rear where it matches.
- **Sealants:** a compatible joint sealant at the storefront perimeter and window frames — never smeared over mortar joints.
- **Sealers:** only breathable sealers, and only where a specific problem calls for one.

## What drives the cost

No responsible contractor can price a mixed-use façade from the sidewalk, but the cost drivers are consistent:

- **Sidewalk protection and access** — canopy, scaffold or lift, and the permit for the public way. On a corridor building this is frequently the largest single line.
- **Storefront beam scope** — clean and protect in place versus full replacement with shoring.
- **Quantity of repointing** and of brick replacement on the upper floors, measured in square feet and units.
- **Parapet and cornice** condition — repoint, reset coping, or rebuild.
- **Phasing** — every extra phase needed to keep a tenant operating adds mobilization.
- **Rear elevation and porch interface.**
- **Mortar and brick matching** on a visible front elevation.

Ask every bidder to quote the same quantities so the numbers can be compared — [why commercial masonry bids vary](/blog/why-commercial-masonry-bids-vary) explains the usual gaps.

### Who pays for it

On a mixed-use building there may be three parties with an interest: the owner, a commercial tenant on a lease that may allocate some exterior costs, and residential tenants who almost never carry them. The lease decides. Read [landlord vs tenant: who pays for masonry on a commercial lease](/blog/landlord-vs-tenant-who-pays-masonry-commercial-lease) before the work is priced, not after.

## DIY versus hiring a professional

A small owner can and should do the annual walk-around. What they should not do is chase the symptoms: caulk over a crack above the storefront beam, patch spalled brick with cement, or smear sealant into open joints. Each of those traps water against the steel or the brick and makes the eventual repair larger. Beam work in particular involves shoring loaded masonry over a public sidewalk — that is professional work with insurance behind it.

## The Chicagoland context

Chicago's **freeze-thaw cycles**, road salt from the busy street out front, and the age of the corridor stock combine to make these some of the hardest-working masonry walls in the region. The front faces a salted arterial; the rear faces an alley where snow is piled against the wall all winter. Many of these buildings have also had decades of sign changes, each leaving new holes in the sign band. And because the ground floor has often been remodelled several times, the original storefront beam is frequently hidden behind newer cladding — which is why it is so often found late.

## Maintenance and prevention

- **Annually:** walk all four elevations and get on the roof. Photograph the coping, cornice, storefront beam line and alley wall.
- **After every sign change:** make sure old anchor and conduit holes in the sign band are properly patched, not left open.
- **Every winter:** keep snow piles off the rear wall and clear the scupper or roof drain.
- **Every few years:** a closer assessment by a mason, with a written condition report and priorities. A [multi-year masonry program](/blog/multi-year-masonry-program-associations-portfolios-il) spreads the cost predictably.
