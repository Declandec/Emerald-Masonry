## The Stone on Your House Probably Is Not Stone

If your home was built or remodelled in Chicagoland between roughly the mid-1990s and today, and it has a stone wainscot across the front, stone-wrapped porch columns, or a stone chimney chase, there is a strong chance you are looking at **manufactured stone veneer** — also sold as cultured stone, adhered stone, or cast stone veneer.

It is not masonry in any structural sense. It is a **thin cement casting, typically around an inch thick**, coloured with iron oxide pigments and moulded from rubber forms taken off real rock. It is glued to your house.

And that is a perfectly legitimate product — when it is installed as a system. The problem is that across an enormous amount of Chicagoland housing stock, it was not.

## How the Assembly Is Supposed to Work

Understanding the failures requires understanding the layers, because every failure is a layer that was skipped.

From the framing outward, a correct adhered stone veneer wall is:

1. **Sheathing** over the framing.
2. **A drainage plane** — a water-resistive barrier. Best practice for adhered veneer is **two layers**, because the wet scratch coat bonds to and effectively consumes the outer layer, leaving the inner one to do the actual work of shedding water.
3. **Metal lath**, fastened through the sheathing into framing.
4. **A scratch coat** of mortar pressed into the lath, then scored.
5. **A setting bed** of mortar and the **stone units** themselves.
6. **Mortar joints** between the units, or a dry-stack appearance with no joints at all.
7. **A weep screed** at the bottom, terminating the assembly and giving trapped water an exit.
8. **Flashing** at every horizontal termination, penetration, window head, deck ledger and roof-to-wall junction.

The critical concept: **this wall is expected to let some water through.** Rain gets past the stone and past the mortar joints — that is normal and unavoidable. The assembly survives because the water hits the drainage plane, runs down, and exits at the weep screed.

**Remove the exit and you have built a sealed pocket against wood.**

## The Six Failures We Actually Find

### 1. No weep screed, or stone run to grade

The most common and the most damaging. When the veneer runs straight down into the soil, mulch, a patio slab or a driveway, water that gets behind it cannot drain and cannot dry. It wicks up from the soil as well. The sheathing behind the bottom of that wall stays wet essentially year-round.

Guidance calls for a clearance above finished grade and above paved surfaces, with your local code governing the exact dimension. Stone taken to the dirt is a defect you can identify from the sidewalk.

### 2. A single layer of building paper, or none

With only one layer, the scratch coat bonds directly to the barrier and destroys its ability to drain. Water then has an unbroken path from the back of the stone to the sheathing.

### 3. Missing or reversed flashing

Every place the veneer stops or is interrupted — window heads and sills, door heads, deck ledgers, roof-to-wall junctions, where the wainscot meets siding above — needs flashing that directs water out over the face of the stone rather than behind it. Missing kickout flashing where a roof edge meets a stone wall is a classic, and it funnels an entire roof valley's runoff straight into the wall.

### 4. Penetrations sealed with caulk instead of flashed

Hose bibs, lights, vents, electrical, railings, address plaques. Caulk is a maintenance material with a limited life, not a substitute for flashing. Every one of these is a hole through the drainage plane.

### 5. Improper mortar and joint work

Joints that were not filled completely, or a dry-stack look installed with no thought given to how water leaves. Mortar too hard or too weak. Stone set on a wall that was not properly scratch-coated, so the bond depends on the mortar alone.

### 6. Freeze-thaw doing what it does here

Once water is trapped behind the stone, Chicagoland's freeze-thaw cycling takes over. Water expands roughly nine percent as it freezes, dozens of times each winter, and it methodically breaks the bond between stone, setting bed, scratch coat and lath. Simultaneously the lath and its fasteners corrode.

## Warning Signs — and Why the Stone Is the Last One

This is the part homeowners most need to hear: **the stone falling off is the end of the process, not the beginning.**

### What you can see outside

- Individual stones **loose, drummy when tapped, or fallen off**
- **Cracked or missing mortar joints**, particularly at corners and terminations
- **Efflorescence** — white powdery bloom — on the stone or at the base of the wall
- **Dark staining** or persistent damp patches that never dry
- Stone **running into soil, mulch, a slab or a deck** with no visible termination
- **No weep screed** visible at the bottom of the wall
- **Rust staining** bleeding through the stone or mortar, meaning lath or fasteners are corroding
- **Caulk instead of flashing** at penetrations and terminations
- A **bulge or wave** in an otherwise flat stone wall

### What you may notice inside

- Musty smell at the base of an exterior wall
- Staining, soft drywall or peeling paint at the bottom of walls behind the stone
- Soft or discoloured trim, especially near door thresholds and under windows
- Flooring cupping along an exterior wall

### The test that tells you most

**Tap along the wall with your knuckles.** A sound adhered assembly is solid and dull. Hollow, drummy areas mean the stone has debonded from what is behind it — and that is where the water is.

## Risks of Waiting

This is not a cosmetic cladding problem, and that is why it deserves urgency that its appearance does not suggest.

The material behind the stone is **wood sheathing and framing**. Held wet for years, that decays. Repairs that start as "reset some loose stone" turn into sheathing replacement, and sheathing replacement occasionally turns into framing repair, which brings in other trades and a considerably larger number.

There is also a transaction problem. Stone veneer defects are well known to home inspectors, and a wall with no weep screed and stone into the dirt gets flagged. Discovering this during a sale, with an inspection report in hand and a closing date, is the most expensive possible moment to find out.

## How a Correct Repair Is Done

1. **Inspect the whole assembly**, not the stone. Terminations, clearances, flashing at every penetration, whether a weep screed exists, and where water is entering.
2. **Tap-test the full elevation** and map the debonded areas.
3. **Open a test area.** This is the step that separates a diagnosis from a guess — remove stone in a representative spot and look at the lath, the scratch coat, the barrier and the sheathing behind it.
4. **Establish the true extent.** Damage behind the wall is almost always larger than the visibly failed area.
5. **Remove the veneer** back to sound material, protecting adjacent finishes.
6. **Repair the substrate.** Replace decayed sheathing and, where needed, bring in framing repair. This is the part that must not be skipped, and the part a cheap bid omits.
7. **Rebuild the drainage plane** with two layers of water-resistive barrier, correctly lapped shingle-fashion.
8. **Install flashing** at every termination and penetration, including kickouts where a roof meets the wall.
9. **Install a weep screed** and establish proper clearance above grade and above paved surfaces.
10. **Lath, fasten and scratch coat** correctly, into framing rather than sheathing alone.
11. **Reset the stone** — salvaged where sound, matched where not — with full setting beds and properly filled joints.
12. **Correct the water sources** — gutters, downspouts, grade, irrigation — so the rebuilt wall is not put back into the conditions that killed the first one.

## Materials, and the Real-Masonry Question

Repairs use standard masonry materials — Type N or Type S mortar as conditions warrant, corrosion-resistant lath and fasteners, and a compatible barrier. **Matching existing stone is the practical limitation.** Manufactured stone is sold in named profiles and colour blends, many of which are discontinued after a decade. Where a match is unavailable, blending salvaged units into the most visible areas and using new material at the least visible is what keeps a repair from reading as a patch.

Which raises the question owners often ask: **should this be replaced with real masonry?**

| | Manufactured stone veneer | Full brick or stone veneer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Assembly** | Adhered to lath and scratch coat | Self-supporting wythe with an air cavity |
| **Weight** | Light; no ledge needed | Heavy; needs a foundation ledge or shelf angle |
| **Drainage** | Drainage plane and weep screed | Open cavity, weep holes and flashing |
| **Failure mode** | Trapped water rots the substrate | Failure is visible in the masonry itself |
| **Repairability** | Match availability is the limit | Units replaceable; matching usually achievable |
| **When it makes sense** | Wainscots, column wraps, chases | Where structure can carry it and longevity matters |

Converting to real masonry requires structural support the house may not have, so it is a case-by-case call — but on a low wainscot with an existing foundation ledge it is sometimes both feasible and worth it. ## Why This Is Concentrated in Chicagoland Subdivisions

Three things line up here.

**Timing.** Manufactured stone veneer became a standard builder detail during exactly the period when the collar counties built out hardest — the late 1990s through the 2000s across Will, DuPage, Kane and outer Cook. A very large number of homes got the same wainscot-and-column-wrap treatment in the same few years, installed by the same subcontractors under the same schedule pressure.

**Age.** Those installations are now fifteen to thirty years old, which is precisely when trapped-moisture damage becomes visible.

**Climate.** Chicagoland's freeze-thaw cycling is unusually punishing for an assembly that depends on drying. A trapped-water detail that might limp along in a mild, dry climate gets taken apart here.

The typical locations on these homes are consistent: the **front wainscot**, **porch and entry column wraps**, the **chimney chase**, and occasionally a full gable end.

## Maintenance and Prevention

- **Keep grade, mulch and landscaping down** off the bottom of the veneer, and never bury the weep screed.
- **Keep irrigation off the wall.** Sprinklers hitting stone veneer several mornings a week keep the assembly permanently wet.
- **Manage roof water.** Gutters, downspout extensions and kickout flashing at roof-to-wall junctions.
- **Maintain the sealant** at penetrations — it is a wear item with a limited life, and it is the only defence at details that were never flashed.
- **Tap-test annually** and note any change in hollow areas.
- **Do not paint or seal the face** in an attempt to waterproof it. That traps moisture inside the assembly.
- **Get a real look before buying.** If you are purchasing a home with stone veneer, have the terminations and clearances examined specifically.

## The Bottom Line

Manufactured stone veneer is a cladding pretending to be masonry, and it survives on details you cannot see: two layers of barrier, a weep screed, correct clearance above grade, and flashing at every interruption. Where those exist, it performs. Where they do not, water goes in, cannot come out, and quietly destroys the wall behind it while the stone still looks fine.

If your stone runs into the dirt, if you cannot find a weep screed, or if the wall sounds hollow when you tap it, the question is not whether the stone needs attention. It is what is behind it — and that is a question worth answering before it answers itself.
