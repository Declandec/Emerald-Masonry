# Masonry or Roofing First? Sequencing Parapets, Coping and Membrane

**On a flat-roofed commercial or multi-family building, masonry work at the parapet should generally be finished before the new roof membrane is terminated.** The coping, the through-wall flashing at the top of the wall and the reglet that receives counterflashing are all masonry-side work that the roofer builds against — so doing the wall first means the membrane is terminated into a finished detail rather than cut back into afterwards. The exception is a roof that is actively failing, where a temporary termination goes in first and the permanent detail follows. Every property manager with a flat-roofed building has lived through this. The roof gets replaced. A year or two later the masonry gets repointed. And the tenant on the top floor is still calling about the same stain in the same ceiling tile.

Both contractors did what they were paid for. **The leak was never in either scope.**

## The six inches nobody owns

At the top of a parapet, five details stack on top of each other within about a foot and a half of wall, and they belong to two different trades:

| Detail | Normally whose work | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Roof membrane and cant strip | Roofer | Sheds water to the drains |
| Base flashing (membrane turned up the parapet) | Roofer | Carries the waterproofing up the wall |
| Reglet or through-wall flashing receiver | **Mason** | Gives the metal something to tuck into and stops water in the wall |
| Metal counterflashing | **Either — name it** | Caps the base flashing so water cannot get behind it |
| Coping and its joints | **Mason** | Caps the top of the wall |

Look at that middle row. **The counterflashing is the single most commonly unassigned element on a combined roof-and-masonry project**, and it is the piece that makes the whole assembly work. A roofer who assumes the mason is setting it, and a mason who assumes the roofer is, will both finish their contract and leave a gap.

The second problem is the coping. A parapet is a wall exposed on both faces with a horizontal top — the worst condition masonry can be asked to occupy. Water enters through open coping joints, runs down inside the parapet core where nothing dries, and emerges on the inside face of the wall well below and often laterally away from where it got in. Our guide to [coping and roofline masonry leaks](/blog/coping-roofline-masonry-leaks) covers the mechanism, and [parapet wall failure signs](/blog/parapet-wall-failure-signs-commercial-buildings) covers how to read it from the roof.

## Why masonry usually goes first

Four reasons, in order of how much money they save.

**1. The roofer needs a finished receiver.** A reglet cut into sound mortar, or a properly turned and lapped through-wall flashing, is what the base flashing and counterflashing terminate into. Cutting a reglet into mortar you are about to grind out is wasted work; cutting it into fresh mortar after the roof is on means disturbing the new termination.

**2. Masonry work is dirty and heavy.** Grinding joints, cutting brick and rebuilding coping generates dust, debris and point loads. Doing it over a brand-new membrane risks puncture, staining and warranty problems. Doing it first means the roofer covers the evidence.

**3. Roof access for masonry is easier before the new roof exists.** Staging feet, material hoisting and equipment on an old roof that is coming off anyway is a much smaller conversation than the same activity on a new warranted assembly.

**4. Coping replacement wants the top of the wall open.** If the coping is original, if its bed joints are failing, or if it has been caulked twice already, the cheapest time to replace it is when the parapet is already accessible and the roof edge is already open. Deferring it means paying for the same access twice.

## When the roof goes first anyway

There is one good reason and it overrides everything above: **the roof is leaking now.** You do not hold a failing roof hostage to a masonry schedule or a funding cycle.

When that is the situation, the right sequence is:

1. Roof goes on, with the base flashing terminated in a **deliberately temporary** detail — surface-mounted counterflashing, properly fastened and sealed, documented as temporary in the roofing scope.
2. The masonry scope, written knowing that condition exists, includes cutting the permanent reglet or restoring the through-wall detail, removing the temporary termination, and setting permanent counterflashing.
3. The roofer returns to tie in, or the mason's scope includes the metal — named explicitly in whichever contract carries it.
4. Both trades and the manufacturer know the plan in advance, so nothing about the second visit is a surprise to a warranty.

The failure mode here is not the sequence. It is a temporary detail that nobody recorded as temporary, which then quietly becomes permanent for fifteen years.

## Put the boundary in the contracts, not in the meeting

Coordination that lives in a conversation does not survive the project. It has to be in the documents, and the cleanest way to do it is to write the interface from both sides.

**In the masonry scope, state explicitly:**

- Coping removal, salvage or replacement, and joint treatment
- Through-wall flashing at the parapet — restored, replaced, or noted as absent and out of scope
- Reglet cutting: where, at what height, in what joint
- Whether counterflashing metal is included
- Repointing extent on the **roof-side face** of the parapet, not just the street face
- The condition the wall will be left in for the roofer, and the date
- Any temporary protection of the existing roof during masonry work

**In the roofing scope, state explicitly:**

- Base flashing height and termination type
- Whether the roofer is furnishing and setting counterflashing
- Cant strip and membrane turn-up details at the parapet
- What condition the roofer requires the wall in, and when
- Notice requirements and manufacturer approvals for later masonry work

## Sealant is not a sequencing strategy

A quantity of Chicagoland parapets are held together, functionally, by a bead of urethane over a coping joint applied at some point in the last decade. It works for a while. It is not a detail.

Coping joints move — thermally, and because the wall beneath them moves. A sealant joint at the top of a parapet needs correct geometry, backer rod, sound substrate on both sides and a realistic service life, and even then it is a maintenance item rather than a permanent solution. [Masonry sealants — silicone, urethane and hybrid](/blog/masonry-sealants-silicone-urethane-hybrid-caulk) covers the material choice. What matters for sequencing is simpler: **if the plan for the top of the wall is "we'll caulk it," nobody has actually assigned the detail.**

## The warranty trap

This one catches owners more than contractors.

Membrane warranties are commonly conditioned on the termination not being disturbed and on the manufacturer being notified before other trades work on the roof. If masonry work follows a warranted roof — cutting a reglet, rebuilding coping, setting staging feet on the membrane — an owner can compromise a twenty-year warranty in an afternoon without anyone intending to.

Equally, a masonry warranty is worth less than it looks if the water reaching the repointed wall is arriving from a roof detail the mason had no control over. [What a masonry warranty should cover](/blog/what-a-masonry-warranty-should-cover) sets out what to ask for. The short version for a combined project: **get both warranties in front of you before either trade starts, and make sure neither one excludes the interface.**

## The coordination that actually works

It is less than people expect. Four things:

1. **One joint site walk before pricing.** Mason and roofer on the roof together, with the owner or manager, agreeing out loud what exists and who is doing which piece. An hour here prevents most of what follows.
2. **A sequence with dates**, not with intentions — masonry complete by a date, wall left in a defined condition, roofer mobilises after.
3. **One handover walk** at the boundary point, photographed.
4. **One owner's representative** who owns the interface. Not the mason, not the roofer — whoever is paying.

What does not help is running both trades simultaneously on the same parapet. Shared access on a roof edge produces conflict, not efficiency. [Masonry sequencing with other trades](/blog/masonry-sequencing-with-other-trades) covers the broader principle across roofing, windows, sealant and painting.

One genuine efficiency is worth arranging deliberately: **shared access.** If staging or a lift is needed for both scopes, putting it in one contract with erection, inspection and insurance clearly assigned is real money saved on a tall building.

## What it costs to get the order wrong

- A reglet cut into mortar that is ground out six months later: pay twice
- Coping deferred until after the roof: pay for roof-edge access twice
- Counterflashing unassigned: leak survives both projects, and diagnosing it later costs more than either detail
- Masonry over a new membrane: puncture risk, staining, and a warranty conversation
- Temporary termination never made permanent: a slow failure nobody is looking for

None of these are large numbers individually. Together, on a mid-size building, they comfortably exceed what a coordinated sequence would have cost.
