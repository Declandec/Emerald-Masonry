# Why Brick Cracks Around Windows and Doors

If your brick is cracking, look at where the cracks are — and there's a good chance they're clustered around your windows and doors. That's not a coincidence. Brick cracks around windows and doors because those openings are the weak points in a masonry wall: the brickwork can't run continuously across a window or door, so the load above has to be carried across the gap, steel lintels sit and rust at those openings, sills collect water there, and different materials meet and move against each other there. The shape and location of the crack is telling you exactly what's wrong. **Emerald Masonry LLC** diagnoses and repairs cracked brick around windows and doors throughout Chicago and the Chicagoland suburbs, and this guide walks through how to read what your cracks mean. The single most useful thing to understand is this: a crack is a symptom, not the disease. Two cracks that look similar can have completely different causes and completely different levels of seriousness. Filling the crack without understanding why it formed is the most common mistake homeowners make — and it's why so many "repaired" cracks come right back. Below, we'll break down why openings crack first, the real causes behind cracked brick around windows and doors, how to tell a cosmetic crack from a structural one, and how a professional actually fixes each type.

## Why Openings Are the Weak Point in a Brick Wall

A brick wall is strong in a very specific way: it carries weight straight down, brick on brick, joint on joint. Interrupt that continuous coursing with a window or a door and you've created a problem the wall has to solve. The bricks above the opening still weigh something — often a lot — but there's nothing directly beneath them for several feet. Something has to bridge that gap and carry the load out to the brick on either side. That something is the **lintel**: a steel angle iron (in most twentieth-century homes) or a stone or masonry header spanning the top of the opening.

Because all of that load funnels down to the two bottom corners of the opening, those corners are stress concentration points. Add in that openings are where builders install steel that can rust, where horizontal sills sit and hold water, and where window frames, caulk, flashing, and brick all meet and expand and contract at different rates, and you have the perfect storm. A wall fails where it's weakest and most stressed — and around windows and doors, it's both.

That's why, when we're called out for **brick repair** across Chicagoland, cracked brick around windows and doors is one of the most common things we find. The good news is that the crack usually tells us the cause before we've even touched it.

## The Real Causes of Cracked Brick at Openings

### Rusting steel lintels and rust jacking

This is the number one cause we see. Most homes built from the 1920s through the 1980s use bare or lightly painted steel angle-iron lintels above windows, doors, and garage openings. Steel and water don't mix. Once water gets behind the brick and sits on the steel — which it will, over decades — the steel corrodes. And here's the mechanism that does the damage: **rust occupies far more volume than the steel it came from.** As the lintel rusts, it swells, and that expansion lifts and cracks the brick sitting on top of it. This is called **rust jacking**.

The signs are unmistakable once you know them: a **horizontal crack** in the mortar joint right above a window or garage door, **orange-brown rust stains** streaking down the brick below, sometimes a slight sag or step in the brick course over the opening, and gaps opening up at the ends of the lintel. Caught early, a lightly rusted but still-sound lintel can sometimes be cleaned, primed, and resealed. Once the steel is scaling and swelling, it has lost strength and needs replacement — which is exactly the kind of work our [lintel repair service](/services/lintel-repair) handles. We cover the rust-stain warning signs in detail in [our guide to lintel rust stains and orange streaks on brick](/blog/lintel-rust-stains-orange-streaks-brick).

### Failed or undersized lintels

Not every lintel problem is rust. Sometimes a lintel was undersized for the span from the start, or has bent under years of load, or was never properly bearing on the brick at its ends. A wide opening — a picture window, a double or triple window, a garage door — puts a lot of brick on a long lintel, and if that steel deflects, the brick above cracks. The crack pattern is similar to rust jacking (horizontal, over the opening), but without the heavy rust staining. Either way, a lintel that has lost its load-carrying ability needs to be replaced and the brick above reset.

### Foundation settlement and movement

This is the cause homeowners worry about most, and it's the one where reading the crack matters. When part of a foundation settles or shifts relative to another part, the wall above has to accommodate that movement — and it cracks where it's weakest, which is at the corners of openings. The signature is a **diagonal step crack**: a crack that climbs in a staircase pattern along the mortar joints, usually starting at the top or bottom corner of a window or door. Doors and windows that suddenly stick, cracks that are visibly wider at one end, and cracks that are clearly getting bigger over months are all signs of **active** settlement.

Here's the honest part: an old, stable hairline step crack that hasn't moved in years is common and often not urgent. A widening step crack is different — it can indicate ongoing structural movement, and no amount of tuckpointing will fix the underlying cause. When we see signs of active settlement, we say so, and we'll recommend bringing in a structural engineer to address the foundation before we do the masonry repair. We go deeper on telling these apart in [hairline versus structural cracks in brick](/blog/hairline-vs-structural-cracks-brick), and on wall movement in [why brick walls bow and bulge](/blog/why-brick-walls-bow-and-bulge).

### Thermal and moisture movement — and missing expansion joints

Brick and mortar are not static. Brick expands slightly as it absorbs moisture over its lifetime and grows and shrinks with temperature; concrete and mortar tend to shrink as they cure and age. These are dissimilar materials moving in different directions, and over a long wall that stress has to go somewhere. Modern masonry uses **expansion joints** (soft, compressible movement joints) to give the wall room to move without cracking. Older buildings — and some poorly detailed newer ones — lack enough of them. When there's nowhere for the movement to be absorbed, the stress relieves itself as cracks, and it does so at the natural break points: the openings. If your cracks are vertical, fairly straight, and located near the ends of long wall runs or beside openings, thermal and moisture movement with missing or failed expansion joints is a likely culprit. We explain the building science in [why brick buildings need masonry expansion joints](/blog/masonry-expansion-joints-why-brick-buildings-need-them).

### Failed window sills

Look below the opening, not just above it. A **limestone or masonry sill** is supposed to shed water away from the wall with its slope and its drip edge. When a sill spalls, cracks, loses its drip edge, or pitches backward toward the house, it starts funneling water into the wall and around the window instead of away from it. That trapped water then drives cracking and spalling in the brick directly below the opening, plus interior water stains and rotted window frames. Cracks concentrated *under* a window — rather than over it — often point back to a failed sill. Our [limestone and sill repair service](/services/limestone-sill-repair) addresses this, frequently with a Dutchman repair that saves the original stone.

### Caulk and sealant failure at the window perimeter

The joint between the window frame and the brick is sealed with caulk or sealant, and caulk doesn't last forever. When it dries out, shrinks, and cracks, water gets in around the perimeter of the opening — behind the brick, onto the lintel, into the wall. This doesn't crack brick by itself, but it accelerates every other cause on this list by feeding them water. Refreshing failed perimeter sealant is part of a complete repair and is handled by our [caulking and joint sealant service](/services/caulking).

### Ordinary mortar deterioration concentrating at openings

Finally, sometimes the "crack" is really just the mortar joints failing — and, again, they fail first at the stressed, weather-exposed edges of openings. Decades of Chicago freeze-thaw turn sound mortar sandy and crumbly; joints recede, hairline cracks open along the bond line, and the wall stops keeping water out. This is the bread-and-butter case for **tuckpointing** (also called repointing): grinding out the failed mortar to a sound depth and refilling with new, properly matched mortar. If you're not sure whether your mortar is at this stage, [the screwdriver test](/blog/how-to-tell-if-mortar-is-failing-screwdriver-test) is a simple ground-level check.

## Cosmetic vs. Structural: How to Read the Crack

You don't need to be a mason to make a first read on a crack — you need to look at its **pattern, location, width, and whether it's changing.** Here's a quick diagnostic guide to the cracks we most often see around windows and doors in Chicagoland homes.

| Crack pattern & location | Most likely cause | Cosmetic or structural? | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hairline crack in a single mortar joint | Normal mortar aging / minor movement | Cosmetic | Monitor; repoint when convenient |
| Horizontal crack in the joint just above a window/garage, with orange rust stains | Rusting steel lintel (rust jacking) | Functional — worsens if ignored | Lintel repair or replacement, then reset brick |
| Horizontal crack over a wide opening, no rust | Undersized or deflecting lintel | Functional/structural | Professional lintel assessment |
| Fine diagonal step crack, not changing | Old, settled movement | Usually cosmetic | Monitor; repoint |
| Widening diagonal step crack from a corner; doors/windows sticking | Active foundation settlement | Structural | Prompt evaluation; structural engineer if active |
| Vertical straight cracks near opening ends / long wall runs | Thermal/moisture movement, missing expansion joints | Functional | Repair/add movement joints, repoint |
| Cracking and spalling below a window | Failed sill funneling water in | Functional | Sill repair, then brick repair below |

Two rules of thumb make this simpler. **Direction:** vertical and stepped cracks are usually about movement and moisture; horizontal cracks over an opening are usually about the lintel. **Change over time:** the most important question isn't how a crack looks today but whether it's *growing.* Mark the ends of a crack with a pencil and a date, and check it in a few months. A crack that isn't moving is far less urgent than one that is — regardless of how alarming it looks.

## How a Professional Diagnoses and Fixes It

A real repair starts with finding the cause, not the crack. When we assess cracked brick around an opening, we trace the water and the load: Is the lintel rusting? Is the sill shedding water or holding it? Is the mortar sound when we probe it? Is the crack a stable hairline or a widening step crack that suggests movement? Is there an expansion joint anywhere on this wall? The answers point to the fix:

1. **Rusting or failed lintel** → temporarily support the brick above, remove the affected course, replace the lintel with new galvanized/primed steel with proper end bearing, address flashing and weeps, and reset the brick in matched mortar. (See [lintel repair](/services/lintel-repair).)
2. **Failed mortar joints** → grind out and repoint to a sound depth with color- and strength-matched mortar. (See [tuckpointing](/services/tuckpointing).)
3. **Individually failed brick units** (spalled, cracked through) → cut out and replace with matched brick, tooled to match the existing joints. (See [brick replacement](/services/brick-replacement).)
4. **Failed sill** → Dutchman repair or replacement, recut the drip edge, and soft-seal the sill ends. (See [limestone and sill repair](/services/limestone-sill-repair).)
5. **Thermal/movement cracking** → repair or introduce expansion/movement joints so the wall can move without cracking, then repoint.
6. **Perimeter sealant failure** → replace failed caulk with quality sealant. (See [caulking](/services/caulking).)
7. **Active structural settlement** → coordinate with a structural engineer to stabilize the foundation first, then do the masonry repair once movement has stopped.

## Why This Isn't a Caulk-Gun DIY Job

It's tempting to run a bead of caulk or smear some mortar into a crack and call it done. The problem is that the crack is the *symptom.* If a rusting lintel is jacking the brick, sealing the crack does nothing to stop the steel from expanding — it keeps rusting behind your patch until the brick fails. If a sill is funneling water into the wall, filling the crack below it just traps more water. And if the crack is from active settlement, cosmetic fill will simply crack again in the same place. Worse, the wrong material — a hard, brittle patch on a soft old wall, or a film-forming sealer over damp brick — can trap moisture and accelerate spalling. Diagnosing the cause, matching mortar to the existing wall, and repairing lintels and sills correctly is specialized work. That's what a masonry contractor does.

## The Chicagoland Angle: Why Our Brick Cracks at Openings

Chicago and its suburbs are unusually hard on masonry at openings, and it comes down to three local realities. First, **freeze-thaw:** our winters cycle water in the wall through freezing and thawing over and over, prying at every joint and crack and rusting every unprotected lintel. Second, **clay soils:** much of Chicagoland sits on clay that swells when wet and shrinks when dry, nudging foundations and driving the differential movement that produces step cracks at openings. Third, the **housing stock:** our brick bungalows, two-flats, greystones, and mid-century ranches were built with bare steel lintels and, in the oldest homes, soft lime mortar — both of which concentrate wear right where the wall is weakest. Put those together and cracked brick around windows and doors is simply one of the most common masonry problems across Cook, DuPage, Will, and the collar counties. It's also one of the most fixable, when the cause is correctly identified. ## Maintenance and Prevention

You can't stop a wall from having openings, but you can keep those openings from becoming problems. Keep gutters and downspouts working and directed away from the house so water isn't sheeting down the wall onto lintels and sills. Refresh window-perimeter caulk before it fails. Keep sills clear so their drip edges can shed water, and address a spalling sill before it soaks the wall below. Repoint deteriorating mortar joints while they're still shallow, rather than waiting until water reaches the lintel and the brick. And walk your walls once a year — mark any crack with a date and watch whether it moves. Small, early masonry repairs are dramatically cheaper and less disruptive than the rebuilds that follow years of ignored water.
