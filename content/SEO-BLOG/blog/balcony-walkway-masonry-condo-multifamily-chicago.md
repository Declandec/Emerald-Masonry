Ask a condo board what masonry they are worried about and they will point at the façade — the brick everyone can see from the parking lot.

Ask which masonry could actually injure somebody, and the honest answer is different: the **balcony slab edge**, the **exterior walkway deck**, and the **brick or concrete a guardrail post is anchored into**. All three are load-bearing on people. All three deteriorate from the inside out. And all three are routinely absent from a reserve study until they show up as an emergency.

## The mechanism: steel inside concrete

The building science here is the same one that destroys lintels, and it is worth understanding because it explains why the failure is invisible until it is not.

Reinforced concrete works because steel and concrete expand at similar rates and the alkaline concrete keeps the steel passive — chemically protected from corrosion.

Two things break that protection:

**Carbonation.** Over decades, atmospheric carbon dioxide reacts with the concrete and lowers its alkalinity. The steel loses its chemical protection.

**Chlorides.** De-icing salt, tracked and shovelled onto balconies and walkways every Illinois winter, penetrates the concrete and attacks the steel directly. This is the dominant mechanism on exterior decks in this climate.

Once the steel starts corroding, it converts to iron oxide, which occupies **several times the volume** of the original metal. The concrete around it cannot move, so the expansion pushes the surface off from inside. That is **spalling**, and by the time you see it, the reinforcement has already lost section.

Which is the crucial point for a board: **the damage is well advanced before anything is visible.** A slab that looks fine can be years into the process.

## Where multi-family buildings fail

### The balcony slab edge

The most exposed concrete on the building. Water runs to the edge, sits at the drip line, and freezes there. Reinforcement near an edge has the least cover protecting it. Spalling almost always starts at the edge and the underside, and it drops material — over an entry walk, a patio, or a parking stall.

### The underside and the soffit

Rust staining and efflorescence on the underside of a balcony is a diagnostic gift. It means water is moving through the slab and reaching steel. Residents rarely look up; a walkthrough should.

### Guardrail anchorage

The item that matters most and gets checked least.

A guardrail post embedded in concrete or bolted into masonry can look **completely sound** while the steel inside its pocket has corroded and lost its grip. Worse, the corroding post expands and cracks the masonry it sits in, which then admits more water, which accelerates the corrosion. It is a closed loop.

A guardrail is a life-safety element carrying a code-defined load. **Any movement under hand pressure is an immediate finding**, not a maintenance note.

### Exterior walkways and stair landings

On courtyard and garden-style buildings, the walkway *is* the only route to the unit. Deck coatings fail, water gets into the slab, and the deterioration runs the length of a bay. Because residents use it daily, this work carries the heaviest access and communication burden of anything on the property.

### The balcony door threshold

The most overlooked detail on the list. Water entering at the door sill and its flashing runs into the slab and the wall below, and it is invisible from the balcony. The characteristic symptom is a **leak reported by the unit below the balcony**, not by the unit with the balcony — which is why these get misdiagnosed as window or façade leaks for years. Our post on [through-wall flashing in masonry walls](/blog/through-wall-flashing-masonry-walls) covers the detail.

### Where the balcony meets the wall

A cold joint between two assemblies that move at different rates, sealed with a bead that has a service life measured in a handful of years. Failed sealant here delivers water directly into the junction.

## What to look for on a walkthrough

- **Rust staining** at slab edges, undersides, or around railing posts
- **Cracks running parallel to a slab edge** — often the first sign of reinforcement corrosion below
- **Hollow sound** when a slab surface is tapped, indicating delamination before spalling appears
- **Any exposed reinforcement** — urgent, without qualification
- **Guardrail posts that move**, or cracked masonry around a post base
- **Efflorescence** on undersides and soffits
- **Ponding water** on a deck, meaning the slope is gone
- **Failed or debonded deck coating**
- **Open sealant** at the wall junction and at door thresholds
- **Blocked or missing scuppers and drains**

Two things a board should record: **date every observation, and photograph from repeatable positions.** Change over time is far more informative than any single inspection, and it is what tells you whether something is stable or progressing.

## Chicagoland makes this worse than most markets

**Freeze-thaw cycling.** A Chicago winter does not freeze once and hold — it thaws and refreezes dozens of times a season. Every cycle is an expansion event inside wet concrete.

**De-icing salt, applied directly.** Unlike a façade, a balcony and a walkway get salt deliberately placed on them, all winter, every winter. Chloride attack on reinforcement is the single largest driver of the damage described here. Our post on [road salt and de-icer damage](/blog/road-salt-deicer-brick-damage-illinois) covers the wider effect.

**Shovelling.** Metal shovels and ice chippers damage deck coatings and surface concrete, opening the path.

**Building stock.** Chicagoland's garden-style courtyard buildings, 1960s and 70s mid-rises, and townhome associations were built with cover depths and detailing that were normal at the time and are now, fifty years on, at the point where this shows.

## How the repair actually works

1. **Assess and sound the concrete.** Delaminated areas are identified by tapping before anything is removed — visible spalling is always smaller than the affected area.
2. **Involve a structural engineer** where reinforcement is exposed, a slab is deflecting, or the extent is unclear. A straight contractor says this rather than quoting around it.
3. **Remove unsound concrete** back to solid material, and fully around any corroded reinforcement — undercutting behind the bar, not merely to its face.
4. **Clean and treat the reinforcement**, and supplement or replace bar where section loss requires it.
5. **Rebuild the section** with an appropriate repair mortar, formed at edges to restore the original profile and drip.
6. **Restore guardrail anchorage** properly — repairing the pocket or resetting the post, rather than injecting sealant around a loose base and calling it done.
7. **Repair the masonry** around anchorages and at the wall junction.
8. **Restore slope and drainage**, so the deck sheds rather than ponds.
9. **Reseal joints and thresholds** with backer rod and correctly tooled sealant.
10. **Reinstate the deck coating or waterproofing system**, once the substrate is sound.

## Materials, and why the wrong patch fails fast

Concrete repair on an exterior deck is genuinely material-sensitive, and this is where cheap work announces itself within a season or two.

**The repair mortar has to be compatible with the substrate.** A patch that is much stronger and stiffer than the surrounding concrete does not share load with it; it debonds at the perimeter and pops out. Matching the modulus and shrinkage behaviour of the parent material matters more than raw compressive strength.

**Bond depends entirely on preparation.** Sound, clean, roughened substrate, removal of every trace of unsound material, and full removal around and behind corroded reinforcement. A patch placed against a bar that is still rusting on its back face is a patch that will be re-done, because the corrosion continues underneath it.

**Edges must be formed, not feathered.** A feather-edged patch at a slab edge has no thickness at its most vulnerable point and fails first. Repairs are saw-cut to a square edge and formed to restore the original profile, including the drip.

**Coatings are a system, not a product.** A pedestrian traffic coating has a primer, a body coat, and a wear coat, applied to a substrate at the right moisture content and temperature. Rolling a coating over a damp or unsound deck is a way to hide a problem for one season.

**Sealant is not a structural repair.** Injecting sealant around a loose guardrail post base does not restore anchorage. It conceals the movement and delays the finding.

## The paper trail a board should keep

This work has a longer institutional memory requirement than most, because it recurs and because boards turn over.

Keep, in one file: dated photographs from repeatable positions of every balcony edge, underside, and railing base; the sounding survey and any engineer's report; the written scope and the marked-up elevations showing what was repaired and where; the coating system and product data used, with the date; and the sealant type and date at thresholds and wall junctions.

Five years on, when the next board asks whether the north stacks were done in the last cycle, that file answers it in a minute. Without it, the answer is another survey.

## Who pays, and what the declaration says

Balconies are frequently **limited common elements** — the association controls repair of the structure while the owner has exclusive use. But this varies genuinely between buildings, and the declaration governs, not general practice.

Read it before commissioning anything, and if the language is ambiguous, get an opinion before the work rather than after the invoice. Our guide to [HOA versus unit owner responsibility for masonry repair](/blog/hoa-vs-unit-owner-who-pays-masonry-repair) walks through the usual arrangements.

## Budgeting and phasing

Balcony and walkway repair belongs in a reserve study as a **recurring cyclical item**, not as a one-time event — because coatings, sealant and joints all have service lives well short of the structure's.

When phasing across budget years:

- **Life-safety first.** Exposed reinforcement and compromised guardrails do not defer. Nothing else on the property outranks them.
- **Group by access.** Staging is a large share of this cost. Doing two stacks on one mobilisation beats doing them in different years.
- **Fix water before appearance.** A recoated deck over a spalling slab is money spent twice.
