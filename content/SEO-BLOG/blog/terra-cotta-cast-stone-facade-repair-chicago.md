**Architectural terra cotta is hollow, glazed, kiln-fired clay cladding anchored to a building's structure with steel — not solid stone.** **Cast stone is a manufactured concrete unit cast to imitate carved stone.** Both fail the same fundamental way: water gets in behind the units, corrodes the steel anchors and shelf angles holding them, and the expanding rust cracks the pieces and eventually pushes them off the building. Chicago is, to an unusual degree, a terra cotta city. Between roughly 1880 and 1940 the material was everywhere: it was fireproof, lighter than stone, could be molded into any ornament a draftsman could draw, and could be produced locally. It clads storefronts, apartment buildings, theaters, churches, schools, banks and civic landmarks across the city and the older suburbs.

A century later, an enormous quantity of that material is reaching the end of its original anchoring system's life at the same time — and unlike a failing brick wall, a failing terra cotta cornice comes down onto a sidewalk.

## How These Facades Are Actually Built

This is the part that changes everything about the repair, and it is the part most building owners have never been told.

### Architectural terra cotta

Each piece is a **hollow shell** of fired clay with a glazed exterior face. Internal webs stiffen it. The unit is hung on the building's structure with **steel anchors, hooks and ties**, often bearing on **steel shelf angles**, and the cavity behind is partially filled with mortar. The glaze is the weather barrier; the clay body behind it is porous.

So a terra cotta facade is not a wall in the way a brick wall is a wall. It is a **cladding system** — thin decorative units, a void, steel anchors, and structure behind. When people say a terra cotta piece "fell off the building," that is literally what happened: it was hung, and the hanger failed.

### Cast stone

Cast stone is a **manufactured concrete masonry unit**, cast in molds with selected aggregates to imitate quarried limestone. It became popular in the early 20th century because it was cheaper and faster than carving stone and could be reproduced identically. Many pieces contain **internal reinforcing steel**.

Cast stone weathers like concrete, not like natural stone: it carbonates, its surface erodes, and where internal reinforcement corrodes it produces rust staining and spalling from within.

### Why this matters

| | Solid limestone | Cast stone | Architectural terra cotta |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Material** | Quarried natural stone | Molded concrete | Hollow fired clay, glazed |
| **Structure** | Solid unit, often bearing | Solid unit, often reinforced | Hollow shell, anchored |
| **Primary failure** | Delamination, erosion, joint failure | Surface erosion, rebar corrosion | Anchor corrosion, glaze crazing, spalling |
| **Falling hazard** | Moderate | Moderate | High — units are hung |
| **Typical repair** | Dutchman, patching, repointing | Patching, replication, repointing | Anchor replacement, patching, replication |

## Why Ornamental Facades Fail

**Water enters.** Through crazed glaze, cracked units, open mortar joints, failed sealant, missing or blocked **through-wall flashing**, and at horizontal surfaces — cornices, sills, belt courses, balustrades, coping — which hold water rather than shedding it.

**Steel corrodes.** The anchors, ties and shelf angles behind the units rust. Corroding steel expands to several times the volume of the original metal, and there is nowhere for that expansion to go except into the surrounding masonry. This is **rust jacking**, the same mechanism that lifts brick above a corroding **steel lintel**, operating behind ornamental cladding where you cannot see it.

**Units crack and displace.** The expansion cracks the terra cotta from behind. The crack lets in more water. The cycle accelerates.

**Freeze-thaw finishes the job.** Chicagoland delivers dozens of crossings of the freezing point per winter. Water trapped inside a hollow terra cotta unit or a porous cast stone piece expands about nine percent each time it freezes.

**Then a piece comes down.** Usually from the highest, most exposed, most ornamental part of the building — cornices and parapet ornament — onto the sidewalk.

## Warning Signs on an Ornamental Facade

### On the units themselves
- **Crazing** — the fine hairline network in the glaze, marking the start of the water path.
- **Glaze spalling** — the glazed face flaking off, exposing the porous clay body.
- **Cracked units**, particularly cracks running in a consistent direction across several pieces.
- **Displaced, tilted or projecting units** — a piece no longer flush with its neighbors is a piece whose anchor has failed.
- **Rust staining** bleeding from joints or from within a unit.
- **Missing pieces**, or fresh voids where ornament used to be.

### On the assembly
- **Open, cracked or missing mortar joints** and failed sealant between units.
- **Cracked or displaced cornice, cap and coping** elements.
- **Efflorescence** on or below the ornamental work.
- **Interior water staining** on top floors near exterior walls.
- **Debris** — fragments on the sidewalk, in gutters, on setbacks or on the roof.
- **Existing netting or scaffolding** left up long-term, which is a containment measure, not a repair.

## The Chicago Facade Ordinance Context

Chicago requires periodic exterior wall inspection and reporting for qualifying buildings, with critical examinations on a defined cycle and immediate action where unsafe conditions are found. Ornamental terra cotta, cornices and parapets are among the most frequent sources of unsafe findings, precisely because they are the highest, most exposed and most heavily anchored elements.

For owners and property managers the practical implication is straightforward: **treat the facade report's findings as a repair schedule.** Deferring a flagged cornice condition does not pause the deterioration; it just moves the work into an emergency, at emergency pricing, potentially with a sidewalk closure attached.

## How Repair Actually Works

1. **Close-up assessment.** Ornamental facades cannot be evaluated from the ground. Real assessment means hands-on access — lift, swing stage or scaffold — sounding units to find hollow and delaminated areas, and probing the anchor condition.
2. **Stabilize immediate hazards.** Loose material is removed or secured, and protection is installed below before anything else proceeds.
3. **Identify and stop the water path.** Joints, sealant, flashing, coping and roof drainage. Repairing units while leaving the water path open guarantees a repeat.
4. **Address the steel.** Corroded anchors, ties and shelf angles are exposed, cleaned or replaced with **stainless** components. This is the actual fix; everything visible depends on it.
5. **Repair the units.** Patching with matched cementitious repair mortars for surface loss; **dutchman inserts** where a section can be cut out and replaced; pinning and stabilizing cracked units.
6. **Replicate where necessary.** Units beyond saving are replaced in new terra cotta, cast stone or **GFRC**, matched for profile, color, texture and sheen. This is the most expensive route and is reserved for pieces that cannot be stabilized.
7. **Repoint and reseal.** Matched mortar at joints, correct sealant and backer rod at movement joints.
8. **Gentle cleaning only.** No sandblasting, no high-pressure washing — both destroy glaze and erode cast stone surfaces permanently.
9. **Document.** Photographs and a written record for the owner's files and the next facade report.

- **Access.** Frequently the single largest line. Scaffolding, swing stage or lift on an occupied city building, plus sidewalk protection and permits.

## Maintenance That Prevents Emergencies

- **Keep the water out at the top.** Coping, cornice flashing, roof drainage and parapet condition protect everything below them.
- **Maintain joints and sealant** on a cycle rather than after failure.
- **Inspect after hard freezes and major storms**, and look at what is landing on the sidewalk and in the gutters.
- **Sound the units periodically** — hollow-sounding terra cotta indicates delamination before anything is visible.
- **Never blast or pressure wash** glazed or cast surfaces.
- **Budget from facade reports** rather than reacting to findings.
- **Address rust staining immediately** — it is the earliest external evidence that the steel behind the facade is going.

## The Bottom Line

Terra cotta and cast stone do not fail like brick, because they are not built like brick. They are decorative units hung on steel, and the steel is what fails first, invisibly, years before a crack appears. By the time ornament is visibly displaced, the anchoring behind it has usually been deteriorating for a long time.

That is also the good news: caught early — while the issue is crazing, open joints and staining rather than displaced units — this is a maintenance problem. Caught late, it is a sidewalk closure.
