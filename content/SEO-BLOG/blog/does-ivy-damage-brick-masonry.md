## The Short Answer: It Depends on the Wall

Here's the quotable version: **ivy growing on sound, hard-fired brick with intact, well-pointed mortar usually causes little structural harm** — and can even shade the wall from sun and driving rain. **But ivy growing on soft, older brick with deteriorating lime mortar and existing cracks is genuinely destructive.** The plant doesn't create weakness out of nothing; it *finds and exploits* weakness that's already there. Since the vast majority of ivy-covered homes in Chicagoland are exactly the older, soft-mortar buildings where that weakness exists, the practical answer for most local homeowners is: ivy is working against your masonry.

That distinction — healthy wall versus compromised wall — is the whole story, so let's look at how the plant actually attaches and what it does once it's on.

## How Climbing Plants Grip Your Brick

Not all "ivy" is the same, and the way a vine attaches determines how much trouble it causes. There are two basic strategies.

**Adhesive pads (holdfasts).** Boston ivy and Virginia creeper — the two most common "ivies" on Chicago-area homes, and not true ivies at all — climb using tendrils tipped with small sticky discs. They glue themselves to the surface rather than boring into it. This is the *less* invasive method, but the pads still leave stubborn residue and can lift the outer skin of weathered brick when torn away.

**Clinging aerial roots.** True **English ivy** (*Hedera helix*) climbs with fine aerial rootlets that don't just stick to the surface — they actively probe into pores, hairline cracks, and open mortar joints, then thicken as they mature. This is the genuinely destructive climber, because the roots behave like thousands of tiny wedges seeking out any gap in the wall.

The key point: a holdfast or rootlet can't drill through a solid, well-fired brick or a tight joint of sound mortar. It can only enter something that's *already* open — a hairline crack, a sandy lime joint, a spalled face. Which is why the condition of your masonry matters more than the plant itself.

## Harmless or Harmful? A Side-by-Side

| Factor | Ivy is relatively harmless when… | Ivy is destructive when… |
|---|---|---|
| **Brick** | Hard-fired, dense, modern brick with a sound face | Soft, older, hand-molded or early machine-made brick; already spalling |
| **Mortar** | Intact, tooled Type N/S mortar joints | Soft lime mortar, sandy or receding joints |
| **Existing damage** | No cracks; wall is tight and well-pointed | Hairline cracks, open joints, prior spalling |
| **Climber type** | Adhesive-pad types (Boston ivy, Virginia creeper) | True English ivy with clinging aerial roots |
| **Exposure** | Wall dries quickly, good drainage | North-facing, shaded, slow-drying wall |
| **Element** | Field of wall, away from details | On chimneys, gutters, flashing, wood trim, sealant |

If your wall lands mostly in the right-hand column — and most pre-1940 Chicagoland brick does — the ivy is a liability, not a feature.

## What Ivy Actually Does to Masonry

When ivy *is* harmful, it works through several mechanisms at once. Here's what's happening behind the green.

### It pries open deteriorating mortar joints and cracks

This is the headline damage. English ivy's aerial roots seek out **deteriorating mortar joints** and hairline cracks, grow into them, and then expand as they mature. A joint that started as a thin gap gets levered wider season after season. On soft **lime mortar** — the kind holding together most century-old Chicago brick — the roots penetrate easily and the damage compounds. What began as a cosmetic open joint becomes a path for water straight into the wall.

### It traps moisture and stops the wall from drying

Even ivy that never cracks a single joint changes how your wall handles water. A dense mat of leaves and stems holds rain against the brick and dramatically slows drying. Masonry is designed to get wet and then *dry out*; when it can't dry, you get the whole Chicagoland deterioration cascade — **freeze-thaw** cycling on saturated brick, **efflorescence** (the white chalky bloom of salts left as water evaporates through the wall), and steady erosion of the mortar. In a climate with dozens of freeze-thaw cycles a winter, keeping a wall permanently damp is one of the worst things you can do to it.

### It accelerates freeze-thaw spalling

Wet brick that freezes spalls — the saturated face pops and flakes off as the trapped water expands. Ivy keeps the brick wetter for longer going into every freeze, so **spalling brick** shows up faster and more widely on ivy-covered walls than on bare ones nearby. Once the face is gone, the exposed body of the brick absorbs even more water and the cycle speeds up.

### It hides deterioration you can't inspect

One of the quietest problems: you simply **can't see the wall**. Cracks, open joints, spalling, a failing lintel, a leaking flashing — all of it can progress for years under a green blanket while you assume everything's fine. By the time a problem shows through the ivy, it's usually well advanced. A wall you can't inspect is a wall you can't maintain.

### It fouls gutters, flashing, sealant, and trim

Ivy doesn't respect the field of the wall. It climbs into gutters and downspouts, works under **flashing** and roof edges, lifts caulk and **sealant** joints, and rots wood trim, fascia, and window frames. Damage to those water-management details then dumps *more* water into the masonry — a second-order problem that's easy to miss.

### It leaves "ivy scars" when you remove it

Finally, the damage that happens on the way *off* the wall. Holdfasts and aerial roots bond tightly; yanking live ivy tears away the brick's outer skin and pulls loose mortar with it, leaving a freckled pattern of residue and pockmarks masons call **ivy scars**. Removal done wrong can do more visible harm in an afternoon than the vine did in a decade — which is why technique matters.

## How to Remove Ivy From Brick Without Wrecking the Wall

If you've decided the ivy has to go, resist the urge to grab and pull. The safe sequence is patient but simple:

1. **Cut the vine at the base.** Sever every stem near the ground and, if you can, treat or dig out the roots so it doesn't resprout.
2. **Let it die and dry.** Wait a few weeks. As the vine dies, its grip weakens and the leaves and stems dry and brown. Detaching dead ivy is far gentler on the brick than fighting a living, gripping plant.
3. **Detach gently, top down.** Ease the dried stems off by hand or with a plastic scraper. Don't power-wash live vines and don't wrench holdfasts off sound brick.
4. **Scrape and soft-brush the residue.** Remove leftover pads and rootlet residue with a scraper and a stiff (non-metal) brush. Metal wire brushes and aggressive grinding damage the brick face — avoid them, especially on soft historic brick.
5. **Inspect and repair the masonry underneath.** This is the step homeowners skip and regret. Removal almost always exposes joints and brick that were **already failing** under the cover. Have the wall inspected and get the necessary **tuckpointing** and **brick replacement** done while the wall is bare and accessible.

## Keeping Ivy Without Hurting Your Brick

If your masonry is genuinely sound and you love the look, you can keep ivy in check rather than banning it:

- **Grow it on a trellis or wire system held off the wall**, so the plant never touches the brick directly.
- **Keep vines off mortar joints, chimneys, gutters, downspouts, flashing, and any wood.** Prune aggressively at these details.
- **Choose adhesive-pad climbers** (Boston ivy, Virginia creeper) over true English ivy if you're planting fresh.
- **Inspect the masonry every year**, ideally where you can actually see the brick, and repoint small problems before they grow.
- **Keep water moving away from the wall** — clean gutters, good downspout extensions, proper grading — so the wall behind the greenery can dry.

## The Bottom Line

Does ivy damage brick? On a tight, modern, well-pointed wall, not much. On the soft-mortar, century-old brick that defines Chicagoland's neighborhoods, **yes — it pries open failing joints, traps moisture, feeds freeze-thaw spalling, and hides the deterioration while it happens.** The plant is only ever as damaging as the wall lets it be, and most old Chicago brick lets it be plenty.
