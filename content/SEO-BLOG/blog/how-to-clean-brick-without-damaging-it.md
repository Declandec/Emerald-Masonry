Brick can be cleaned safely — but only with the gentlest method that actually works: low-pressure water, soft brushing, and masonry-specific chemical cleaners selected for the particular stain. **Sandblasting, high-pressure washing, wire brushing and film-forming coatings permanently destroy the fired face of the brick** and set off years of accelerated deterioration. ## The One Thing to Understand About Brick

A brick is hardest at its surface. During firing, the outer skin of the unit vitrifies — it becomes denser, harder and far less absorbent than the body of the brick underneath. That fired face is the brick's weather protection. It is the reason a well-made brick sheds a century of rain.

**That skin is thin, and it does not grow back.**

Every destructive brick-cleaning method does the same fundamental thing: it removes that skin and exposes the soft, porous interior. The wall looks clean and bright the day the work is done. Then it starts absorbing several times more water than it did before, and in a climate that delivers dozens of freeze-thaw cycles per winter, saturated brick **spalls** — the faces flake, shell and pop off.

This is why professional masonry cleaning follows one rule: **use the gentlest method that achieves an acceptable result.** Not the fastest. Not the most dramatic. The gentlest.

## Diagnose the Stain Before You Touch It

Most "dirty" brick is communicating something about water. Identify what you are looking at first — the right treatment and, more importantly, the right underlying repair depend entirely on it.

| What you see | What it usually is | What it means | Treatment approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Green or greenish-black film** | Algae, moss, lichen | The wall stays wet — shade, splash, gutters, vegetation | Biological cleaner + soft brush + fix the moisture source |
| **Chalky white bloom** | Efflorescence (mineral salts) | Water is actively moving through the masonry | Dry brush or masonry cleaner; find and stop the water path |
| **Black sooty film** | Carbon, soot, urban grime | Combustion or airborne pollution deposits | Masonry degreasing cleaner with dwell time |
| **Orange / rust streaks** | Corroding steel lintel or embedded metal | Steel is rusting and expanding inside the wall | Cleaning is cosmetic — the lintel needs repair |
| **White crusty deposits low on the wall** | Salt from deicers and splash | Road salt is being driven into the masonry | Rinse and neutralize; manage splash and grade |
| **Dark streaks below sills and ledges** | Concentrated water runoff | A sill, coping or flashing has stopped shedding water | Repair the detail above the staining |
| **Blistering or peeling paint** | Trapped moisture behind a film coating | The wall cannot dry outward | Assess before removal; often signals spalling underneath |

Notice the pattern: in nearly every row, the stain is a symptom and the water is the problem. Cleaning a wall without addressing the moisture source buys you a season or two.

## Methods That Are Safe on Brick

**Low-pressure water rinsing.** Modest pressure, a wide fan tip, generous standoff distance, and the wall pre-wetted so it does not drink in cleaning chemicals. Water alone, applied patiently, removes a surprising amount of surface soil.

**Soft or medium natural-bristle brushing.** Slower than a machine and far kinder to the brick. This is the workhorse of safe masonry cleaning.

**Masonry-specific chemical cleaners, chosen by stain type.** There is no universal brick cleaner. Biological growth wants a biocidal cleaner; carbon and grease want a degreaser; efflorescence and mineral deposits want a mild acidic masonry cleaner used carefully. Every one of them requires pre-wetting the wall, controlled dwell time, and a thorough neutralizing rinse. Left to dry on the masonry, an acidic cleaner will etch brick and burn mortar joints.

**Always test a discreet patch first.** Different brick from different eras responds differently, and a small test area in an inconspicuous spot answers the question before the whole facade does.

**Diluted bleach solutions** are widely used on algae and moss, applied to a pre-wetted wall with a dwell period and a full rinse. Protect plantings below, and never mix bleach with acidic cleaners.

## Methods That Damage Brick — Avoid Them

**Sandblasting and abrasive blasting.** This is the worst thing you can do to a brick building. It strips the fired face outright, hollows the mortar joints, erases the crisp arrises of the brick, and converts a durable wall into an absorbent one. On historic masonry it is irreversible destruction of original material. Older Chicago brick — softer, often handmade or locally produced — is especially vulnerable.

**High-pressure washing.** The same damage delivered by water instead of grit. High pressure blasts mortar out of the joints, etches the brick face, and forces water deep into the wall assembly, where it shows up later as interior damage and efflorescence.

**Wire brushes and aggressive grinding.** Fast, satisfying, and destructive to both brick face and joint edges.

**Muriatic acid used casually.** Strong acid on a dry wall burns mortar joints, discolors brick and can drive iron staining permanently into the masonry. Acidic cleaning has a place in professional work; undiluted hardware-store acid on a Saturday afternoon does not.

**Paint and film-forming "waterproof" coatings.** Applied to make a wall look clean and stay clean, they seal water *in*. Chicagoland brick needs to dry outward. A wall that cannot dry spalls its faces from behind, and painted brick is one of the most expensive conditions to reverse — often the paint cannot be removed without damaging the brick underneath.

## The Chicagoland Context

Regional conditions determine which stains you get and how fast they escalate.

**North elevations grow things.** Shaded walls with little sun exposure and slow drying are where algae, moss and lichen establish. It is common to find one elevation of a Chicago bungalow green and the opposite elevation clean — the difference is drying time, not dirt.

**Freeze-thaw punishes anything that increases absorption.** This is what makes aggressive cleaning so much more dangerous here than in a mild climate. A wall that has lost its fired face in Chicago will show spalling within a few winters.

**Road salt and deicers** are driven into the lowest courses by splash all winter, producing crusty white deposits and contributing to surface loss at grade.

**Urban soot and carbon** on city buildings, chimneys and fireplaces produce the dark films that homeowners most often want removed — and on chimneys, staining frequently accompanies actual masonry failure.

**The housing stock is old and soft.** Chicago's **brick bungalows**, **two-flats**, worker cottages and **greystones** were largely built between 1890 and 1940 using soft brick, **lime mortar**, and enormous quantities of porous **Chicago common brick**. That masonry does not tolerate aggressive cleaning at all.

## A Safe Cleaning Sequence

1. **Assess the wall first.** Identify the stain, the moisture source and the condition of the joints.
2. **Repoint failing joints before cleaning.** Washing a wall with open joints drives water into the wall assembly. Repointing first, cleaning second, is the correct order.
3. **Repair the water source.** Gutter, downspout, sill, coping, flashing, grade, vegetation — whichever applies.
4. **Protect the surroundings.** Plantings, windows, adjacent materials and walkways.
5. **Pre-wet the masonry thoroughly** so it will not absorb cleaning chemicals into its depth.
6. **Test a small, inconspicuous area** with the intended cleaner and method.
7. **Apply the cleaner, respect the dwell time, agitate softly** with an appropriate brush.
8. **Rinse completely at low pressure**, neutralizing where the product requires it.
9. **Let the wall dry fully and reassess.** Repeating a gentle process twice beats escalating to an aggressive one once.
10. **Consider breathable protection** only if warranted, and only vapor-permeable products.

## DIY or Hire a Professional?

There is a reasonable DIY zone here, and there is a zone where the downside is permanent.

**Reasonable to do yourself:** light algae or moss on sound, modern, ground-floor brick with intact mortar joints. A garden hose, a masonry-safe cleaner or a properly diluted bleach solution, a soft natural-bristle brush, patience, and a thorough rinse will handle it. Test a small area first, protect the plantings, and resist the urge to reach for the pressure washer when it goes slowly.

**Worth hiring out:** historic or soft brick of any kind, including **Chicago common brick**; painted brick; any wall with failing or receding mortar joints; heavy staining that a gentle method will not touch; chimneys and anything else requiring ladders or staging; commercial facades; and any stain that turns out to be a symptom — rust from a lintel, streaking below a failed sill, efflorescence from an active leak. In every one of those cases the cleaning is the smaller half of the job.

The asymmetry is what should drive the decision. A DIY cleaning that underperforms costs you a Saturday. A DIY cleaning done with the wrong pressure or the wrong chemical costs you the fired face of your brick, and there is no undoing that.

## Keeping Brick Clean the Easy Way

The most effective brick cleaning is the cleaning you never have to do, and it comes down to keeping walls dry:

- **Keep gutters clear and downspouts extended** away from the building. A single overflowing gutter produces years of streaking and algae on the wall below it.
- **Slope grade away from the foundation** and control splash-back at the base of walls.
- **Trim vegetation back** off the masonry so walls get air and sun; keep ivy off walls with questionable joints.
- **Repoint failing joints promptly** — sound joints keep water out, and water is what feeds nearly every stain in the table above.
- **Repair sills, crowns, coping and flashing** as soon as they stop shedding water.
- **Inspect annually**, and after major storms.
