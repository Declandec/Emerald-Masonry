## A Brick Fell Off Your House. Do This First.

**Keep people away from the area beneath it, photograph everything before moving anything, and get a licensed masonry contractor out to assess it — because whatever released that piece is still holding the units around it.** Fallen masonry is the one thing in this trade that is genuinely time-sensitive. Most masonry problems can wait for the right season. This one cannot, and the reason is simple: **a brick does not fall by itself.** Something failed — a joint, a tie, a lintel, an anchor — and that same failure is present in the masonry immediately around the piece that came down.

## The First Hour

1. **Clear the area beneath and beside it.** People, vehicles, the spot where the kids come out the side door. If it is above a public sidewalk, an entrance, a gangway or a parking area, treat that ground as closed.
2. **Photograph before you touch anything.** The fallen material where it landed, the void it came from, the surrounding wall, and a wide shot showing the whole elevation. This matters for diagnosis and for any insurance conversation.
3. **Note the weather.** A hard freeze, a heavy storm, a wind event or a nearby impact is directly relevant to both the cause and whether an insurer will treat it as sudden damage.
4. **Move the debris out of the walkway — but keep it.** Do not clean the mortar off. The **fracture surface and adhering mortar tell a mason a great deal** about why it failed, and sound original units are frequently reused in the repair.
5. **Look up, carefully, from a safe distance.** Anything else visibly loose, tilted or projecting?
6. **Call a masonry contractor** and say plainly that material has fallen. That should route you differently than a routine estimate request.

## Hazard or Nuisance? How to Tell

Not everything that lands on the ground is an emergency. This is roughly how we triage a call.

| What came down | Likely severity | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Loose mortar crumbs, sandy debris** | Low — but diagnostic | Joints failing; schedule repointing |
| **A single spalled face fragment** | Low to moderate | Brick surface failing; assess the elevation |
| **A whole brick from a wall** | **High** | A tie, joint or bond failed — neighbors are suspect |
| **Brick from above an opening** | **High** | Likely a corroding steel lintel lifting masonry |
| **Cornice, coping or ornamental stone** | **High** | Anchors have failed; more units are anchored the same way |
| **Anything from a chimney** | **High** | Most exposed masonry; often accompanies a lean |
| **Anything above a sidewalk or entrance** | **Highest** | Safety and liability, regardless of size |

**Three conditions are urgent whether or not anything has fallen yet:** a **leaning chimney**, a **bulging or bowing wall**, and **visibly displaced masonry** projecting out of plane.

## The Six Conditions That Should Not Wait Until Spring

Most masonry genuinely can wait for better weather. These six cannot, and it is worth being able to recognize them yourself.

**1. A leaning chimney.** A stack out of plumb has lost structural integrity, and it sits above a roof and usually above ground people use. There is no version of this that improves over a winter.

**2. A bulging or bowing wall.** Sight along the wall from a corner. A wall that is not flat has lost cohesion or its ties to the structure behind it — and repointing does not address either.

**3. Masonry you can move by hand.** If a unit shifts under light pressure, it is being held by friction rather than by mortar.

**4. Anything displaced above an entrance, sidewalk, gangway or parking area.** Size is irrelevant here. A fist-sized piece of cornice from three stories up is a serious object.

**5. Cracked or lifted brick above an opening with rust staining.** That is a **corroding steel lintel** actively expanding inside the wall. It does not pause, and the failure mode is masonry dropping over a doorway or window.

**6. Fresh debris appearing repeatedly.** One event may be a single failed unit. Debris appearing after each freeze means an active, progressing condition.

Everything else — receded joints, isolated spalling, efflorescence, a cracked chimney crown — is genuinely a scheduling question rather than an emergency. **Knowing which list your problem is on is most of the decision**, and it is a reasonable thing to ask a contractor directly on the phone.

**A note on winter specifically.** These calls cluster after hard freeze cycles, and that timing is awkward: the conditions that caused the failure are also the conditions that make permanent repair difficult, since mortar must cure above freezing. That is exactly why stabilization and repair are separate steps. **Making it safe is a winter job. Repairing it properly is often a spring one.** A contractor who tells you that is being straight with you, not stalling.

## Why It Fell

Almost always one of five things, and knowing which one determines the repair.

### Freeze-thaw and open joints
The most common. Water enters through failed mortar joints, freezes, expands roughly nine percent, and progressively breaks the bond and the brick faces. Chicagoland delivers dozens of those crossings each winter — which is why fallen masonry calls spike after a hard freeze cycle.

### A corroding steel lintel
Brick from directly above a window, door or garage opening usually means the **steel lintel** spanning it is rusting. Corroding steel expands to several times its original volume, lifts the courses above, cracks the joint line and eventually pushes units out. **Rust staining below the opening is the warning that precedes this.**

### Failed ties on veneer
On brick veneer, metal ties anchor the brick skin to the structure behind it. They corrode when the cavity stays wet, and when enough of them go, the veneer loses its attachment. This is the one you cannot see coming.

### Failed anchors on ornament
Cornices, coping, terra cotta and cast stone are hung on the building with cramps, dowels and shelf angles. Same mechanism as lintels — corroding steel expanding inside rigid masonry — and the same escalation, on the tallest and most public part of the building.

### A parapet losing cohesion
On flat-roofed commercial buildings, the wall above the roofline is weathered on both faces with no interior drying it. Deep joint failure there loosens units directly over the sidewalk.

## What a Contractor Actually Does on an Urgent Call

Two visits, two scopes. Worth understanding so the pricing makes sense.

**Visit one — stabilization.**
1. Assess the immediate hazard and the surrounding masonry.
2. **Remove or secure loose material**, working down from the top so nothing is left overhead.
3. **Install protection below** — barricades, netting, or a sidewalk canopy on a commercial building.
4. Photograph and document conditions.
5. Advise on whether the area can reopen.

**Visit two — the actual repair**, scoped properly once nothing is falling:
1. Full assessment of the elevation, not just the failure point.
2. Identify the water path feeding it.
3. Written scope with photographs and a priority ranking.
4. Repair — repointing, matched **brick replacement**, [lintel repair](/services/lintel-repair), anchor replacement, [parapet rebuilding](/services/parapet-wall-repair) or a [chimney rebuild](/services/chimney-repair) as the diagnosis requires.

**Stabilization is not the repair.** It is what makes the site safe enough to plan the repair without pressure.

## The Insurance Question

Be realistic about this. **Sudden damage from a covered peril** — a storm, wind, vehicle impact, falling tree — may be claimable. **Gradual deterioration, freeze-thaw damage and deferred maintenance generally are not**, because insurers classify those as maintenance no matter how expensive they have become.

What helps: photographs of the event, dated photographs of the prior condition if you have them, weather records for the date, and a **written contractor assessment distinguishing sudden damage from pre-existing deterioration**. That last document is what turns an argument into a claim.

## Commercial, HOA and Multi-Family

The stakes shift when the ground below is public or shared.

- **Falling masonry above a public sidewalk** is a life-safety and liability matter first, a repair second.
- **Chicago's exterior wall inspection requirements** apply to qualifying buildings, and an unsafe finding carries obligations. Requirements vary by municipality and building type.
- **Boards and property managers** should document the event, the stabilization and the repair, and route it through the insurer and counsel as their governing documents require.
- **Protection below stays up** until the repair is complete — netting and canopies are containment, not a fix.

## What This Means for the Rest of the Building

Here is the part homeowners resist, and it is the most important sentence on this page.

**One unit fell because a condition exists. That condition is not local to the unit that fell.**

If a joint failure released it, the joints around it are the same age and the same mortar. If a lintel released it, the other openings have lintels of the same era. If ties released it, the ties across that elevation went in the same week in the same weather.

Which is why the right response to a fallen brick is an assessment of **the elevation** — not a patch of the hole.

## If It Is a Shared or Neighboring Wall

On Chicago's narrow lots this comes up constantly. If masonry has fallen from a **party wall**, from the neighbor's building into your gangway, or from your building into theirs, document it the same way and notify the other owner in writing the same day. Do not begin repairs on shared masonry unilaterally — responsibility usually depends on the deed, any recorded party wall agreement, and where the wall actually sits, and a well-intentioned unilateral repair can complicate both the cost split and any insurance claim. In a condo or HOA, exterior masonry is normally a common element and the association handles it. Protect the ground below immediately regardless of who ultimately pays.

## Preventing the Next One

- **Walk your perimeter after hard freezes and storms** and note what is on the ground. Mortar crumbs are early warning.
- **Treat rust staining below any opening as urgent** — it is the visible stage of an invisible structural process.
- **Keep water off the masonry**: gutters clear, downspouts extended, grade sloping away, splash controlled.
- **Repoint before spalling starts** with mortar matched softer than the brick — [tuckpointing](/services/tuckpointing) is the cheapest rung on the ladder.
- **Sight along your walls** from a corner once a year, looking for bulges.
- **Get chimneys and parapets inspected** on a cycle; they fail first and they fail onto things.
- **Never let anyone seal weep holes** on veneer, or repoint soft old brick with hard modern mortar.
