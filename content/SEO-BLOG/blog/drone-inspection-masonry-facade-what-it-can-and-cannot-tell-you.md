## The short answer

**A drone is an excellent reconnaissance tool and a poor substitute for touching the wall.**

It will get you clear, dated imagery of a parapet, a coping line, a cornice and a chimney that nobody has looked at in twenty years, for a fraction of what a lift or a swing stage costs. That is real value and we use it.

What it cannot do is **sound** the masonry for hollow units, **probe** mortar to find out whether it has gone soft, determine whether a coping joint is open **through the wall** or only at the surface, or see the **steel and flashing inside the assembly.** Those four things are where the expensive masonry failures live, and none of them photograph.

Used properly, a drone survey **targets** a hands-on assessment. Sold as a replacement for one, it produces a beautiful report and an underestimated budget.

## What a drone genuinely does well

Let us be fair to the tool, because it earns its place.

**It reaches what you cannot.** Parapets, copings, cornices, upper elevations, chimney crowns, roof edges, and the alley-side elevation nobody has photographed since the building changed hands. On a mid-rise, getting eyes on the coping line otherwise means a lift, a swing stage, or a rope tech.

**It is cheap enough to repeat.** Which makes it useful as a **monitoring** tool. Fly the same elevations annually, compare imagery, and you can see a crack lengthen or a coping joint open over time — a genuinely valuable record that hands-on inspection at that frequency would never be funded for.

**It creates a dated baseline.** For insurance, for storm-damage claims, for tenant disputes, for a sale. Dated photographic evidence of what a wall looked like on a specific day is worth having, and it is the same argument as a pre-construction survey.

**It triages the expensive access.** This is the strongest use. Fly first, find the three elevations and the two roof-edge sections that actually need close examination, then spend the lift day where it matters instead of spreading it thin across a whole building.

**It is safe.** Nobody is on a ladder over a sidewalk to find out whether the parapet needs looking at.

## The four things it cannot see

### 1. Hollow and debonded units

This is the big one, and it is not a resolution problem — no camera solves it.

**A brick can be completely debonded from the wall and look perfect.** The face is intact, the joints around it read normally, the color matches. The only way to find it is to **tap the wall** — with a hammer handle, a mallet, a sounding rod — and listen. Sound masonry rings solid. A hollow or debonded unit returns a distinctly different tone, and an experienced mason hears it immediately.

That matters commercially because **the count of failed units is most of the price.** An assessment that cannot produce that count cannot produce a scope. It can only produce an allowance.

It also matters for safety: a debonded unit above an entrance is exactly the failure that stops being a repair and becomes an incident, and it is invisible in imagery.

### 2. Mortar that has gone soft

Mortar fails from the inside before it retreats from the face. A joint can photograph as full and flush while being soft enough to rake out with a house key.

The test is physical: **push a screwdriver tip or a key into the joint.** Sound mortar resists and barely marks. Failed mortar crumbles, gives way, or lets the tool sink in.

Related and equally invisible: **mortar hardness compatibility.** On pre-1930s soft brick, a previous repointing done in hard Portland-heavy mortar is actively damaging the building, because the joint is now stronger than the unit and stress goes through the brick face instead. From the air that wall looks recently maintained. Up close it is a wall that has already been harmed and whose next repair has to undo the last one.

### 3. Whether a coping joint is open *through* the wall

A drone will show you a coping joint that has opened. Good — that is genuinely useful and it is where a lot of parapet failure begins.

What it cannot tell you is **how far in it goes.** A surface-open joint on an otherwise sound parapet is a reset-and-repoint. A joint open through to the collar joint, with the parapet core saturated and the wythes separating from inside, is a **rebuild** — and that distinction is one of the largest single swings in any commercial masonry budget.

Determining it means getting up there, lifting a coping stone or probing, and finding out. A photograph of the top of the joint is the beginning of that question, not the answer to it.

### 4. Concealed steel and flashing

**Shelf angles.** The steel carrying a veneer at each floor line. As it corrodes it expands — **rust jacking** — and lifts the courses above, drawing a horizontal crack running *level* across an elevation. A drone will photograph that crack beautifully. It will not tell you whether the steel behind it has surface rust or has lost half its section, and that difference decides between coating and replacement.

**Lintels.** Same story over every opening. Rust staining down the brick is the visible early warning; the condition of the steel is not.

**Through-wall flashing and weep holes.** On any cavity wall, this is the drainage system, and it is the most common cause of persistent leaks on veneer construction. Whether the flashing was turned up the backup, whether it was cut short, whether the weeps are open or mortared shut and buried — that is a close-up determination.

**Wall ties.** By the time a wall bows enough to read in an aerial image, the ties have been failing for years.

## What about thermal imaging?

Infrared thermography comes up in the same conversation and deserves the same honesty.

Under the right conditions — appropriate temperature differential, correct time of day, dry surface, an operator who knows what they are looking at — it can indicate **moisture in an assembly** and thermal anomalies that correlate with saturation or missing insulation. That is useful, and on a wall with a leak nobody can locate it can genuinely narrow the search.

But it is **interpretive**. It shows temperature difference, not water, not voids, not corroded steel. Weather, sun exposure, wall composition and surface finish all confound it. Treat it the way you treat the drone: a pointer toward where to investigate, not a verdict.

## Chicago: do not assume a drone satisfies a requirement

In the City of Chicago, exterior wall conditions are enforced by the Department of Buildings, and buildings above certain heights and construction types carry **periodic exterior wall examination** obligations on cycles the Department sets.

These regimes are generally built around **close-up, hands-on examination** of the wall — for exactly the reasons above, since a photographic survey cannot establish whether units are debonded. Requirements vary by building and they change.

**Confirm with the Department of Buildings what is acceptable for your specific address.** Do not let a vendor's marketing be your compliance advice.

## Why this matters more in Chicagoland than most places

Our climate produces the exact failure mode that cameras miss.

Chicagoland runs through dozens of freeze-thaw cycles in a typical winter — repeated crossings of 32°F rather than one sustained freeze. Each crossing expands water held inside saturated brick and mortar. **Wet masonry plus repeated freezing** works units loose from the inside, debonds them from their beds, and softens mortar behind an intact-looking face, long before anything shows on the surface.

Add heavy de-icing salt at entrances and loading docks, lake-effect moisture, and a building stock ranging from soft pre-1930s common brick and greystone through post-war veneer over block, and you get a region where **the gap between how a wall looks and how a wall is** is unusually wide.

That gap is precisely the gap a drone cannot cross.

## What to ask for, whatever tool is used

Any assessment you pay for should hand you:

1. **Findings by elevation, with quantities** — square footage of repointing, count of failed units, number of lintels, linear feet of coping
2. **Immediate vs. reserve**, split explicitly
3. **Parapets, lintels, shelf angles and flashing addressed by name** — if those words are absent, they were not examined
4. **Access limitations disclosed** — "north and rear elevations assessed from grade and aerial imagery only" is an honest report; silence is not
5. **Dated photographs keyed to locations**, not a gallery
6. **A stated method** for what happens when the wall is opened and reveals more

## A sensible inspection routine

- **Twice a year**, walk the envelope at grade — spring and fall — photographing the same locations
- **After every major storm**, and after any work that penetrated a wall
- **Aerial survey annually** on buildings where parapets and upper elevations are otherwise unreachable, comparing year over year
- **Hands-on close assessment** on a longer cycle, and immediately if imagery shows change, if anything has been observed falling, or if you are buying, refinancing or budgeting a phase
- **Keep the record.** The value of all of this compounds only if last year's images are still findable
