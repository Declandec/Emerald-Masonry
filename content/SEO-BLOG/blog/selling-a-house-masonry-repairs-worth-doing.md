## A Different Job Than Restoration

If you are selling, you are not restoring the building. **You are trying to keep masonry from becoming a negotiating item.**

That is a narrower job with a smaller budget, and the repairs that serve it are not always the ones that look worst from the curb. The organizing question is simple: **what will end up in the inspection report, and what will a buyer's agent do with it?**

## What Inspectors Reliably Flag

A general home inspector is not a mason, but the masonry items they consistently write up are predictable:

- **Loose, missing or spalled brick** — visible and unambiguous.
- **Mortar joints visibly receded** behind the brick face.
- **Rust staining above windows and doors** — the steel lintel corroding.
- **Cracked or missing chimney crown**, deteriorated flashing, missing cap.
- **Step cracks** running diagonally through joints.
- **Efflorescence**, especially on a basement wall.
- **Any active water entry** or staining on interior surfaces.
- **Grade or mulch piled against the brick.**
- **Bowing, bulging or leaning masonry** — which escalates immediately.

**Every one of those becomes a line item.** And a line item without a number attached is the expensive kind, because the buyer fills in the number themselves.

## What Inspectors Generally Do Not Evaluate

Worth knowing, because it tells you where *not* to spend:

- **Mortar hardness relative to the brick** — the most consequential thing about your wall, and a trade judgment.
- **Joint preparation depth** on a past repointing job.
- **Whether the mortar color matches** — cosmetic, rarely mentioned.
- **Joint profile** and tooling quality.
- **Whether the correct mortar type was used** on a prior repair.

So a wall with a slightly mismatched previous repair is usually a non-event in a sale, while a chimney crown with a visible crack is a line item every time.

## The Priority Ranking for Sellers

### Tier 1 — Fix these
Things that trigger a contingency, imply water in the house, or read as a safety issue.

**Chimney crown, cap and flashing.** The chimney is the most exposed masonry on the house and inspectors always look at it. A cracked crown is cheap to address and expensive to have written up.

**Rust-stained lintels.** Visible, unambiguous, and a buyer will assume the worst about what is behind it.

**Loose or spalled brick anywhere reachable.** Reads as neglect and as a safety issue.

**Anything actively admitting water.** Interior staining traced to masonry is the single worst item to leave on the table.

**Obviously receding joints on the front elevation.** Highest visibility, first impression, first photograph.

### Tier 2 — Get priced, then decide
**Repointing a secondary elevation.** Get the number; whether it pays depends on how bad it looks and how the market is moving.

**Sill repair.** Worth doing if cracked or dropped, less so if merely weathered.

**Step cracks.** Worth an assessment so you can say whether they are dormant — a documented dormant crack is a far smaller negotiating item than an undocumented one.

**Grade and drainage.** Often nearly free and it removes an inspection note.

### Tier 3 — Usually skip
**Mortar color mismatch** from an old repair. **General staining and algae** — a gentle cleaning is enough. **Efflorescence with no active water** behind it. **Full-envelope repointing** on a house you are leaving. **Cosmetic tuckpointing on the back elevation** nobody photographs.

## The Repair-vs-Credit Math

Sellers regularly ask whether to fix it or credit it. In most cases, **fixing costs less.**

A repair costs what the repair costs. **A credit costs what the buyer imagines the repair costs** — and buyers, advised by agents, imagine generously. An inspection note reading "deteriorated mortar joints observed, recommend evaluation by a qualified contractor" has no ceiling on it. The buyer's number will be larger than yours, and it will be argued from a position where you have already accepted the premise.

There is also the risk that is not about money: **masonry uncertainty is a common reason buyers walk.** Brick problems sound structural to people who do not know masonry, and "the inspector flagged the brick" ends more deals than the actual repair cost would justify.

**The cheapest move, regardless of which you choose, is having a written scope with a real number in hand before you list.** Then the negotiation is against your document rather than their imagination.

## What a Pre-Listing Assessment Should Produce

Not a conversation — a document. It should state:

- **Which elevations and elements** were examined, and what could not be reached.
- **Every condition an inspector is likely to flag**, photographed.
- **A priority grade per item** — fix before listing, optional, or leave.
- **What each item costs** to address.
- **What genuinely does not need doing** — frequently the more valuable half, because it tells you where not to spend.
- **The mortar type appropriate to the wall**, so any work done is correct rather than merely fast.

## How to Talk About Masonry With a Buyer

Once the inspection report exists, the conversation is about framing as much as facts, and sellers regularly damage their own position in predictable ways.

**Do not minimize it.** "That's normal for an old brick house" is often true and always sounds like a dodge. It invites the buyer to get their own opinion, and now you have lost control of the number.

**Do produce your document.** A written scope with photographs, priorities and a real price converts an open-ended worry into a defined line item. Buyers negotiate line items. They walk away from open-ended worries.

**Do separate the fixed from the remaining.** If you repaired the chimney and left the back elevation, say exactly that, with the invoice for one and the quote for the other. Partial work you can document reads as maintenance. Partial work you cannot document reads as a cover-up.

**Do let a mason answer masonry questions.** Buyers ask what caused it and whether it will come back, and those are trade questions. A contractor's written answer carries more weight than a seller's reassurance, and costs nothing to obtain.

**Do not over-repair to win the argument.** Full-envelope repointing on a house you are leaving rarely returns what it costs. The goal is to remove line items, not to hand over a restored building.

## Timing and Weather

Start earlier than feels necessary, for two reasons.

**Mortar needs temperature to cure.** Chicagoland late-fall and winter work is restricted or requires protection, so a repair you planned in November may not happen on your schedule.

**Fresh mortar cures lighter** and keeps darkening for weeks to months. Work done in early spring reads as settled by summer; work done the week before photos reads as brand new.

If you are listing in spring — which most Chicagoland sellers are — the practical window for masonry is the preceding fall or the first workable stretch of the year.

## Do the Repair Properly Even Though You Are Leaving

There is a temptation to have the cheapest available crew smear something over the problem, since you will not be there.

Two reasons not to.

**A bad repair is visible to the next inspector.** Mortar skimmed over old joints, hard gray mortar packed against soft brick, or caulk troweled into brickwork are all recognizable, and finding a bad repair is worse in a negotiation than finding the original problem — because now the buyer distrusts everything.

**Disclosure.** Illinois sellers complete a residential real property disclosure regarding known material defects. A documented, properly executed repair is a straightforward thing to describe. A cover-up is a different situation, and one worth discussing with your attorney rather than improvising.

## If You Are Buying Instead

The same list read backwards is a buyer's checklist, and it is worth stating because the two sides misread each other constantly.

**A masonry note in an inspection report is not a reason to walk.** Older brick homes in this market have masonry line items — that is normal, and a seller who has documented and priced theirs is doing you a favor rather than hiding something.

**What deserves a closer look before the contingency expires** is the same Tier 1 list: chimney crown and flashing, rust staining above openings, spalled or loose brick, and anything suggesting active water entry. Those are the items where the real number and the imagined number diverge most.

**Get the assessment inside the window.** An inspection note recommending evaluation by a qualified contractor has no price attached, and without a price it is not negotiable. A written scope is.

The reason this matters to a seller is that a well-advised buyer will do exactly this, so the seller who has already done it is negotiating from the same document rather than against a stranger's estimate.

## Chicagoland Context

Local factors that shape this decision here.

**The stock is old brick.** Bungalows, two-flats, Georgians, greystones and post-war ranches across Chicago and the suburbs are predominantly masonry, and buyers in this market are used to seeing masonry line items. That cuts both ways: a modest amount of wear is normal and expected, but a chimney in visibly poor condition stands out because buyers here have seen plenty of good ones.

**Freeze-thaw makes deterioration visible.** Dozens of crossings of the freezing point each winter mean damage progresses on a schedule everyone can see. A wall that looked acceptable two springs ago may not now.

**Soft brick punishes cheap repairs.** Pre-1930 Chicago common brick is softer than modern face brick, and a repointing job done in hard Portland-heavy mortar will spall the faces within a few winters. If you are doing the work, do it with the right mix — it costs essentially the same and it does not backfire.

## What Not to Do Before Listing

Four moves that reliably cost sellers more than they save.

**Do not paint the brick.** It is the fastest way to make a tired elevation look uniform and the fastest way to alarm an informed buyer, because paint on masonry traps moisture and is effectively irreversible. Buyers and inspectors in this market know that, and painted brick reads as something covered up.

**Do not power-wash it.** High pressure strips the fired skin off older brick and drives water deep into the wall. A gentle, low-pressure cleaning is fine; a pressure washer on soft Chicago common brick causes damage that will still be visible at the next inspection.

**Do not caulk your mortar joints.** A bead of sealant troweled into deteriorating brickwork is one of the most recognizable cover-ups there is, and finding it costs you far more credibility than the eroded joints would have.

**Do not seal the wall to hide staining.** A film-forming waterproofer over damaged masonry traps whatever is already in there, and if the buyer's inspector notices fresh sealer over spalled brick, every other item on the report gets read less charitably.

The pattern in all four is the same: **cheap cosmetic fixes on masonry are legible to anyone who knows the material**, and being caught doing one turns a repair negotiation into a trust negotiation.
