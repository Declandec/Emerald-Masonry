## Two Trades That Both Lay Brick

**A new-construction mason builds walls from known modern materials to a specification. A restoration mason repairs an unknown existing wall without damaging it.** Both are genuinely skilled, but they are trained on opposite problems — and on soft pre-1930 Chicagoland masonry, the wrong approach spalls the brick faces within a few winters. This is not a knock on new-construction masons. **The trade is demanding and they are very good at it.** Laying a plumb, level, tolerance-accurate wall at production speed is real skill that restoration work does not require.

But it is a different job, and the difference is not effort. It is what the work is optimized for.

## The Two Problems, Side by Side

| | New construction | Restoration |
|---|---|---|
| **Starting point** | Known materials, a spec, a drawing | An unknown wall of unknown composition |
| **Goal** | Build to tolerance, on schedule | Repair without damaging what exists |
| **Materials** | Ordered to spec, consistent | Matched to what is already there; often salvaged |
| **Mortar** | Specified — usually Type N or S | **Analyzed, then formulated** to match |
| **Speed** | An advantage | Frequently the enemy |
| **Joint prep** | Not applicable — new joints | Grind or **hand-rake**, decided per wall |
| **Units** | Uniform, from a pallet | Variable; matched for absorption and hardness |
| **Failure mode** | Out of plumb, out of tolerance | **Destroying original material** |
| **Success looks like** | Crisp, uniform, clearly new | Invisible — you cannot find the repair |

That last row is the whole thing. **New construction succeeds by looking new. Restoration succeeds by disappearing.**

## The One Rule That Separates Them

**A masonry wall is designed so the mortar is softer than the brick it holds.** Mortar is the sacrificial layer — it absorbs movement and moisture stress and wears out first, because replacing mortar is cheap and replacing century-old brick is not.

Modern construction satisfies that rule easily: dense machine-made brick with **Type N** or **Type S** mortar. The relationship is correct by default, which is why a new-construction mason can use the same mix on nearly every job and never see a problem.

**Pre-1930 masonry inverts the arithmetic.** Soft, low-fired brick — including enormous quantities of **Chicago common brick** — with lime-rich mortar. Apply the standard modern mix and the joints become the strongest element in the wall. Every freeze-thaw cycle then drives stress into the brick faces instead of the joints, and three to eight winters later they begin popping off.

**Those faces do not come back.** That is the difference, stated plainly: on an old house, using the default answer is not a shortcut — it is destruction on a delay.

## What Restoration Technique Actually Involves

Beyond mortar, five practices distinguish the work.

**Mortar analysis and formulation.** Determining the original's hardness, binder and sand, then blending to match — often a **Type O** or custom lime mix rather than anything off a pallet. Judged on **cured** samples, since mortar dries lighter than it finishes.

**Hand joint preparation.** Power grinding is efficient and correct on modern masonry. On soft brick it permanently widens joints and chews the arrises, so joints are raked by hand. **A crew that arrives at a greystone with only a grinder is the wrong crew.**

**Salvaged unit sourcing.** Pre-1940 brick is largely no longer manufactured. Matching means relationships with salvage yards and matching on **absorption and hardness**, not just color and size.

**Unit-by-unit decisions.** Patch, **dutchman** insert, repin, or replace — chosen per stone or per brick rather than applied across the board.

**Knowing what not to do.** No sandblasting. No high-pressure washing. No paint or film-forming sealers on absorbent masonry. Every one of those permanently increases how much water the wall takes on.

## Does Your House Need One?

A rough rule.

**Restoration technique matters:**
- **Pre-1930 masonry** of any kind.
- Visibly **soft or handmade brick**.
- **Greystones**, and any building with carved limestone.
- **Landmark or historic-district** properties.
- Any wall where a **previous repair caused spalling**.
- Churches and older institutional buildings.
- Exposed **Chicago common brick** on side walls, rear elevations, coach houses and party walls.

**Standard technique is fine:**
- Post-war brick veneer.
- Modern construction.
- Concrete block.
- Most 1950s-onward chimneys and walls.

**Match the mason to the masonry, not to the job title.** A 1962 ranch does not need restoration technique, and paying for it is not a virtue.

## How to Tell Who You Are Talking To

Six questions. The answers sort it immediately.

1. **What mortar are you proposing, and why that one for this wall?**
2. **How did you determine what the original mortar was?**
3. **Will you grind or hand-rake, and how did you decide?**
4. **If brick needs replacing, where will the match come from?**
5. **What joint profile will you tool to?**
6. **Will you do a test patch and let it cure before we commit?**

A restoration mason answers these specifically and will have asked about your build era before you finished describing the problem. **Someone who gives the same answer regardless of the house has told you what you need to know** — and that is fine, if your house is the kind that answer suits.

## Warning Signs of the Wrong Fit on an Old Wall

- **A single mortar mix** proposed for every job.
- **No question about the age** of the building.
- **Power grinding** proposed on visibly soft brick.
- **Sandblasting or high-pressure washing** suggested for cleaning.
- **Sealing proposed instead of repointing.**
- **New brick** offered as a match for pre-1940 masonry without discussion of salvage.
- **No test patch** offered.
- **Speed presented as the main advantage.**

That last one is worth sitting with. On new construction, fast is good. **On a century-old wall, fast is usually a symptom.**

## Where the Two Overlap

The distinction is real, but it is not a wall between two species of tradesman, and it would be misleading to present it that way.

**Plenty of excellent restoration masons started in new construction**, and the fundamentals transfer completely — reading a wall, laying to line, striking a joint, working safely at height. What gets added is a different set of judgments: analyze before you specify, match before you build, and remove as little original material as the repair allows.

**Many firms genuinely do both**, and do both well. A crew that lays new veneer on Monday and repoints an 1890s greystone on Thursday is perfectly plausible — provided they actually change approach between the two, which is the whole question.

**The relevant test is not background. It is behavior on your specific wall.**

- Did they ask what era the building is before proposing anything?
- Did they examine the existing mortar rather than assume?
- Did the proposed mix change based on what they found?
- Do they own or have access to a grinder *and* hand tools, and can they say when they use each?
- Do they have salvage sources, or does "we'll match it" mean a trip to the supply house?

**A firm that does both well will answer those without hesitation. A firm that only does new construction will find the questions slightly odd** — not because they are careless, but because on their normal work those decisions are already made by the specification.

There is also a genuine overlap in the other direction worth acknowledging. **Some restoration situations call for new-construction skills** — rebuilding a collapsed section, laying a new chimney above the roofline, building a replacement wall behind a salvaged facade. Restoration is not always delicate hand work, and a firm that cannot lay a fast, plumb, structurally sound wall is not fully equipped either.

**The best outcome on an older building is a contractor who has both modes and knows which one the wall in front of them requires.** That is what to hire for, and it is why the six questions above beat any label.

## What It Costs, Honestly

Restoration work is **more expensive per square foot**, and it should be. Hand-raking is slower than grinding. Custom mortar takes formulation and test batching. Salvage sourcing takes time and sometimes delays a start. Unit-by-unit decisions cannot be made from a truck.

But the comparison that matters is not restoration versus standard repointing. **It is restoration versus standard repointing plus the brick replacement it causes.** A mismatched repair on soft masonry converts a maintenance item into permanent loss of original units — and then you pay for the correct repair anyway.

Cost drivers: scope and elevations, hand work required, mortar matching complexity, height and access, salvage sourcing, undoing prior bad repairs, and any historic review.

## Chicagoland Context

This distinction matters more here than in most markets, for two reasons.

**The housing stock.** Chicago and the older suburbs hold an extraordinary concentration of pre-1940 masonry — **brick bungalows, two-flats, three-flats, greystones, worker cottages, courtyard buildings**, plus the churches and institutional buildings built alongside them. Soft brick and lime mortar, everywhere.

**The climate.** Dozens of freeze-thaw crossings each winter, with no lake buffer across most of the suburbs. A mortar mismatch that might take fifteen years to show in a mild climate shows here in five.

Together they explain something you can see on almost any older Chicago block: **walls visibly damaged by their own repairs.** A band of spalling that corresponds exactly to where somebody repointed a decade ago.

## What Good Restoration Work Looks Like Afterward

A short calibration, because "it looks fine" is not the same as "it was done right."

**You should struggle to find the repair.** Coursing continues, joint widths match, the profile is tooled the same, and any color difference is fresh-mortar pale rather than wrong-hue gray. Give it a season and the pale narrows considerably.

**The brick should be untouched.** No chipped arrises, no widened joints, no grinder scoring across the faces. Damage to the units during joint preparation is the clearest sign of the wrong technique on soft masonry.

**And the wall should still be sound in five winters.** That is the real test, and it is the one a mismatched mortar fails — a band of spalling appearing exactly where the work was done.
