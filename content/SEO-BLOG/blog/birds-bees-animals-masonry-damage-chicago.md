Nothing living chews through a brick wall. Squirrels do not gnaw masonry, birds do not peck through mortar, and no bee in Illinois can drill into fired clay.

What animals do is far more useful to know: **they find the part of your building that has already failed, and they move in.** Which means an infestation is rarely just a pest problem. It is a survey result — free, unsolicited, and generally accurate.

## The Principle: They Find the Weakness

Sound masonry is an inhospitable building material. It is dense, the joints are hard, and there are no openings. Every wildlife problem on a masonry building traces back to one of a short list of failures:

- **Mortar that has lost its binder** and is soft enough to excavate
- **An uncapped or improperly capped chimney**
- **A cracked or deteriorated chimney crown** leaving gaps at the flue
- **Open joints, holes or missing units** in a wall
- **Vent and duct terminations** without functioning covers
- **Gaps where masonry meets roof, soffit or fascia**
- **Ledges, cornices and corbels** that hold debris and give purchase

Fix the masonry and the wildlife problem generally does not recur. Remove the wildlife without fixing the masonry and you have booked yourself an annual appointment.

## Bees in the Mortar Joints

This is the one that genuinely surprises homeowners: small round holes, roughly pencil-width, appearing in the mortar joints of a brick wall, with bees going in and out.

**They are not drilling through masonry.** Solitary bees — mason bees and their relatives — excavate nesting tunnels in mortar that has already deteriorated to the point of being workable. Sound, properly cured mortar of appropriate hardness is not excavatable. Sandy, crumbling, binder-depleted mortar is.

So the presence of bees in your joints is a direct statement about the condition of those joints. It usually correlates with the same wall failing the [screwdriver test](/blog/how-to-tell-if-mortar-is-failing-screwdriver-test).

**Carpenter bees are a different animal, literally.** They bore into wood — fascia, soffits, trim, deck rails — leaving clean round entry holes. If the holes are in wood, that is a carpentry problem. If they are in mortar, it is a masonry one.

**The masonry answer is repointing**, done after the nesting season and after any needed treatment, cutting the joints to sound depth and repacking with mortar matched for color, texture, profile and hardness. Spot-treating the holes with sealant does nothing except hide the condition that attracted them.

## Birds

### In the chimney

The classic. Uncapped flues are attractive nesting sites, and a nest in a flue is a genuine hazard rather than a nuisance: it obstructs draft, which can push combustion products back into the building.

Chimney swifts deserve a specific mention — they nest in chimneys by preference, and they are a protected migratory species. Removal timing matters, and exclusion after the nesting season, followed by capping, is the correct sequence.

**The masonry fix is a properly fitted cap on a sound crown.** A cap alone on a cracked crown solves the animal problem while leaving the water problem entirely intact, which is why we assess the crown at the same time — see [chimney crown vs. chimney cap](/blog/chimney-crown-vs-chimney-cap).

### In vents and ducts

Dryer vents, bath fans and range hoods terminating through masonry are prime nesting sites when the damper or cover has failed. Beyond the nesting itself, a blocked dryer vent is a real fire risk, and the moisture a failed vent dumps into the wall does its own masonry damage — covered in [dryer vents, furnace exhaust and brick damage](/blog/dryer-vents-furnace-exhaust-brick-damage).

### On the facade — pigeons and starlings

Roosting causes damage in three ways:

1. **Droppings are acidic.** They etch limestone, cast stone and terra cotta and accelerate mortar deterioration on prolonged contact.
2. **They hold moisture.** Accumulation keeps masonry damp, which in Chicagoland means more freeze-thaw cycles doing more damage.
3. **Nesting debris blocks drainage.** Nests in gutters, on ledges, behind cornices and in scuppers dam water onto masonry — which is a far bigger structural problem than the droppings.

On commercial and institutional buildings the accumulation on **cornices, corbels, parapets and window heads** is where it hurts most, because those are the details already holding water. There is also a health-and-safety dimension to significant guano accumulation that belongs to a remediation specialist, not a mason.

**The masonry-adjacent fixes** are exclusion hardware — netting, spikes, wire, slope devices — installed so the anchors themselves do not become new water paths into the wall. Anchoring anything into masonry has rules of its own, covered in [anchoring into brick](/blog/anchoring-into-brick-awnings-handrails-signs).

## Squirrels, Raccoons and Bats

**Squirrels** enter at the roofline: gaps where masonry meets soffit, fascia or roof deck, uncapped flues, and openings around vents. The masonry work is closing those gaps properly — repointing, rebuilding a deteriorated section, or restoring the flashing and closure detail — rather than stuffing them.

**Raccoons** are large enough to work loose a compromised chimney crown or a poorly secured cap, which is why cap quality and crown condition matter more than they look like they should.

**Bats** roost in very small gaps — deteriorated joints, gaps behind loose trim, openings at parapets and roof edges. Bats are protected, exclusion is seasonally regulated, and it must be done by a qualified professional before any masonry is closed up. Sealing bats inside a wall is both unlawful in many circumstances and a genuinely bad outcome for the building.

## The Correct Sequence

Getting this order wrong is the most common mistake, and it is worth stating plainly:

1. **Identify what is living there**, and whether it is a protected species or in an active nesting season.
2. **Humane, lawful exclusion by a wildlife professional.** Never seal animals in.
3. **Clean and remediate** the affected area, with appropriate precautions for accumulated droppings or nesting material.
4. **Assess the masonry**, because there is always a reason they got in.
5. **Repair the masonry** — repoint failed joints, rebuild deteriorated sections, repair or rebuild the chimney crown, restore flashing and closures.
6. **Install proper hardware** — chimney cap with spark screen, vent covers with functioning dampers, weep-hole inserts that keep drainage open, exclusion devices where roosting is the issue.
7. **Seal only after everything above.**

## What Not To Do

- **Do not seal weep holes.** They are the drain on a brick-veneer wall, and closing them turns a wall designed to shed water into one that holds it — see [weep holes in masonry walls](/blog/weep-holes-masonry-walls). Use vented inserts.
- **Do not fill bee holes with caulk or expanding foam.** It hides deteriorated mortar and traps moisture in the joint.
- **Do not cap a chimney over live animals.**
- **Do not pressure wash guano off limestone.** High pressure strips the stone's weathered skin permanently, and there are health precautions that a hose does not address.
- **Do not treat exclusion hardware as a masonry substitute.** Netting over a failing cornice keeps birds off a wall that is still deteriorating underneath.
- **Do not anchor exclusion hardware carelessly** — every fastener into masonry is a potential water path if it goes into the brick face rather than the joint.

## DIY vs. Hiring a Mason

Homeowners can reasonably handle the observation side: walking the walls each spring, checking that the chimney cap is still there, keeping gutters and vent covers clear, and trimming branches back from the roof.

The repair side is not a DIY project, for reasons specific to this problem:

- **Wildlife law and timing.** Several of the species involved are protected during nesting seasons. Exclusion has to be done correctly and lawfully, and getting the sequence wrong seals animals into a wall.
- **Reading the mortar.** Judging how deep a joint has to be cut, and specifying mortar hardness that suits the units, is the difference between a repair that lasts and one that spalls the brick within a few winters.
- **Chimney work at height.** Caps, crowns and top courses are roof work on the most weather-damaged masonry on the building.
- **Not making it worse.** Foam in a joint, caulk over bee holes and sealed weep holes are all common homeowner fixes that trap water in a wall.

## Chicagoland Context

Three regional factors make this a persistent issue here.

**The housing stock has a lot of soft mortar.** Chicago's bungalows, two-flats, three-flats and greystones were laid in lime-rich mortar, which is correct for those buildings — but once it has weathered past its service life it becomes exactly the workable material solitary bees look for. The mortar that is right for the building is also the mortar that goes soft first.

**There are a great many unused chimneys.** Decades of furnace and fireplace conversions left Chicago full of stacks nobody thinks about, uncapped and unmaintained — and an unused chimney is still a masonry structure that weathers, leaks and hosts wildlife. Whether to repair or remove one is its own decision, covered in [should I remove my chimney instead of repairing it](/blog/should-i-remove-my-chimney-instead-of-repairing).

**Winter compounds everything.** Every blocked gutter, dammed scupper and moisture-holding accumulation feeds a masonry wall going through dozens of freeze-thaw cycles a season. Wildlife damage in this climate is mostly water damage with a biological cause.

## Maintenance and Prevention

- **Inspect the chimney annually** — cap present and secure, crown sound, joints intact
- **Check vent terminations** each spring for nesting and failed dampers
- **Keep gutters, scuppers and roof drains clear** of nesting debris
- **Walk the walls once a year** looking for new holes in joints, particularly on south-facing elevations where bees are most active
- **Trim branches back** from the roofline — most squirrel access starts as a tree limb
- **Repoint on schedule** rather than waiting for something to move in; sound joints are the whole defense
- **Treat any infestation as an inspection prompt.** They found something. Find out what.
