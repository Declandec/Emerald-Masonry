## Athens Marble: What It Is and Why It Matters

**Athens Marble is the dolomitic limestone quarried in the Des Plaines valley around Lemont and Joliet beginning in the 1830s, when digging the Illinois and Michigan Canal cut straight into the bed.** It is not marble — it is a hard, cream-to-buff, magnesium-rich limestone that built Chicago's **Water Tower and Pumping Station**, which survived the 1871 fire, along with much of Lemont, Lockport and Joliet and thousands of foundations across the region. **The quarries closed generations ago**, which makes repairing original material a materials reality rather than a preference. If you own a pre-1900 building anywhere along the canal corridor, or an older Chicago house with a stone foundation, there is a good chance you own some.

## Where the Name Came From

In the 1830s the settlement that became Lemont was, for a period, called **Athens**. When the canal excavation produced enormous quantities of usable building stone, it was marketed under that name — **Athens Marble** — because "marble" sold better than "dolomite."

The stone traveled by canal boat into Chicago and became a default building material for the pre-fire city. It is the reason the Water Tower is still standing: when the 1871 fire took the wood city, the limestone landmarks came through.

Similar dolomitic limestone was quarried around **Joliet** and marketed as **Joliet limestone** or **Joliet marble**. For repair purposes the two behave essentially the same.

## How to Recognize It

- **Color**: cream, buff, pale gray, sometimes with a warm yellow cast; weathers to gray with darker biological staining.
- **Layering**: visible horizontal **bedding planes** running through the face of the stone.
- **Finish**: often rough-faced, roughly squared or coursed rubble on utilitarian work; smooth-tooled and carved on prominent buildings.
- **Where**: pre-1900 buildings in Lemont, Lockport and Joliet; foundations under Chicago **bungalows, two-flats, greystones** and worker cottages; church and institutional buildings; retaining walls and canal structures.
- **Weight and hardness**: noticeably hard for a limestone — dolomite is denser than ordinary calcium limestone.

## The Failure Mode: Delamination

Every material has a characteristic way it goes. For this stone, it is **delamination along the bedding planes**.

Limestone is sedimentary — deposited in layers, and those layers are planes of relative weakness. The stone is also **porous**, so it takes on water.

In a Chicagoland winter that absorbed water freezes inside the stone, expands roughly nine percent, and **splits it along the bedding**. The face separates and sheds in sheets or shells. Once the outer layer is gone, the exposed interior absorbs water faster than the weathered face did, and the process accelerates.

### The other failure modes

- **Spalling** at edges, noses and corners, especially on sills and steps.
- **Cracking** across a sill span or radiating from a bearing point.
- **Surface erosion** — carved and tooled profiles rounding over.
- **Displacement** — units dropping or tilting because the mortar bed beneath failed.
- **Biological staining** — dark growth on persistently damp faces.

### Where it fails first

**Horizontal surfaces**, always. Sills, water tables, steps, coping and wall caps hold water rather than shedding it. Then anything **at or below grade**, where splash-back, snow banking and road salt keep the stone saturated for months.

## Telling the Regional Stones Apart

Chicagoland buildings use several limestones, and knowing which one you have changes the repair approach and the replacement options.

**Athens Marble / Lemont-Joliet dolomite.** Cream to buff, sometimes warm yellow, hard and dense for a limestone, with pronounced horizontal bedding. Usually rough-faced or roughly squared on utilitarian work. **Pre-1900**, concentrated along the canal corridor and in older Chicago foundations. **Quarries closed** — no new material.

**Indiana limestone (Bedford).** Pale gray to buff, notably uniform and fine-grained, with far less visible bedding than the local dolomite. Takes crisp carving, which is why it dominates ornamental work — sills, lintels, water tables, cornices and full facades on early-20th-century buildings. **Still quarried**, which makes it the standard substitute for dutchman inserts and replacement units.

**Chicago greystone facades.** Most of the celebrated greystone rowhouses use **Bedford limestone** on the front elevation, over common brick behind. So a greystone front and a canal-era foundation in the same building are two different stones with two different behaviors.

**Cast stone.** Manufactured concrete cast to imitate carved stone, common from the early 1900s onward. Weathers like concrete, not stone, and can spall from internal reinforcing steel corroding.

| | Athens Marble | Indiana limestone | Cast stone |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Look** | Cream/buff, visible bedding, often rough-faced | Uniform pale gray-buff, fine grain | Molded, sometimes with aggregate visible |
| **Era here** | Pre-1900 | 1890s onward | 1900s onward |
| **Typical use** | Foundations, walls, canal-town buildings | Sills, trim, ornament, greystone facades | Trim and ornament imitating stone |
| **Fails by** | Delamination along bedding | Spalling, erosion, sugaring | Surface erosion, rebar corrosion |
| **Available new?** | No | Yes | Yes |

**The practical consequence:** if your building has Athens Marble, repair beats replacement by a wide margin because there is nothing to replace it with. If it has Bedford, a matched replacement unit is genuinely obtainable — but repair is usually still cheaper and always preserves more of the original.

## The Mortar Rule That Governs Everything

Here is the point that decides whether a repair preserves or destroys this stone.

**A masonry wall is designed so the mortar is softer than the units it holds.** Mortar is the sacrificial layer — it absorbs movement and moisture stress and wears out first, because replacing mortar is cheap and replacing 1840s quarried stone is, given closed quarries, close to impossible.

Canal-era walls were laid in **soft, lime-based mortar**. Repoint them with hard modern **Portland mortar** and the relationship inverts: the joints become the strongest element, and every freeze-thaw cycle drives stress into the stone faces instead. On layered dolomite that means **accelerated delamination** — the repair destroying the material it was meant to protect, typically visible within three to eight winters.

| | Correct for historic stone | Wrong for historic stone |
|---|---|---|
| **Mortar** | Lime-rich, soft, breathable | Portland-heavy, hard, dense |
| **Joint prep** | Hand-raked, shallow tools | Aggressive power grinding |
| **Cleaning** | Low pressure, soft brush, masonry-safe cleaners | Sandblasting, high-pressure washing |
| **Protection** | Vapor-permeable sealer, or nothing | Film-forming coating, paint |
| **Repair approach** | Patch, dutchman, repin | Wholesale replacement |

## The Repair Hierarchy

Least invasive first — because with irreplaceable stone, that ordering is not aesthetic conservatism, it is arithmetic.

1. **Repointing.** Restoring joints with matched lime mortar. Stone sound, joints failed.
2. **Matched patching.** A cementitious patch rebuilding lost material where spalling is localized.
3. **Dutchman repair.** Cutting out the damaged section cleanly and fitting a matched new stone piece into the original unit. This is the workhorse of stone restoration.
4. **Repinning and resetting.** Removing, rebedding and anchoring a shifted unit with stainless pins.
5. **Replacement.** A new matched unit — last resort, and usually **Indiana limestone** as the closest available substitute since the original quarries are gone.

## How the Work Is Done

1. **Assessment and documentation.** Walk the building, photograph conditions, evaluate the existing mortar's hardness and composition, flag prior inappropriate repairs.
2. **Correct the water path first.** Failed joints, missing caulking, bad flashing, clogged gutters, downspouts discharging onto stone, grade. Repairing stone while water keeps arriving is a repair with a countdown.
3. **Mortar analysis and matching.** A lime-based mix formulated to the original — soft enough to protect the stone, durable enough to serve.
4. **Hand joint preparation.** Raked by hand where the stone is soft; power tools only where the masonry can safely take them.
5. **Undo bad repairs** where hard mortar must come out — carefully, without taking stone with it.
6. **Repoint in lifts**, packed and compacted, tooled to the original joint profile.
7. **Repair units** — patching, dutchman, repinning — chosen unit by unit rather than applied across the board.
8. **Gentle cleaning and breathable protection**, if any.

## What Never to Do to This Stone

**Sandblasting.** It strips the weathered outer surface, exposes the porous interior, and dramatically accelerates delamination. On carved detail it is simply destruction.

**High-pressure washing.** Same damage delivered by water, plus it drives moisture deep into the wall.

**Hard Portland repointing.** Covered above, and the most common of these mistakes because it looks like ordinary competent masonry work.

**Painting or film-forming sealers.** Porous stone must dry outward; trap the moisture and it spalls the face off from behind.

**Wholesale replacement when repair was possible.** Every original unit removed is gone permanently, and the replacement is a substitute stone.

## Cost Drivers

- **Number of units** and whether repair or replacement is required per unit.
- **Repair method** — patching is less involved than a dutchman; a dutchman less than replacement.
- **Height and access** — upper elevations, church towers and bluff sites need real staging.
- **Custom lime mortar** blending and matching.
- **Undoing prior hard-mortar repairs**, often the largest variable on an older building.
- **Carved and ornamental work**, which is slower and more skilled.

## Chicagoland Context

The climate is why this matters so much here. **Dozens of freeze-thaw crossings each winter**, with no lake moderation across most of the region — and it is the crossings, not the depth of cold, that break stone. Add lake-effect moisture keeping north elevations damp, road salt at grade attacking the lowest and wettest courses, and mature canopy over the historic districts slowing drying.

A stone that would last for centuries in a mild dry climate is, in the Des Plaines valley, on a clock — and the clock runs faster every time somebody points it with the wrong mortar.

## Maintenance

- **Keep water off it** — gutters clear, downspouts extended, grade sloping away, splash controlled.
- **Repoint on schedule** with a lime-based matched mix, before delamination starts.
- **Repair sills, coping and horizontal surfaces first**; they soak everything below.
- **Inspect annually**, especially at grade, on north elevations, and under gutter runs.
- **Never blast, pressure wash or paint** it.
- **Watch for a band of new damage** matching a recent repointing — the signature of a mortar mismatch.
