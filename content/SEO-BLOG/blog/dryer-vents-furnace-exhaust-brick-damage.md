## The Halo Around Your Vent

**Go outside and look at the brick immediately around your dryer vent, your furnace exhaust, and your bathroom fan outlet.** If it is spalled, crusted white, or crumbling while the wall two feet away is perfectly sound, you are looking at damage you have been causing on purpose for years — by venting warm, wet air directly at porous masonry.

It is one of the most consistent patterns in residential masonry: **a distinct halo of deterioration centered on a penetration**, sharply different from the wall around it. Once you know to look for it, you see it on half the block.

## Why Vents Damage Masonry

Brick and mortar are **porous**. They absorb water, hold it, and release it slowly. A masonry wall survives Chicagoland winters by drying out faster than it wets. Anything that tips that ratio causes damage.

A vent tips it dramatically, in three ways.

### 1. Moisture volume
A clothes dryer removes several pounds of water from a load of laundry and discharges nearly all of it as vapor. Some clears the wall. A great deal of it condenses on and around the hood, on the masonry right at the termination.

**That brick is wet for a large share of every week, year-round.** Nothing else on your elevation gets that treatment.

### 2. Warm, wet, then freezing
Warm moist air condenses on cold brick — the same reason a window fogs. It soaks in, and then the outdoor temperature drops. In Chicagoland the temperature crosses the freezing point dozens of times each winter, and each crossing is a load cycle on saturated masonry.

**Water expands roughly nine percent when it freezes.** Inside a saturated brick, that is enough to break the face off.

### 3. Chemistry, on the furnace side
This is the one people do not expect.

**A high-efficiency condensing furnace vents cool, wet flue gas out a sidewall**, usually through a PVC pipe low on the wall. That is how the appliance achieves its efficiency — it extracts so much heat that the exhaust condenses. And **that condensate is mildly acidic.**

Aim it at lime-based mortar and soft brick for several heating seasons and it etches the joints and deteriorates the faces. The damage pattern is distinctive: a **plume shape** spreading out and usually downward from the termination, exactly following where the discharge lands.

## Which Vents Cause the Most Trouble

| Vent type | What it discharges | Typical damage |
|---|---|---|
| **Clothes dryer** | Large volume of warm, humid air; lint | Spalling and efflorescence halo; joint erosion |
| **High-efficiency furnace / boiler sidewall** | Cool, wet, **mildly acidic** flue gas | Plume-shaped etching and face loss; worst of the group |
| **Bathroom exhaust fan** | Warm, humid air, intermittent | Milder halo; efflorescence common |
| **Kitchen range hood** | Warm, humid, greasy air | Staining plus moisture damage; grease complicates cleaning |
| **Water heater / older atmospheric flue** | Warm flue gas, usually via chimney | Damage shows in the chimney, not the wall |

**The high-efficiency furnace sidewall vent is the aggressive one**, because it combines moisture, cold discharge and acidity. It is also increasingly common, since condensing equipment replaced chimney-vented furnaces across Chicagoland over the past two decades — which is why this damage pattern is far more widespread now than it was a generation ago.

## The Warning Signs

### Spalling in a halo
Brick faces flaking, shelling or popping off in a rough circle or plume centered on the vent.

### Efflorescence
White powder or crust on the brick and joints around the penetration. Not damaging by itself — but proof that water is moving through that masonry in quantity.

### Eroded, sandy mortar joints
Joints around the vent noticeably softer, more receded, or rakeable with a key when joints elsewhere on the wall are firm.

### Staining and streaking
Discoloration running down the wall below the vent, sometimes with a greasy component from a kitchen hood.

### A gap around the penetration
Failed or missing sealant where the vent pipe or hood meets the masonry — a direct opening into the wall.

### Ice in winter
Ice forming on and below the hood during cold snaps means condensation is happening right there, on your brick, repeatedly.

### Interior clues
Damp, staining or efflorescence on the inside face of the same wall, or in the basement below a low sidewall vent.

## Why the Location Makes It Worse

A vent penetration is not just a wet spot. **It is a hole through your wall's drainage plane** — through the veneer, through whatever weather barrier is behind it, through the sheathing.

On a properly built wall that penetration was flashed and sealed so water reaching it gets directed back out. On many houses it was drilled through and caulked, and the caulk is now fifteen or twenty years old.

So the vent area is simultaneously **the wettest part of the elevation** and **the part with the most compromised water management.** That combination is why the damage concentrates there rather than spreading evenly.

## How the Repair Actually Works

Repairing the brick without addressing the discharge just resets the clock. A complete repair has two halves.

### Fix the cause
1. **Assess the discharge** — where the plume actually lands on the masonry.
2. **Extend or redirect the termination** where possible. A hood that projects further from the wall, or a termination angled and located to throw discharge clear, makes an enormous difference. This is usually HVAC work, and it is worth coordinating.
3. **Clean the duct**, especially on dryers. A restricted duct runs longer and hotter, increasing both moisture output and fire risk.
4. **Confirm the appliance is vented properly** — terminating into a soffit, a gangway that never dries, or too close to grade all concentrate the problem.

### Fix the masonry
5. **Cut out and replace spalled units** with matched or salvaged brick, removed through the joints so the neighboring brick are not damaged.
6. **Repoint the eroded joints**, cut back to sound mortar and refilled with a mix matched to the brick — softer than the units, never harder.
7. **Properly flash and seal the penetration** with backer rod and an appropriate sealant, in a joint that can move, rather than a smear of caulk over the gap.
8. **Restore the drainage path** so anything getting behind the veneer at that point can get back out.
9. **Cure and clean down** before mortar hardens on the faces.

## Materials and Choices That Matter

**Mortar type.** Match the existing and keep it softer than the brick. On pre-1930 Chicago common brick that usually means a soft, lime-rich mix or **Type O**; on mid-century and newer brick, **Type N**. Portland-heavy mortar on soft brick spalls the faces within a few winters — the exact damage you are repairing.

**Brick matching.** Vent halos are small areas, which makes matching both easier and more visible. Salvaged brick of the right size, color and texture beats new brick that is close but wrong.

**Sealant.** Polyurethane or hybrid at the penetration perimeter, over backer rod, tooled properly. Not a bead of silicone smeared across the brick face.

**No coatings.** Do not seal a vent halo with a film-forming waterproofer. You will trap the moisture the vent keeps delivering, which is worse than doing nothing.

## What Drives the Cost

- **How many units** need replacing — usually a dozen or two, sometimes more on a furnace plume.
- **How much repointing** surrounds the replacement area.
- **Access height** — a first-floor dryer vent versus a second-floor bathroom outlet.
- **Brick matching difficulty** on older or unusual units.
- **Whether the penetration needs reflashing** rather than just resealing.
- **Coordination with HVAC** if the termination is being modified.

This is generally one of the more contained masonry repairs, because the damage is localized by definition. **Left alone, it stops being contained** — the halo grows outward every season, and once water is consistently getting behind the veneer at that point, the scope changes.

## DIY vs. Hiring a Pro

Cleaning your dryer duct is genuinely a homeowner job and worth doing annually.

The masonry is not. Cutting out brick without damaging the surrounding units takes practice, and the mortar decision — matching hardness, color, sand and profile — is where most DIY and handyman repairs go wrong. **A vent halo repointed in hard bagged mortar looks fixed for two years and then spalls a wider circle than the original problem.**

There is also a diagnostic component. The same symptoms can come from a failed lintel above, a leaking gutter, or a sill throwing water at the wall, and a vent nearby makes it easy to blame the wrong thing.

## Chicagoland Context

Three local factors make this a bigger problem here than in milder climates.

**Freeze-thaw frequency.** Chicagoland crosses the freezing point dozens of times each winter. Every crossing is a stress cycle on saturated masonry, and vent-area brick is the most consistently saturated masonry on the building.

**The housing stock.** Bungalows, two-flats, Georgians and post-war ranches across Chicago and the suburbs were built with soft brick and lime mortar and were never designed around sidewall appliance venting — because sidewall appliance venting did not exist yet. Every one of those terminations is a retrofit through a wall that did not anticipate it.

**The furnace conversion wave.** The move to high-efficiency condensing equipment put an acidic sidewall exhaust on an enormous number of Chicagoland brick homes over the last twenty-odd years. The damage takes several seasons to become obvious, which is exactly the point many of those installations are at now.

## Prevention and Maintenance

- **Look at every vent penetration once a year**, ideally in early spring when winter damage is freshest.
- **Clean the dryer duct annually** — shorter cycles, less moisture, less fire risk.
- **Keep the sealant at penetrations current.** It is a few dollars of material protecting a wall.
- **Watch for ice** forming at a termination in winter; it is the clearest signal that condensation is landing on your masonry.
- **When you replace an appliance**, ask where the new termination will go and whether it can throw discharge clear of the wall. It is far easier to decide before the hole is cut.
- **Never coat the area** with a film-forming sealer.
