## The short answer

**A property condition assessment gives masonry about one paragraph. On a Chicagoland brick building, that paragraph is where the money hides.**

A PCA is a survey-level document covering roofing, mechanicals, paving, structure, life safety and the envelope across an entire property. For most of those systems, a walk-through and an allowance are genuinely adequate. **Masonry is the exception**, because the range between "repoint two elevations" and "rebuild the parapet, replace fourteen lintels and restore flashing at every floor line" is an order of magnitude — and almost none of that difference is visible from the sidewalk.

A useful masonry section tells you the **wall assembly**, the **findings by elevation with quantities**, an explicit split between **immediate repairs and reserve items**, and a direct statement about **parapets, lintels, shelf angles and flashing**. Anything less cannot be priced, cannot be negotiated, and cannot be budgeted.

## Why masonry breaks the PCA format

Three reasons, and they compound.

**One: the expensive failures are concealed.** The brick face is the part everyone photographs and the part that matters least. What drives cost is the **steel and the flashing inside the wall** — shelf angles at floor lines, lintels over openings, through-wall flashing, wall ties. None of it is visible, and all of it is expensive.

**Two: masonry deteriorates in steps.** Roofing degrades on a fairly smooth curve, which is why remaining-useful-life tables work for it. Masonry sits stable for years and then crosses a threshold — a coping joint opens, the parapet core fills with water, one winter later it needs rebuilding rather than repointing. A single line saying "façade in fair condition, monitor" tells you nothing about which side of that threshold you are on.

**Three: the same photograph supports two very different numbers.** Spalled brick at a base course could be splash and salt on an otherwise sound wall, or it could be the visible edge of a wall that has been saturated for a decade because the weeps were mortared shut. Same picture. Very different cap-ex.

## What the masonry section must contain

Hand this list to whoever is writing your report.

### 1. The wall assembly, named

Not "brick façade." Specifically:

- **Solid masonry** — multiple wythes, common on Chicago building stock built before roughly 1950. The wall's mass is the weather barrier.
- **Brick veneer over CMU or steel stud** — a single four-inch skin over a backup, standard from the 1960s onward. A **drained cavity**, where water is expected to pass the face and exit via flashing and weep holes.
- **Precast, panelized or curtain wall** — a different trade conversation entirely.

This one determination changes everything downstream. On solid masonry, eroded joints and top-of-wall details dominate. On veneer, **the drainage path and the concealed steel dominate**, and a report that recommends repointing without ever mentioning weeps or shelf angles has assessed the wrong building.

### 2. Findings by elevation, with quantities

"The building requires tuckpointing" is not a finding. This is:

> *North elevation: approximately 1,800 sf of joints receded 1/4"+ behind the brick face; 40–60 spalled units; 3 lintels with active rust staining and horizontal cracking above. West elevation: approximately 600 sf; base course spalling 0–3 ft. South and east: sound, monitor.*

Quantities are what convert a report into a price, and a price into a negotiating position.

### 3. Immediate vs. reserve, split explicitly

| Category | Definition | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Immediate** | Safety, active water entry, or condition worsening within a season | Loose parapet units over an entrance, failed coping, corroded lintel with displaced brick |
| **Short-term (1–3 yr)** | Failing now, cost climbing | Weather-elevation repointing, sealant replacement, base-course units |
| **Reserve (4–10 yr)** | Sound but finite | Remaining elevations, eventual coping replacement |
| **Monitor** | Stable, re-inspect | Hairline cracking, isolated staining |

A lender's report needs this split anyway. A buyer needs it to know what is a closing negotiation and what is a budget line.

### 4. The four concealed items, addressed by name

If the report does not use these words, it did not look:

- **Parapets and coping** — the highest-risk element on any commercial building, weathered on both faces and the top, and invisible from the ground
- **Shelf angles** — steel carrying the veneer at floor lines
- **Lintels** — steel over every opening
- **Through-wall flashing and weep holes** — the drainage path on any cavity wall

### 5. Access limitations, disclosed

If the assessment was ground-level only, it should say so and say what that means. "Rear and north elevations viewed from grade only; parapet and coping not inspected" is an honest report. Silence on access is how a buyer ends up believing a parapet was evaluated when nobody went up.

### 6. Dated photographs keyed to locations

Not a gallery. Photographs tied to elevation and location, dated — which also becomes your baseline record if a dispute arises later.

## The warning signs that change the number

### Horizontal cracking at a consistent height

The most consequential finding in commercial masonry due diligence, and the most frequently misread. A crack running **level** across an elevation at one height is a **corroding shelf angle**. Rust occupies several times the volume of the steel it came from, so the expansion lifts the courses above it.

It is routinely written up as settlement. **Settlement does not draw a level line across an elevation.** Repairing it means opening the wall, replacing steel and restoring flashing — a different order of magnitude from repointing.

### Rust staining above openings

Orange-brown streaks weeping down from the joint above a window or storefront. The early warning of lintel corrosion, and it commonly precedes visible cracking by years. Count them; each one is a line item.

### Receded mortar joints

Sight along the wall at a low angle. Joints set back a quarter inch or more behind the face have lost real section. Push a key into one — sound mortar resists, failed mortar crumbles.

### Spalling brick

Faces flaking or popping off. Concentrated at base courses (splash, snow, de-icing salt) and anywhere water sits. On soft older brick it is also the signature of **a previous hard-mortar repointing**, which is its own finding: it means the wall has already been damaged by a bad repair and the next one has to undo it.

### Efflorescence

White mineral bloom. Proof that water is actively moving through the wall — a symptom pointing at a source, not a cosmetic issue.

### Bowing or bulging veneer

Sight up the wall from a corner. Any outward deflection means **wall ties** have failed. That is a rebuild condition, not a repointing condition, and it belongs in the immediate column.

### Open coping joints

Only visible from the roof. The coping is the roof of the parapet; open joints let water into the wall core where it freezes and separates the wythes from inside. This single condition determines whether a parapet is repointable or has to come down.

## The building science a buyer should understand

**Brick is not waterproof and never was.** Brick and mortar are porous and absorb water; wind-driven rain pushes moisture through the face. A wall stays healthy by **drying** between wettings.

Chicagoland is hostile to that arrangement. We run through dozens of freeze-thaw cycles in a typical winter — repeated crossings of 32°F rather than one long freeze — and each one expands water held inside saturated masonry. **Wet masonry plus repeated freezing is the entire mechanism** of deterioration in this market. Add lake-effect moisture and heavy de-icing salt at entrances and loading docks, and deferral is punished harder here than in most regions.

**Mortar is the sacrificial element**, and it must always be **softer than the unit it holds**. This matters in due diligence because a prior repointing done in hard Portland-heavy mortar on soft pre-1930s brick has actively damaged the building — the joint is now stronger than the brick, so stress goes through the brick face instead. A report noting "recently tuckpointed" as a positive without commenting on mortar compatibility has missed something material.

## Chicago-specific: the compliance overlay

In the City of Chicago, deferred façade condition is not purely an economic question. The Department of Buildings enforces exterior wall conditions, and buildings above certain heights and construction types carry **periodic exterior wall examination** obligations on cycles the Department sets.

For a buyer that means two due-diligence items beyond the physical assessment:

1. **Confirm with the Department of Buildings what applies to the specific address**, and whether examinations are current.
2. **Check for open violations or prior citations**, because an inherited enforcement matter arrives with a clock already running.

Requirements turn on the individual building and they change — verify rather than assume.

## Turning findings into a position

The negotiation runs on quantities, not adjectives.

1. **Get quantities by elevation** in the assessment.
2. **Get unit-price ranges** attached to them, so the total is defensible.
3. **Separate immediate from reserve** — sellers argue about reserve items and rarely win on immediate safety items.
4. **Price the concealed risk explicitly.** Where shelf angles or flashing cannot be confirmed without opening the wall, that uncertainty is itself a negotiating item: either a holdback, a credit, or an exploratory opening before closing.
5. **Ask for prior repair records.** A wall repaired twice in the same place has an uncorrected water source, and that is worth knowing before you own it.

## How the repair actually proceeds, so you can sanity-check a number

1. Assess and document by elevation; resolve **access and staging**, often the largest single cost driver
2. **Correct the water source** — coping, flashing, weeps, sealant, roof drainage
3. **Cut failed joints to depth**, roughly two and a half times the joint width, never skimming over failed material
4. **Repoint with matched mortar** — lime-rich on soft historic units, **Type N** for most commercial veneer, **Type S** only where exposure or structure warrants it
5. **Match color, texture and profile** against a **cured** sample panel
6. **Replace failed units** matched by size, color, texture and era
7. **Address the steel** — clean and coat where section is sound, replace where it is not, with flashing and weeps restored as the masonry goes back
8. **Cure and protect**; seal only afterward, and only with a **breathable penetrating** product, never a film-forming coating

On cleaning: the **gentlest method that works**. **No sandblasting or high-pressure washing** — both permanently strip the weathered skin from limestone, terra cotta and soft brick.

## What drives the cost

**Height, access and staging.** **Overhead protection** over public walks and occupied entrances. **Proportion of stone and terra cotta to brick.** **How much concealed steel** is in the scope. **Occupancy** and off-hours restrictions. **Permit and public-way timelines.** And **hidden conditions** — which is why any bid you take on the work should carry **unit prices** so discovered scope has a number before it is discovered.

## Why not just have the engineer do it

Both have a role. **A structural engineer** is the right call for movement, bearing, and anything load-related, and a lender's report may require a licensed professional. **A restoration mason** is who can tell you what the repair physically involves, what quantities are really there, and what it costs to execute — because they price and perform that work every week.

The failure mode we see most is a report that is structurally competent and commercially useless: correct that the façade shows deterioration, silent on how much, where, and what it takes to fix.
