The front of the building, with its face brick, limestone sills and decorative trim, is the best-built wall on the property. The masonry that fails first is usually the **common-brick side walls** in the gangway, the **rear wall** behind the back porches, the **parapet** along the roofline, and the **chimney**. **Emerald Masonry LLC** repairs two-flats across Chicago and the inner suburbs, wall by wall. The two-flat is Chicago's signature small apartment building: two stacked units, a garden or basement level, a flat roof, front and back stairs, and solid brick walls. Many were built between about 1890 and 1930 and are now well past a century old. A great many are owner-occupied, with the owner living in one unit and renting the other. That makes masonry decisions harder than on a single-family home. Water from a failed parapet leaks into your tenant's ceiling, not yours. Scaffolding blocks a shared gangway. And the bill has to be weighed against rent, not just resale value.

> A Chicago two-flat is really four masonry buildings in one: a show front, two plain side walls, a working rear wall and a roofline nobody looks at. Each fails differently.

This guide walks around the building, wall by wall, explaining how each part was built, where it fails, how it should be repaired and what to do first.

## How a Chicago two-flat is built

### Solid masonry, not veneer

A pre-war two-flat is **solid masonry**. The exterior walls are two, three or more wythes of brick bonded together, carrying the floors and the roof. There is no air space and no flashing system behind the brick as there is on a later brick veneer house. Water that gets into the wall has to **dry out through the mortar joints and the brick faces**. That is why the mortar on a two-flat matters more than almost anything else, and why the wrong mortar does so much damage.

### Face brick in front, common brick everywhere else

Builders saved money by putting the expensive, hard-fired, uniform **face brick** only where it showed: the street front, and sometimes the first few feet of each side wall. Everywhere else they used **Chicago common brick**, a softer, more porous, less uniform brick made from local clay. Common brick is perfectly serviceable when protected by soft lime mortar and kept reasonably dry. It deteriorates quickly when it stays wet or is repointed with hard mortar.

| | Face brick (street front) | Common brick (sides and rear) |
|---|---|---|
| **Firing** | Hard-fired, dense | Softer, more variable |
| **Water absorption** | Lower | Higher |
| **Appearance** | Uniform colour and size | Salmon to red, irregular |
| **Typical failure** | Mortar erosion, lintel cracking | Spalling, crumbling faces, eroded joints |
| **Right mortar** | Matched Type N or lime-rich | Soft lime-rich; never harder than the brick |

### Limestone, steel and a flat roof

The front usually carries **limestone**: sills, lintels, a water table, sometimes a full stone bay. Window and door openings on the sides and back are spanned by **steel lintels** or brick arches. The roof is **flat**, hidden behind a brick **parapet** with a stone or terra-cotta **coping** cap. Chimneys carry flues from the original coal or gas heating, and many are no longer in use.

## Wall by wall: where two-flat masonry fails

### The front façade

The front is usually the soundest wall, but it has its own weak points:

- **Limestone sills** with open end joints, lost slope or cracked stone.
- **Steel lintels** over windows rusting and lifting the brick above, showing as horizontal cracks and stepped cracks from the corners. See our guide to [lintel rust stains and orange streaks](/blog/lintel-rust-stains-orange-streaks-brick).
- **Front stairs and stoop** masonry pulling away from the building.
- **Old, mismatched patches** where earlier tuckpointing used grey Portland mortar on buff or red joints.

The front usually needs **spot repointing**, sill and lintel repairs, and careful matching. It rarely needs full tuckpointing at the same time as the sides.

### The side walls and gangway

This is where we find the most damage. Side walls are tall, plain, built in common brick and often face a narrow **gangway** a few feet from the next building. They get little sun, dry slowly and collect water from roof runoff and neighbouring downspouts.

**Warning signs:**

- **Crumbling, sandy mortar joints** recessed well behind the brick face.
- **Spalling brick** with faces flaked off, often worst in the lower courses and near downspouts.
- **White efflorescence** in bands and patches.
- **Step cracks** following the joints, especially at window openings.
- **Bowing or bulging** areas where the outer wythe has separated. That needs prompt attention. Read [why brick walls bow and bulge](/blog/why-brick-walls-bow-and-bulge).

Side walls usually need **full tuckpointing** of the worst elevation, plus brick replacement where faces have spalled. Access is the main cost driver. Gangway work often needs narrow staging and sometimes an access agreement with the neighbour.

### The rear wall and back porches

The rear wall of a two-flat is a working wall. It carries the **back porches**, often wood or steel frames bolted into the brick at each floor. It takes water off the porch decks and the rear stairs, and it is where kitchen and bathroom vents, gas lines and cable runs were drilled through the brick over the decades.

**Warning signs:**

- **Cracked or loose brick around porch ledger bolts** and anchors.
- **Rust streaks** below porch connections.
- **Open joints and holes** around old penetrations.
- **Saturated brick** where porch decks drain against the wall.

Porch rebuilding and rear-wall masonry should be **planned together**. If a porch is coming down for replacement, that is the cheapest moment to repair the brick behind it. Our guide to [anchoring into brick](/blog/anchoring-into-brick-awnings-handrails-signs) covers how attachments should be set.

### The roofline: parapet, coping and chimney

The parapet is exposed on both faces and the top, so it fails years before the walls below. Cracked coping joints, eroded mortar and failed flashing at the roof are the most common cause of **water in the upper unit's ceilings and top-floor walls**. We cover this in detail in [your two-flat has a parapet](/blog/two-flat-parapet-roofline-masonry-chicago-il).

Chimneys on two-flats are often **abandoned flues** from original heating. With no warm flue gases to dry them, unused chimneys stay wet and deteriorate fast. Options range from repointing and a new crown, to capping, to taking an unused chimney down below the roofline. See [should I remove my chimney instead of repairing it](/blog/should-i-remove-my-chimney-instead-of-repairing).

### The foundation and garden unit

Many two-flats have a **garden unit** or finished basement. Foundation walls may be brick, stone or concrete, and water problems there are often a mix of masonry and drainage. Before anyone parges or waterproofs, check gutters, downspouts and grade. Our guide to [basement water: foundation masonry or drainage](/blog/basement-water-foundation-masonry-or-drainage) explains how to tell.

## What to fix first on a two-flat

Most owners cannot or should not do everything at once. A sensible order:

1. **Anything loose or overhead.** Loose parapet bricks, coping, chimney tops or bowing wall areas are a safety issue for people below.
2. **Roofline water.** Parapet, coping and chimney repairs, because they leak into the building and damage the walls below.
3. **The worst side wall**, usually the one facing the weather or the wettest gangway.
4. **The rear wall**, timed with porch work if possible.
5. **Front façade details**: sills, lintels and spot repointing.
6. **The remaining walls** as joints reach the point of needing repointing.

A good scope is written **by wall and by item**, so each phase can be priced, approved and scheduled separately. Our guide to [prioritising masonry repairs on a budget](/blog/how-to-prioritize-masonry-repairs-on-a-budget-chicagoland-il) goes further.

## How a professional repairs a two-flat

1. **Assessment.** A mason walks every elevation, the roof and the parapet, records joint depth, brick condition, lintels and sills, and photographs problems.
2. **Written scope by wall.** Quantities and methods for each elevation, with options.
3. **Mortar matching.** Colour, sand, hardness and joint profile are recorded, and cured sample patches are made for approval.
4. **Access set-up.** Staging, ladders or lifts sized for the gangway, roof protection, and coordination with your tenant.
5. **Cutting out joints** to at least twice the joint width, by hand or with grinders used carefully so brick edges are not chipped.
6. **Brick replacement** of spalled and cracked units with salvaged or matched brick, toothed in.
7. **Repointing** in layers with lime-rich or Type N mortar, tooled to the original profile.
8. **Lintel, sill and coping work** as scoped.
9. **Cleaning and curing**, protecting fresh mortar from sun, wind and frost.
10. **Walk-through** and a written record of what was done, which helps at resale and refinance.

## Materials and techniques

- **Lime-rich mortar or Type N**, matched to the original. Never Type S or straight Portland on soft common brick. See [lime vs Portland mortar](/blog/lime-vs-portland-mortar).
- **Salvaged Chicago common brick** for side and rear wall replacement, because new brick rarely matches its colour or softness. See [salvaged brick sourcing](/blog/salvaged-brick-sourcing-matching-chicago).
- **Mortar colour matching** by sand and pigment, checked on cured samples.
- **Galvanised or stainless steel** for replacement lintels, primed and flashed where the wall allows.
- **Breathable water repellents only**, and only where needed. Film-forming sealers trap water in solid masonry.

## DIY vs hiring a pro on a two-flat

Small jobs, like clearing a gutter or caulking a window perimeter, are fine for an owner. Masonry repair on a two-flat is not. Wall heights mean ladder and staging work two and three storeys up. Mortar on old common brick has to be softer than the brick, and a hardware-store mix is usually too hard. Cutting out joints badly widens them and chips brick edges for good. ## Chicago context: why two-flats age the way they do

Chicago sees dozens of **freeze-thaw cycles** a year, and lake-influenced moisture keeps walls wet through spring and fall. Two-flats line tight city lots in neighbourhoods from Brighton Park, McKinley Park and Bridgeport to Logan Square, Avondale, Pilsen and the Southwest Side, and into Cicero, Berwyn and Oak Park. Many had their first tuckpointing in the mid-twentieth century with hard Portland mortar, which has since damaged soft common brick. Neighbouring buildings a few feet away shade side walls and dump water into gangways. And because the roof is hidden behind a parapet, the most exposed masonry on the building goes uninspected for years.

Brick **greystones** share the same layout with a limestone front; their stone façade has its own repair needs, covered in our [greystone façade restoration](/blog/greystone-facade-restoration-chicago) guide.

## Maintenance and prevention

- **Walk the building every spring and fall.** Look at each wall from the gangway and the alley; use binoculars on the parapet and chimney.
- **Get on the roof once a year**, safely or with a roofer, to check coping joints and parapet flashing.
- **Keep gutters and downspouts working**, and direct downspouts away from gangway walls.
- **Seal penetrations** in the rear wall when vents or lines are removed.
- **Watch for interior signs**: stains on top-floor ceilings and walls, peeling plaster at exterior walls. See [water coming through a brick wall: the causes](/blog/water-coming-through-brick-wall-causes).
- **Don't paint or seal brick** with film-forming coatings.

On a Chicago two-flat, the front is usually the last wall you need to worry about.
