## The Only Ranking That Matters

On a rental, masonry competes for dollars with the roof, the furnace, the turnover and the vacancy. So the useful question is not "what is wrong with this building." It is:

**Which masonry problems get dramatically more expensive if I wait, and which genuinely hold for a few more years?**

## Priority 1: Anything at the Top

**Water enters at the top and travels down.** Every masonry defect below a top-of-building failure is being actively fed by it, which is why repairs lower on the wall do not hold while the top is still open.

This tier includes:

- **Parapet walls and coping** on flat-roof buildings — exposed on two faces with no heated interior moderating them, and the single most common source of water in a multi-unit brick building.
- **Chimney crowns** — a cracked crown funnels water straight into the stack.
- **Roof-to-wall and counter flashing.**
- **Scuppers, gutters and downspouts** that are blocked, disconnected or discharging against the building.

**Cost curve: steep.** A parapet that needs resetting coping this year needs rebuilding in a few. And a top-of-building leak is the one most likely to become an interior claim, which is where masonry money turns into drywall, paint, flooring and a rent abatement.

## Priority 2: Corroding Steel

**Rusting steel is the fastest-compounding problem in masonry**, and on rental stock it is everywhere: lintels over every window and door, relieving and shelf angles on veneer buildings, and steel in porch and stair structures.

Steel corrodes when it gets wet. **Rust occupies several times the volume of the metal it replaced**, and inside a rigid brick wall there is nowhere for that expansion to go, so it lifts the masonry above it. That is rust jacking, and it is why a steel problem announces itself as a horizontal crack in a mortar joint.

The visible warning is **rust staining running down from an opening** — which means the steel has already been corroding for a while.

**Cost curve: steep and accelerating.** Caught early, it is one lintel replaced and a few courses rebuilt. Caught late, it is the lintel plus everything it displaced, and on a multi-unit building with twenty identical openings that multiplies fast.

## Priority 3: Open Joints and Failed Sealant

Deteriorated mortar joints and split sealant are how water gets into the field of the wall.

- Joints **receded behind the brick face**, or raking out as sand.
- **Split or missing sealant** at window perimeters and control joints.
- Joints around **sills, lintels and penetrations**.

**Cost curve: moderate, with a cliff.** Repointing is comparatively cheap per square foot and stays cheap — until faces start spalling off the brick. Once units are failing, you are in replacement, which is several times the cost. **Spalling is the threshold to watch.** Everything before it is manageable.

## Priority 4: Grade-Level Damage

Splash-back, snow banked against the wall, road salt and de-icer, and soil or mulch piled against the brick all concentrate in the bottom courses.

**Cost curve: moderate, and mostly correctable for free.** Extending downspouts, pulling mulch back off the wall and keeping shoveled snow off the building costs nothing and slows the whole process. The masonry repair itself is comparatively cheap because it is at grade and needs no staging — until it reaches the foundation, at which point excavation makes it one of the more expensive repairs on the property.

## Priority 5: Genuinely Cosmetic

- **Efflorescence** — the white mineral bloom.
- **Mortar color mismatch** from an old repair.
- **Staining, algae and general grime.**
- **Minor variation** in a previous repair's joint profile.

**Cost curve: flat.** These do not get worse in a way that costs more later.

**One caveat: efflorescence is cosmetic but it is not meaningless.** It is proof that water is actively moving through that masonry, so trace it even when you defer the appearance.

## The Priority Table

| Condition | Priority | Cost curve if deferred |
|---|---|---|
| Parapet, coping, chimney crown, flashing | **1 — now** | Steep; risks interior claims |
| Corroding lintels, shelf angles | **2 — now** | Steep and accelerating |
| Open mortar joints, failed sealant | **3 — this season or next** | Moderate, cliff at spalling |
| Grade-level damage, drainage | **4 — plan it** | Moderate; steep if it reaches the foundation |
| Efflorescence, staining, color mismatch | **5 — defer** | Flat |
| Bowing, bulging, displaced walls | **Assess immediately** | Not a maintenance item |

**Bowing, bulging or visibly displaced masonry sits outside this ranking.** That is a structural condition, not a maintenance one, and it needs proper assessment on its own timeline.

## The Case for Scoping by Elevation

Ask for the scope **broken out by elevation with quantities**, not as a single building number. Three reasons this matters more on rental property than on an owner-occupied house.

**Elevations deteriorate at different rates.** South and west walls take the sun, thermal cycling and driving rain, and are typically years ahead of north and east. Pricing the building as one object either overpays for two sound walls or underscopes two failing ones.

**Phasing becomes real.** An itemized scope lets you take the parapet and the lintels this year and the field walls next, in the order that actually protects the building.

**Bids become comparable.** Two contractors quoting "the building" are quoting two different jobs and you cannot tell. Two contractors quoting the same elevations with the same quantities are actually competing.

## Running It Across a Portfolio

If you hold several buildings, rank line items **across the portfolio**, not building by building. The urgent parapet on the third building should be funded ahead of the discretionary repointing on the first, and that only becomes visible when everything is scoped the same way.

A workable cycle:

1. **Scope every building the same way**, by elevation, with quantities and a priority grade per item.
2. **Merge the line items into one list** and sort by risk.
3. **Fund from the top down** until the budget runs out.
4. **Re-walk annually.** Grades move — mostly upward.
5. **Bundle by geography** where you can. Mobilization and access are real costs, and two buildings a mile apart done in the same window cost less than the same work six months apart.

## What Drives the Cost

- **Height and access** — ground and lift work versus scaffold or swing stage. On taller buildings access frequently exceeds the masonry itself.
- **Repointing-to-replacement ratio.** Replacement is the expensive part.
- **Steel involved** — each corroded lintel or angle adds shoring, removal and rebuild.
- **Parapet condition** — resetting coping is minor, rebuilding a parapet is major.
- **Mortar matching complexity** on older brick.
- **Prior bad repairs** that have to come out first.
- **Occupancy constraints** — protection over entries, walks and parking, and scheduling around tenants.
- **Water-source correction** at gutters, flashing and grade.

## Working With Tenants in Place

Nearly all of this is exterior work, staged by elevation, and rarely needs interior access. What makes it go smoothly on occupied property is mundane:

- **Notice for the elevations being worked**, so tenants can move vehicles and close windows.
- **Protection** over entries, walks, decks and parked cars.
- **Keeping egress and parking usable** throughout.
- **Predictable hours**, agreed up front.
- **Phasing by elevation** rather than surrounding the whole building at once.

## The Vacancy Cost Nobody Puts in the Masonry Budget

Masonry decisions on rental property get evaluated against the repair cost, and that leaves out the two numbers that are usually larger.

**Interior damage.** A parapet or flashing failure does not stay outside. Once water is tracking down inside a wall it reaches drywall, paint, flooring, insulation and sometimes electrical — and the interior repair on a single unit routinely exceeds the masonry repair that would have prevented it.

**Lost rent.** Water in an occupied unit means an abatement, a repair schedule you do not control, and sometimes a tenant who leaves. On a small building, one displaced unit for a couple of months can cost more than the parapet work.

**That is why the priority ranking follows the water path rather than the visible damage.** The parapet you cannot see from the sidewalk outranks the eroded joints you look at every time you pull up, because only one of them ends up inside the building.

## Chicagoland Context

Three local realities shape rental masonry here.

**The stock is old and it is masonry.** Two-flats, three-flats, courtyard buildings, bungalows and greystones across Chicago and the near suburbs were built 1890–1940 with soft brick and lime mortar. That masonry is durable but it is unforgiving of hard modern mortar, which means the mortar decision on your building matters more than it would on newer stock.

**Freeze-thaw does the damage.** Dozens of crossings of the freezing point each winter, each one a stress cycle on saturated masonry. This is why deferral math is harsher here than in milder markets — a Chicagoland winter is not a neutral year for a wall.

**Flat roofs mean parapets.** Most multi-unit stock in this market has a parapet, and parapets are the highest-risk masonry element on any building. If you own multi-unit property here and have not had the parapets looked at, that is the first call.

## DIY vs. Hiring a Pro

Some of the highest-value work on this list genuinely is not masonry: extending downspouts, clearing gutters and scuppers, pulling mulch back off the wall, keeping shoveled snow off the building. Do those.

The masonry itself is specialized, and the failure mode of getting it wrong is expensive rather than merely ugly. **Mortar harder than the brick will spall the faces off a wall within a few winters** — the most damaging error in the trade, and the most common one on budget repointing jobs. On rental property, where you may be repeating the same repair across twenty identical openings or four identical buildings, a wrong mortar decision multiplies.
