## What a Step Crack Actually Is

A step crack is a crack that follows the mortar joints of a brick wall in a diagonal, stair-stepped line rather than cutting straight through the bricks. The reason it takes that shape comes down to basic building science: **mortar is the weakest path through a masonry wall.** Brick units are hard and strong in compression, but the mortar joints between them are softer and are the first thing to give when a wall is stressed. So when something forces part of a wall to move relative to the rest, the crack travels along the line of least resistance — up one head joint, over along a bed joint, up the next head joint — producing the familiar staircase.

That's also why step cracks are usually a *symptom*, not the disease. The crack is the wall telling you that two parts of it are no longer moving together. Reading a step crack correctly means asking **why** the wall is moving, not just noticing that it cracked.

## What Causes Step Cracks in Brick Walls

Step cracks have several possible causes, and more than one can be at work on the same wall. This is the part homeowners most often get wrong — they treat the crack as the problem instead of tracing it back to what's moving the wall.

### Differential foundation settlement

The most common structural cause. All foundations settle a little, but problems start when **one part settles more than another** — differential settlement. When a corner or a section of footing drops relative to the rest, the brick above it is pulled apart diagonally, and a step crack opens along the joints. Settlement cracks are often wider at one end than the other, which is a useful diagnostic clue.

### Expansive clay soil movement

The Chicago region is notorious for **expansive clay soils** that swell when they take on water and shrink when they dry out. That seasonal swelling and shrinking lifts and drops foundations, especially where drainage is poor or where a big tree pulls moisture out of the soil on one side of the house. This soil movement is behind a large share of the step cracks we see across Cook, DuPage, and Will County homes.

### Water and poor drainage

Water is the quiet driver behind most masonry failure. Downspouts that dump against the foundation, negative grading that slopes soil *toward* the house, clogged gutters, and failed below-grade drainage all keep the soil around the footing saturated and unstable — and feed moisture straight into the mortar joints.

### Freeze-thaw cycling

Chicago winters put masonry through roughly a hundred **freeze-thaw cycles** a year. Water that gets into an open joint or crack freezes, expands about nine percent, and pries the joint a little wider — then melts and does it again. Freeze-thaw rarely starts a structural step crack on its own, but it aggressively *widens* existing cracks and deteriorates the mortar around them.

### Tree roots

Large trees near the foundation both dry out the clay soil unevenly (causing shrinkage settlement) and, in some cases, physically push against footings and buried masonry. A step crack that appears on the side of a house closest to a mature tree is a familiar pattern.

### Rusted lintels and localized overload

Not every step crack is about the foundation. A **rusted steel lintel** over a window or door expands as it corrodes (rust jacking), lifting the brick above it and cracking the surrounding joints — often producing cracks that step *out and up from the corners of an opening* rather than rising from ground level. Overloaded or altered walls can crack the same way.

## How to Read Severity: When to Worry

Not every step crack is an emergency, and not every one is harmless. Here's how professionals gauge whether a crack is cosmetic or structural.

| Sign | Likely cosmetic / stable | Likely structural — inspect promptly |
|---|---|---|
| Crack width | Hairline, under ~1/8 inch | Wider than ~1/4 inch |
| Change over time | Same for years | Visibly widening or lengthening |
| Taper | Even width | Wider at top or bottom (V-shaped) |
| Offset | Faces still aligned | Two sides no longer line up (shifted) |
| Wall shape | Flat and plumb | Bowing, bulging, or leaning |
| Doors/windows | Operate normally | Suddenly sticking or out of square |
| Location | Isolated, upper wall | Near foundation, corners, or spreading |

A hairline stair-step crack that has looked the same since you moved in is usually just failed mortar that needs **repointing**. A crack that is widening, offset, V-shaped, or paired with a **bowing brick wall** or sticking doors is signaling active movement and deserves a prompt professional look. A quick way to monitor a borderline crack yourself: mark the ends with a pencil and note the date, or bridge it with a strip of painter's tape, and check whether it moves over a few weeks.

**Horizontal** cracks and long **vertical** cracks are a separate, often more urgent category than diagonal step cracks — a horizontal crack across a wall can indicate serious lateral pressure and should always be inspected.

## The Diagnosis and Repair Process

A durable repair fixes the cause before it fixes the crack. Skipping that order is why so many patched cracks come back.

1. **Inspect and diagnose.** We look at the whole wall and its surroundings — crack pattern and width, wall plumb, grading and downspouts, nearby trees, lintels, and the foundation — to determine what's actually moving the masonry.
2. **Address the cause.** That might mean correcting drainage and grading, redirecting downspouts, addressing a rusted lintel, or, where there's real ongoing settlement, stabilizing the foundation before any brickwork.
3. **Rake out the failed joints.** The cracked and deteriorated mortar is ground and raked out to a sound depth, not just skimmed over.
4. **Replace damaged brick.** Any cracked, spalled, or loose bricks along the crack are removed and replaced with matching units.
5. **Repoint with matched mortar.** New mortar — typically **Type N or Type S** depending on the wall — is packed into the joints and tooled to match the surrounding profile. **Mortar matching** for color, texture, and hardness is what makes the repair disappear and keeps it from damaging the brick.
6. **Rebuild if needed.** Where a section is badly displaced or the wall is bulging, we rebuild that area of brick rather than trying to repoint a wall that's no longer sound.

## Repair vs. Rebuild: How the Call Gets Made

Most step cracks are handled at the **repair** level — rake, replace a few brick, repoint. A **rebuild** of the affected section becomes the right call when the brick along the crack is crushed or displaced, when the wall has bowed out of plane, or when repointing alone would just be cosmetic over a wall that's structurally compromised. The deciding factors are how much the brick itself has moved and whether the cause is now stable. An honest on-site assessment — not a guess from a photo — is how that decision should be made.

## DIY vs. Hiring a Professional Mason

It's tempting to grab a tube of caulk or a bag of premixed mortar and fill a step crack yourself. The problem is that this treats the visible symptom and buries the real one. If the wall is still moving, the patch cracks again within a season or two. Worse, sealing a crack with the wrong material — caulk where mortar belongs, or a mortar that's harder than the brick — traps moisture and can accelerate **freeze-thaw** and **spalling** damage. Diagnosing *why* a wall is cracking, matching mortar correctly, and knowing when a crack is structural is exactly the specialized judgment a professional mason brings.

## Maintenance and Prevention

You can't stop clay soil from moving, but you can remove most of what accelerates step cracking:

- **Manage water.** Keep gutters clear, extend downspouts several feet from the foundation, and make sure soil **slopes away** from the house.
- **Repoint failing mortar** before it becomes an open path for water — sound joints don't let freeze-thaw get started.
- **Watch big trees** near the foundation and address roots and uneven soil drying.
- **Inspect annually**, especially after a very wet spring or a hard winter, and mark any crack you're watching so you can tell if it's moving.

Catching a crack while it's still hairline and stable is far cheaper than waiting until a wall bulges.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are step cracks in brick walls serious?
It depends on the crack. Hairline stair-step cracks that stay the same width for years are often cosmetic and only need repointing. Cracks that are widening, wider than about a quarter inch, offset side-to-side, or paired with a bulging wall or sticking doors are signs of active foundation movement and should be inspected promptly.

### What causes stair-step cracks in brick?
They almost always follow the mortar joints because mortar is the weakest path through a wall. The underlying cause is usually differential foundation settlement, expansive clay soil swelling and shrinking with moisture, poor drainage against the foundation, tree roots, a rusted steel lintel, or freeze-thaw damage over many Chicago winters.
