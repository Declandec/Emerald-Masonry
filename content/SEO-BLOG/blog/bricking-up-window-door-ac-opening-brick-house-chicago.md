# Bricking Up a Window, Door or AC Opening in a Brick House

**Bricking up a window opening**, an old door, or a **through-wall air conditioner sleeve** is a common job on Chicago-area brick homes. Done properly, the frame comes out, new brick is **toothed into the existing courses**, tied to the wall and matched in brick and mortar, and the inside is insulated and finished. The result is a solid, dry wall that is hard to pick out from the street. Emerald Masonry LLC, a family-owned masonry contractor serving Chicago and the Chicagoland suburbs, does this work. Owners close openings for plenty of reasons: an AC sleeve replaced by central air, a side door no longer used after a remodel, a coal chute from a century ago, a basement window that leaks, or a kitchen layout that needs a solid wall. This guide covers how the work is done, the details that decide whether it blends in, and the rules to check first.

> The difference between a patch and a proper infill is whether the new brick is woven into the old wall or just stacked inside a hole.

## What kind of wall you have

Before planning the work, a mason needs to know how the wall is built, because the infill has to match it.

### Solid masonry

Most older Chicago homes, including **bungalows, two-flats and greystones**, have **solid masonry walls**: two or more layers (wythes) of brick, with face brick outside and softer **Chicago common brick** behind. Bricks laid sideways across the layers tie them together. An infill on these walls fills every layer.

### Brick veneer

Most homes built from the mid-century onward have **brick veneer**: one layer of brick over a wood or block backup wall, with an air space, **masonry ties**, **flashing** and **weep holes**. An infill here has to keep those details working.

| Feature | Solid masonry | Brick veneer |
|---|---|---|
| Typical homes | Pre-war bungalows, two-flats, greystones | Post-war ranches, raised ranches, newer homes |
| Layers to fill | All wythes | Face brick plus backup wall |
| Ties to existing | Toothed and bonded across wythes | Toothed face plus veneer ties to backup |
| Water management | Mass wall, dries outward | Air space, flashing and weep holes |
| Insulation | Interior side | In the backup wall |

## Common openings people close

### Through-wall AC sleeves

Many Chicago bungalows, two-flats and apartments had **through-wall air conditioners** cut in under windows or in living rooms. Once central air goes in, the sleeve is a leaky, drafty hole. Brick around them is often cracked from the cut, and the lintel or support over the sleeve may be minimal.

### Unused doors

Side, back and basement doors removed during remodels. These are larger, may have a step or stoop outside, and often have a substantial lintel above.

### Windows

Windows closed for a kitchen, bathroom or closet layout, or a basement window that keeps leaking.

### Coal chutes and old service openings

Small iron doors near the base of older homes that once took coal deliveries. They rust, leak and let in drafts and pests.

## Check the rules first

- **Permits**: closing an opening changes the exterior wall, and many municipalities require a permit. Check with your village or the City of Chicago.
- **Egress**: sleeping rooms need an **egress window** under building codes. A bedroom's only window, or a basement bedroom's egress window, generally cannot be bricked up.
- **Light and ventilation**: some rooms have minimum window or ventilation requirements.
- **Historic districts and landmarks**: visible exterior changes may need review.
- **Neighbors and lot lines**: on tight city lots, a wall on the property line may have additional rules.

## How a mason bricks up an opening

Every opening differs, but a proper job follows this sequence.

1. **Remove the unit, frame and sleeve.** Take out the window, door, AC unit or chute and its frame, and any caulk or foam.
2. **Check the lintel.** Decide whether the existing **steel lintel** or arch stays, gets replaced, or comes out with the brick above supported.
3. **Tooth out the edges.** Cut back alternating bricks along each side of the opening so new brick can interlock with the existing courses, rather than leaving a straight vertical seam.
4. **Prepare the sill area.** Remove the old sill if it will not stay, and set a solid base course.
5. **Build the backup.** On solid walls, fill the inner wythes. On veneer walls, frame or block the backup to match the wall around it.
6. **Lay the face brick.** Use matched brick, keeping each course aligned with the courses on either side and toothed into the existing wall.
7. **Tie it in.** Bond across wythes on solid walls, or install veneer ties on veneer walls.
8. **Keep water out.** Install flashing and weep holes at the base of veneer infills and wherever the original wall had them.
9. **Match the joints.** Tool the mortar to the same profile and color as the surrounding wall.
10. **Finish the inside.** Insulate, frame and finish to match the interior wall.

## Matching the brick and mortar

The infill is judged on how it looks from the street, so matching is the main event.

| Element | What to match | Tips |
|---|---|---|
| Brick size | Height, length and thickness | Older brick is often not modern modular size |
| Color range | The blend of shades in the wall | Pull several samples; one color rarely works |
| Texture | Smooth, wire-cut, sand-struck, rough | Texture changes how color reads |
| Weathering | Age and soiling of the wall face | **Salvaged brick** often blends better on old walls |
| Mortar color | Sand and pigment of existing joints | Match to cleaned, weathered mortar |
| Joint profile | Concave, raked, flush, struck | Must match or the infill outlines itself |

A useful trick on very visible walls: take brick from a hidden area of the same house, such as a garage wall or the back of a chimney, use it in the visible infill, and replace the hidden spot with new brick.

## Materials and techniques

- **Brick**: matched new brick, salvaged brick of the same era, or brick reclaimed from a hidden area of the house.
- **Mortar**: Type N for most residential veneer, or a lime-rich mix on older soft brick, matched in color. Mortar should never be harder than the brick.
- **Ties**: corrosion-resistant veneer ties, or header bonding on solid walls.
- **Flashing and weeps**: flexible flashing and weep vents at the base of veneer infills.
- **Lintels**: primed or galvanized steel where a lintel stays or is replaced.
- **Insulation**: on the interior or in the backup wall.

## Mistakes we see

- **Straight seams**: new brick stacked inside the opening without toothing, leaving a crack-prone vertical line on each side.
- **Wrong brick size**: modern modular brick in an old wall, so the courses do not line up.
- **Gray mortar** in a tinted or weathered wall.
- **Rusted lintel left in place**, which keeps expanding and cracks the new brick.
- **No flashing on veneer walls**, trapping water at the base of the infill.
- **Concrete block or plywood** behind a single row of face brick, with no ties.
- **No insulation**, leaving a cold spot inside.

## Questions to answer before you close an opening

A little planning keeps the job from turning into two jobs.

### Is the opening really unused?

Check for anything still running through it: a dryer vent, a gas line, a cable or electrical run, or a basement window that a furnace room depends on for combustion air. Those have to be rerouted by the right trade before the opening is closed.

### What is on the inside?

Decide early whether the interior side will be drywall, plaster or tile, and whether you will hire that work separately. The mason needs to know how far the infill should come to the inside face and where the insulation goes.

### Could you want it back?

If there is any chance a future owner will want a door or window there again, say so. The lintel can be left in good condition and the infill laid so it can be removed cleanly later.

### Is anything else nearby failing?

Openings often sit next to other problems: a rusted lintel on the window beside it, worn mortar on the same wall, or a cracked sill. Doing them in the same visit saves a second setup and helps the new work blend with the old.

## DIY vs hiring a pro

Removing an AC unit is a reasonable DIY step. Bricking up the opening is not. Toothing into an existing wall without loosening it, keeping courses level, matching brick and mortar, and handling lintels, flashing and ties take experience, and mistakes show on the outside of your house for good.

## Maintenance after the work

- **Expect a slight color difference** at first; new mortar and brick weather in over a few seasons.
- **Keep weep holes clear** at the base of veneer infills.
- **Watch the seams** for the first winter; hairline shrinkage cracks in fresh mortar can be repointed.
- **Do not seal or paint** the new brick to make it match; it traps moisture.
