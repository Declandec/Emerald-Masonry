Homeowners assume the skill in this job is setting the new brick. It is not — **any competent mason can lay a unit.**

**The skill is getting the old one out.**

## Why Removal Is the Hard Part

A brick sits in a wall surrounded by four to six neighbors, all bonded to it by mortar. Getting it out means separating it from all of them **without transferring force into any of them.**

Done correctly, the mortar joints around the failed unit are cut and cleared first, so the brick is freed rather than fought. It then comes out cleanly and its neighbors are untouched — same faces, same crisp edges, same bond.

Done in a hurry, somebody attacks the **brick** with a hammer and chisel. The unit fractures, force travels into the surrounding masonry, and you end up with **chipped arrises on three adjacent bricks and a cracked fourth** — none of which needed replacing when the crew arrived.

**That is how a twelve-brick job becomes a twenty-brick job, and the homeowner pays for the difference without ever being told why.**

It is also why a fast quote on brick replacement is worth a second look. Removal time is where the corners get cut, and the damage is invisible until it is done.

## Spalling vs. Joint Failure

The distinction that determines the whole scope.

**Mortar failure is repointing. Brick failure is replacement.**

Mortar is the sacrificial layer in a masonry wall — deliberately softer than the brick so it absorbs stress and wears out first. When only the joints have gone, [tuckpointing](/services/tuckpointing) restores the wall and the units are untouched.

Once water has been entering long enough to saturate the **brick itself**, freezing inside it breaks the face off in layers. That is **spalling**, and **the fired outer skin that made the brick weather-resistant does not grow back.** Those units get cut out.

Most Villa Park jobs are a mix, plus fixing whatever let the water in.

## Matching: Four Things, Not Two

| What must match | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Size** | Dimensions varied by era and manufacturer; off-size throws the coursing |
| **Color and texture** | The visible match |
| **Absorption** | How much water the unit takes on relative to its neighbors |
| **Hardness** | How it behaves under freeze-thaw beside the original units |

Get size and color right and absorption and hardness wrong, and the repair looks perfect on day one, then behaves differently through every wet-freeze cycle.

## Signs You Need Brick Replacement

- **Spalling faces** — flaking, shelling, popping or crumbling.
- **Cracked brick**, particularly cracks running through the unit rather than following joints.
- **Loose brick** that moves under hand pressure.
- **Deep pitting** where units have lost real depth.
- **Rust staining** below a window or garage opening — a corroding steel lintel needing attention alongside the brick.
- **A patch of damage** under a gutter run, below a sill, or at splash height near grade.
- **Brick debris** collecting at the base of the wall.
- **Efflorescence** on the surrounding wall, showing water is moving through.

## Our Process

1. **Free on-site assessment.** We count the failed units, take a sample for matching, and find where water is entering.
2. **Fix the cause first.** Replacing brick while a rusting lintel, failed sill or overflowing gutter keeps running guarantees a repeat.
3. **Source the match.** Current production where one exists, salvage or a blend where it does not. Options shown before we proceed.
4. **Cut the joints, not the brick.** The mortar around each unit is cut to free it, protecting the surrounding masonry and its edges.
5. **Set the new units**, properly bedded and aligned to the existing coursing.
6. **Repoint** with mortar matched for hardness, color and sand — **Type N** for most post-war walls, **Type S** where conditions warrant.
7. **Tool the joints** to the original profile.
8. **Distribute any variation** across the area rather than clustering it, and clean down gently. Never sandblasting or high-pressure washing.

## What Drives Cost

- **Number of units**, and whether the damage is localized or spread.
- **Removal difficulty** — tight bond, hard mortar, or units in a decorative course.
- **Match difficulty** — a discontinued color adds sourcing time as much as cost.
- **Height and access** — ground level versus staging or a lift.
- **Underlying cause** — a rusting lintel or failed sill adds scope beyond the brick.
- **Extent of accompanying joint work.**

## What Waiting Costs

Spalling is progressive. Once a brick loses its fired face, the porous body underneath absorbs water far faster, so that unit deteriorates more quickly and **starts wetting its neighbors**. A dozen brick becomes fifty. And because spalling means water is already inside the wall, the steel lintels and ties in there are corroding on the same timeline.

There is a sourcing argument too: **the longer you wait, the more units you need**, and the harder it becomes to find enough matching material for a larger repair.
