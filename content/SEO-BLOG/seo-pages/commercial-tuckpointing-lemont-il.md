## The rule that governs every joint in this village

**Mortar must always be softer than the units it surrounds.**

Mortar is the sacrificial element in a masonry wall. It is designed to take the movement and the weathering so the stone or brick does not have to, and to be renewed periodically — that is what repointing is.

Reverse that relationship and the wall destroys itself. Put a hard, Portland-heavy modern mortar around soft native limestone, and when the building moves, the stress has nowhere to go but into the stone. The faces spall. And unlike a joint, a spalled limestone face does not get renewed — it gets cut out and patched, or the unit gets replaced with stone that will never quite match a hundred and forty years of weathering.

This is the single most common way well-meant repairs ruin historic Lemont buildings, and it is why the mortar specification matters more here than the price per square foot. Our guide to [lime versus Portland mortar](/blog/lime-vs-portland-mortar) covers the chemistry.

## Where the commercial work is

**The downtown historic core.** Nineteenth-century limestone and brick commercial buildings along Main and Stephen Street and the blocks around them — storefronts, upper-floor apartments, and civic buildings, many of them with original stone that has been standing since the quarry era. Joint width, tooling profile, mortar colour and sand all read as part of the building's character on these elevations, and the work is slower and more exacting than a modern wall.

**The canal and Sanitary & Ship Canal corridor.** Industrial and warehouse buildings along the old I&M alignment and the working canal — long brick and block elevations, loading openings, and the uniform weathering that comes with a hundred feet of unbroken wall facing the same weather.

**The Archer Avenue and State Street commercial stretches.** Mid-century and later retail and service buildings, storefront returns, and flat roofs with parapets above.

**Multi-unit and mixed-use.** Upper-floor residential over ground-floor commercial in the downtown, where the board or owner and the tenant have different priorities and the scope has to serve both.

**The newer Lemont Road and 127th Street commercial corridor**, where the buildings are veneer and the issues are sealant, expansion joints, weeps and flashing rather than wholesale repointing.

## Signs a commercial elevation is due

- **Joints you can rake out** with a key — soft, sandy, or receded well behind the face
- **Missing mortar** leaving open joints, particularly at the top of the wall and around openings
- **Spalling or delaminating stone faces**, which on limestone lifts in sheets along the bedding planes
- **Efflorescence** — white mineral bloom indicating water actively moving through the wall
- **Step cracking** through the joints
- **Rust staining** below a window or door head, meaning a steel lintel is corroding
- **Damp interior walls** or tenant complaints on the weather elevation
- **Previous repairs in obviously hard grey mortar**, which is often the reason the stone beside them is failing

## How we do the work

1. **Assess elevation by elevation** and state square footages separately — deterioration is never uniform, and one elevation is usually years ahead of the rest.
2. **Establish the existing mortar** — type, softness, colour, sand — before proposing a replacement.
3. **Cut the joints back** to a depth that lets the new mortar key in, cleanly and without over-cutting into the units. Depth is invisible in a proposal and decisive in the result.
4. **Build a mock-up panel** and let it cure and dry before approval, so colour, profile and match are agreed as a physical standard. See [mock-ups and test panels](/blog/mock-ups-test-panels-masonry-contract).
5. **Repoint** with matched mortar, tooled to the existing profile.
6. **Repair or replace failed units** — brick or stone — matched in size, colour and texture.
7. **Address the water sources** feeding the wall: coping, flashing, sealant, downspouts.
8. **Clean down** with the gentlest effective method. No sandblasting and no high-pressure washing on historic stone — both strip the weather-resistant outer skin permanently. See [why sandblasting damages brick](/blog/why-sandblasting-damages-brick).

## What drives the cost

Elevation area and height. Access — ground reach, lift, or scaffolding. Whether the masonry is brick or native limestone, since stone work is slower and the matching harder. Joint condition and how much has to be cut. Unit repair and replacement volume. Whether the building is in the historic downtown and the work is subject to review. Overhead protection where the public passes beneath. Whether work must happen outside trading hours. Weather windows, since mortar needs workable temperatures to cure properly.
