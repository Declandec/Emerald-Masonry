Drive the subdivisions that went up between the early 1990s and the mid-2000s — the larger homes off Laraway, along 80th and 88th, out toward Lincoln-Way and the Prestwick area — and you will see tall, handsome brick chimneys on a great many of them.

**Most of them are not masonry chimneys.** They are **brick-clad chases**: a wood-framed box, sheathed and faced with a single wythe of brick veneer, built around a factory-made metal fireplace and a metal flue pipe. From the street it is indistinguishable from a real masonry stack. From a repair standpoint it is a different structure entirely.

## Chase or chimney? Look at the top

| | True masonry chimney | Brick-clad chase |
|---|---|---|
| At the top | Clay flue tiles through a sloped concrete or mortar **crown** | Metal flue pipe through a flat sheet-metal **chase cover** |
| Structure | Solid masonry, self-supporting, own footing | Wood frame with one wythe of brick veneer |
| Typical failure | Crown cracking, joint loss, spalling, leaning | Rusted or ponding chase cover, veneer cracking, flashing and cricket failure |
| Correct repair | Rebuild crown, repoint, replace units, rebuild above roof | Fabricate and replace the cover, repair veneer, re-detail flashing |

Getting that wrong is expensive in both directions. A slab of mortar troweled over the top of a chase is not a crown and will crack within a season. Conversely, a real masonry crown on a century-old stack in the older part of the village near the Metra station and Breidert Green needs to be rebuilt properly, not covered with a metal lid.

## Where Frankfort chimneys actually fail

**At the top, first and worst.** The top of a chimney is the most exposed masonry on your house — no roof over it, wet on every face, freezing and thawing more times per winter than any wall. Whether it is a cracked crown or a rusted chase cover holding standing water, this is where the water gets in. [Why the top of your chimney fails first](/blog/why-the-top-of-your-chimney-fails-first) and [chimney crown versus chimney cap](/blog/chimney-crown-vs-chimney-cap) cover the distinction in detail.

**At the roof line.** Step flashing, counterflashing and the joint where they lap is a chronic leak source because it sits on the boundary between the mason's work and the roofer's. On Frankfort's steeper roofs there is a second issue: a wide chimney on the **up-slope** side of a roof needs a **cricket** — a small framed diverter behind it — or every rainfall runs down the slope and ponds against the masonry. Plenty of these houses were built without one. See [chimney flashing leaks](/blog/chimney-flashing-leaks-chicago).

**Spalled brick below the crown.** Once water has been entering the top for a few years, the brick beneath it saturates and starts losing faces. On a veneer chase there is no second wythe behind it, so spalling reaches the sheathing faster than it would on a solid stack.

**Leaning and separation.** Less common on newer stock, but real on the older houses in and around the village center. A stack that has moved out of plumb or pulled away from the wall is a structural question, not a cosmetic one — [why brick chimneys lean and pull away](/blog/why-brick-chimneys-lean-pull-away-from-house) covers what that means.
