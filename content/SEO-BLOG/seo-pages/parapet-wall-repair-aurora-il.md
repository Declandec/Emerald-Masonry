## Why Parapets Deteriorate Ahead of Everything Else

The wall below your roofline has two advantages: it is exposed on one face only, and it has a heated interior behind it that helps drive moisture out. A parapet has neither.

- **Weathered on both faces.** Street side and roof side, roughly double the wetting, with no dry side to migrate toward.
- **No interior heat.** Nothing dries it between storms, so it stays saturated far longer.
- **It carries the roof's water.** Everything leaving the roof edge passes at or near the parapet, concentrating flow exactly where the masonry is weakest.
- **Horizontal surfaces on top.** Coping is a horizontal joint line at the highest, most exposed point of the building — and horizontal joints hold water rather than shedding it.
- **Thermal movement.** The parapet expands and contracts more freely than the restrained wall below, opening joints and cracking corners.

Then Kane County freeze-thaw does the rest: dozens of crossings of the freezing point each winter, each one expanding water trapped inside saturated masonry.

## The Item Everyone Skips: Through-Wall Flashing

Most parapet scopes cover repointing and coping. Fewer address **through-wall flashing** — and it is frequently the actual fix.

A parapet will take on some water no matter how good the joints are. Through-wall flashing is the membrane inside the wall that catches that water and directs it back out. A parapet without functioning flashing has nowhere to send the water it inevitably absorbs, so it sends it **down inside the wall and into the top floor.**

This is why buildings keep leaking after the roof is replaced and the joints are repointed. The water was never entering at the roof or the surface — it was entering at the top of the wall and running behind everything.

## Signs Your Aurora Parapet Needs Work

- **Cracked, loose, displaced or missing coping stones** along the top of the wall.
- **Open, sandy or receded mortar joints**, on the roof side as often as the street side.
- **Spalling brick or delaminating limestone** on either face.
- **Efflorescence** on the parapet or the wall below it.
- **A visible lean, bow or bulge**, sighted along the wall from a corner.
- **Interior water staining** on the top floor near exterior walls.
- **Step cracks** at parapet corners — the classic thermal-movement signature.
- **Vegetation growing** out of the joints, proving persistent saturation.
- **Debris on the roof or sidewalk** below the wall.

On a downtown building with a public sidewalk directly below, loose masonry at a roof edge is a life-safety and liability matter, not deferred maintenance.

## Historic vs. Modern Parapets — Different Mortar Entirely

Aurora's building stock splits cleanly, and the mortar decision follows.

**19th-century downtown blocks** were laid with **soft lime mortar** and softer brick, with quarried limestone coping and trim. **The governing rule is that mortar must be softer than the brick it holds**, so these walls need a lime-based mix matched to the original. A hard modern Portland mortar inverts that relationship and drives freeze-thaw stress into the brick faces, spalling them within a few winters — on a historic facade, that is permanent loss of original material.

**Modern commercial buildings** — brick veneer, CMU, precast — take **Type N or Type S** as conditions warrant, with cast stone coping.

Getting this backwards on a downtown Aurora building is one of the more expensive mistakes available.
