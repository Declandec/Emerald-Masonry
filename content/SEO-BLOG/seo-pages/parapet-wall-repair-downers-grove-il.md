## Why parapets fail before the rest of the building

A parapet is the section of wall that continues past the roof line, and it is punished in ways no other masonry on the property is.

- **Weathered on three surfaces.** Street face, roof-side face, and top. Everything else on the building has weather on one side and conditioned space on the other.
- **No thermal buffer.** There is no heated interior behind it, so it goes through the full swing of an Illinois day on both faces — which means it moves more than the wall beneath, and movement opens joints.
- **It takes water straight down into its core** the moment the coping joints open.
- **The roof-side face is invisible from the ground**, so half the deterioration is never seen by anyone who is not on the roof.

The practical result is consistent: **the top four feet of a masonry building is almost always in worse condition than the forty feet below it.**

## The failure sequence

**Coping first.** Stone, cast stone, precast, or metal — it is the parapet's roof. Open joints, cracked units, or a failed metal seal, and water goes directly into the top of the wall.

**Through-wall flashing second.** Older parapets have flashing meant to intercept water inside the wall and direct it back out. Corroded, discontinuous, or never properly installed, and the water that entered has nowhere to go but down into the building.

**Joints on both faces.** Eroding at the same rate on the roof side as the street side, and nobody sees the roof side.

**Movement.** A parapet that has been wet for decades starts to lean, bow toward the street, or rack along its length. This is where it stops being maintenance.

**Unit loss.** Spalled brick or block faces — and on a parapet, spalled material has somewhere to fall.

## How we scope it

1. **Assess from the roof.** The roof-side face and the coping tell you far more than the street elevation does, and neither is visible from a sidewalk.
2. **Establish repoint versus rebuild**, and how far down sound work begins.
3. **Write the trade boundary explicitly** — what is masonry, what is roofing, and who owns the counterflashing — before either scope is priced.
4. **Protect the roof.** Staging and material handling on a membrane roof is planned, not improvised.
5. **Dismantle to sound masonry** where rebuilding is required, salvaging units where they can be reused.
6. **Rebuild plumb and true** with matched brick and matched mortar.
7. **Install or restore through-wall flashing** so water entering the wall has a designed route back out.
8. **Set coping** with proper slope, overhang, drip, and correctly detailed joints.
9. **Repoint, seal where sealant belongs, and clean down.**

## Materials

Mortar matched in type, colour, sand, and joint profile. Salvaged brick reused where sound; replacements matched in size, colour and texture. Through-wall flashing appropriate to the assembly and detailed continuous with end dams, because flashing that stops short of the ends drains into the wall. Coping set with slope and a real drip edge. Backer rod and elastomeric sealant at coping joints, sized for movement — not a bead of caulk smeared across a gap.
