## The Matching Problem, Explained Properly

When a section of wall has to be rebuilt, the finished result lives or dies on the match. That match has three separate parts, and homeowners usually only think about the first.

**Color and blend range.** Postwar and 1970s face brick was frequently a variegated blend rather than a single shade. Order a flat colour that matches the average and the rebuilt area reads wrong even though every individual brick looks close.

**Size and texture.** Unit dimensions and surface texture have to line up or the coursing and the shadow give it away from the street.

**Mortar.** Colour, sand and joint profile do the visual work — and **hardness matched to the units** does the structural work. Mortar harder than the brick makes the brick the sacrificial element and spalls it again within a few winters.

### Why the hidden elevation matters

On a **fully bricked** house, the professional answer to an unavailable blend is straightforward: harvest sound original brick from a concealed elevation — behind a deck, the back of a detached garage, a wall facing a fence line — use those units in the visible repair, and set the modern brick in the hidden spot. The visible wall stays original.

On a **partial-brick** house there is no concealed elevation to borrow from. Every square foot of brick is on display. That pushes the match toward reclaimed stock of the right era, or toward the closest current production combined with genuinely careful mortar matching — which does more of the work than people expect, because the eye reads the joint pattern before it reads individual units.

This is worth knowing before anyone starts cutting. It changes sourcing, lead time and sometimes the sensible extent of the rebuild.

## What Fails on Palos Heights Homes

The village built out largely through the **1950s to 1980s** — brick ranches, split-levels, Georgians and raised ranches on generous lots, with the masonry porches, planters, piers and freestanding walls that came with that era. Fifty to seventy Cook County winters later, the failures cluster in predictable places.

**Freestanding and grade-level masonry first.** Planters, front piers, porch walls and mailbox structures are exposed on every face, sit in the splash and de-icing salt zone, and usually have a cap that has stopped shedding water. They fail decades ahead of the house.

**Wide openings.** Picture windows and attached garage headers span further and carry more brick on bare steel, which is why the front of the house so often goes first.

**The bottom three courses.** Splash-back off walks and drives, snow banked all winter, salt, and foundation plantings holding moisture against the wall.

**And the mechanism is always the same.** Water enters a tired unit or an open joint, freezes, expands about nine percent, and forces the masonry apart. Once the fired face pops off, the softer interior absorbs faster and the failure spreads sideways through the course — the process laid out in [what causes brick to spall in Illinois](/blog/what-causes-brick-to-spall-illinois).
