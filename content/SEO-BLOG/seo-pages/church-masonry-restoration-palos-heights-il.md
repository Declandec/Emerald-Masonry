That means **mid-century church architecture**: tall, plain brick walls, a slender freestanding bell tower or campanile, large window walls of stained or faceted glass, and limestone or precast trim used sparingly.

> A mid-century church does not fail like a 1900s parish. Its mortar is harder, its walls are longer and its tower is more slender — so the cracking, the leaks and the falling-hazard risk all show up in different places.

## What church masonry restoration in Palos Heights involves

On a mid-century campus, the work usually falls into five areas:

| Area | Typical construction | What fails |
|---|---|---|
| Long nave walls | Tall brick walls, often with few movement joints | Vertical cracks near corners and window walls, bowed sections |
| Bell tower or campanile | Slender brick shaft, steel inside, stone or precast cap | Open cap joints, rust jacking at steel, loose brick at the top |
| Window walls | Stained or faceted glass set in masonry openings | Failed sealant, rusting steel frames and lintels, cracked jambs |
| Trim and roof edges | Limestone or precast coping, sills and bands | Open joints, spalled edges, water feeding walls below |
| Parish hall and school | Later additions in different brick | Cold joints at additions, parapet leaks, worn entrances |

### Long walls with nowhere to move

Brick expands permanently as it absorbs moisture and moves with every temperature swing. Many churches from this era were built with long, uninterrupted brick walls and **too few expansion joints**. The wall relieves the stress itself — with long vertical cracks near corners, at the junction of nave and tower, and beside large window openings. Repair means cutting in properly sized **movement joints** and sealing them, not just filling the cracks with mortar so they reopen next winter.

### The bell tower

A freestanding tower is the tallest and most exposed masonry on campus, wet on all four faces and topped by a single cap. Many have embedded steel that rusts and expands once water gets in. Because towers are rarely inspected up close, they are where a church is most likely to have a **falling-masonry hazard** without knowing it. We inspect from a lift or scaffold, not binoculars.

### Glass and window walls

The large art-glass walls that define mid-century churches sit in masonry openings held up by steel lintels and frames. When the sealant fails and the steel rusts, the brick above cracks and the glass panels take on stress and water. We repair the masonry, replace failed lintels and reseal the perimeter, and coordinate with a stained-glass studio where the glass itself needs work.

## Signs your church needs masonry restoration

- **Long vertical cracks** in nave walls, especially near corners or windows.
- **Rust staining** below the tower cap, window frames or lintels.
- **Loose or displaced brick** near the top of the bell tower.
- **Open joints or tipped coping** on limestone or precast trim.
- **Water stains** inside on walls beside window walls.
- **White efflorescence** on walls under roof edges and downspouts.
- **Cracks where the parish hall or school meets the church.**

## Risks of waiting

A tower or roof-edge piece that falls near an entrance used by families and children is a safety problem, not a maintenance one. Water entering through open coping and window perimeters damages plaster, organs and art glass far more expensively than the masonry repair. And cracks in a long wall that cannot move only grow each winter.
