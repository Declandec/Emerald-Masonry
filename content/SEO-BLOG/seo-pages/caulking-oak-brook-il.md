## Caulking Is Not Tuckpointing — And the Difference Matters

This confusion causes real damage, so it is worth stating plainly.

| | Tuckpointing / repointing | Caulking / joint sealant |
|---|---|---|
| **What it fills** | Mortar joints between masonry units | Joints designed to move |
| **Material** | Cementitious mortar — rigid | Elastomeric sealant — flexible |
| **Purpose** | Structural bond and weather seal | Accommodate movement, seal against water |
| **Where** | Brick and block bed and head joints | Expansion joints, control joints, perimeters, panel joints |
| **Fails as** | Erosion, recession, cracking | Loss of adhesion, splitting, hardening |

**Put rigid mortar in a joint designed to move and it cracks on the first thermal cycle.** Put sealant where mortar belongs and you have no structural bond. Buildings need both, in the right places, and a contractor should be able to tell you which is which on your wall.

## What Fails, and How to Spot It

Sealant is a **wear item**. UV exposure degrades it, thermal cycling works it back and forth thousands of times, and the joint it sits in never stops moving. Failure shows up as:

- **Adhesion failure** — the sealant has pulled away cleanly from one side of the joint, leaving a visible gap.
- **Cohesive failure** — the sealant has split down its own middle.
- **Hardening and cracking** — the material has lost elasticity and crazed.
- **Chalking or a powdery surface** — UV breakdown of the sealant face.
- **Missing sealant** entirely in sections.
- **Bubbling, sagging or wrinkling**, usually a sign of a poor original installation.
- **Staining below a joint**, where water has been running the wall.
- **Interior water staining** on upper floors with a sound roof — the classic symptom.
- **Elevated heating and cooling costs**, from air infiltration through open joints.

**South and west elevations almost always fail first**, because they take the most sun and the widest daily temperature swings.

## Our Caulking Process

The failure mode we see most often is not old sealant — it is **new sealant installed badly**. Doing this correctly is mostly about preparation and joint geometry.

1. **Free on-site assessment.** We walk the elevations, evaluate joint condition, joint width and movement, substrate type and prior repairs, and identify where water is actually entering.
2. **Full removal of old sealant.** Cut out completely, not caulked over. Sealant applied over failed sealant fails again quickly, and it is the single most common shortcut in this trade.
3. **Substrate preparation.** Joint faces cleaned to sound, clean material; residue and contaminants removed so the new sealant can bond.
4. **Correct backer rod.** Closed-cell or open-cell rod sized to the joint, set to the correct depth. Backer rod establishes the **hourglass profile** that lets sealant stretch — without it, sealant bonds three-sided and tears itself apart.
5. **Priming** where the substrate and product require it.
6. **Sealant installation.** Gunned and tooled properly to wet both joint faces and shape the bead.
7. **Cure and inspection**, with documentation of completed elevations for your records.

## What Drives Caulking Cost in Oak Brook

- **Linear footage** of joint, by joint type.
- **Height and access** — ground level versus swing stage, boom lift or scaffold on a multi-story office building. Access is frequently the largest single component.
- **Removal difficulty** — old silicone and some urethanes are labor-intensive to cut out cleanly.
- **Joint size and condition** — irregular, oversized or damaged joints need repair before sealing.
- **Substrate repairs** — spalled precast edges or deteriorated masonry at a joint must be addressed first.
- **Product selection** — high-movement and specialty sealants cost more than standard.
- **Occupancy and scheduling** — phasing around tenants and business hours.
- **Documentation** — elevation drawings and completion records for property managers and ownership.

## Why Deferred Sealant Gets Expensive

Sealant is inexpensive relative to almost everything it protects. Once joints open, water enters the wall assembly and goes after the things that actually cost money: **corroding embedded steel, anchors and shelf angles, saturated insulation, deteriorating panel edges, interior finishes and tenant space**. On precast and panel construction, water reaching the anchorage is a structural conversation, not a maintenance one.

Replacing sealant on a cycle is one of the highest-return maintenance items on a commercial building. Replacing it after the leaks start means paying for the sealant plus everything the water reached.
