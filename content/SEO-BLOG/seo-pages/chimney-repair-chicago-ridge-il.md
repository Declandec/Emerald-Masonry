Most Chicago Ridge homes went up in the 1950s, 60s and early 70s. They are compact brick ranches and raised ranches with one masonry chimney that once carried exhaust from a gas furnace and a water heater together. Over the last twenty years most of those furnaces have been replaced with high-efficiency units that vent out the side of the house through plastic pipe.

What often stays behind is the water heater — still venting, on its own, into a brick flue that was built for two appliances.

> A small, cool exhaust in a big, cold masonry flue condenses. The moisture soaks into the mortar and brick from the inside, freezes in winter, and the chimney starts deteriorating from the top down — usually years before anything looks wrong from the driveway.

## Why chimney repair in Chicago Ridge often starts inside the flue

Flue gas contains water vapour. When the flue is warm and the exhaust is strong, that vapour leaves the top of the chimney. When the flue is oversized and the exhaust is weak, the vapour cools, condenses on the masonry, and is absorbed.

That moisture is mildly acidic, and the coldest part of the chimney — the section above the roof — gets the most of it. The pattern we find on Chicago Ridge ranches is consistent:

- **White staining** streaking down the upper courses, often worst on the north side.
- **Spalled brick faces** in the top few courses while the brick lower down is fine.
- **Crumbling mortar** that comes out sandy when scraped, again concentrated near the top.
- **A cracked or broken crown**, with water now entering from above as well as below.
- **Debris at the cleanout** in the basement: flakes of tile, sand and mortar.

Whether the flue itself needs a liner is a question for a chimney sweep or HVAC contractor, and we will say so. **Our job is the masonry**: rebuilding what the moisture has destroyed so the chimney is sound and shedding water again.

## Other signs your Chicago Ridge chimney needs repair

- A **water stain on the ceiling or wall** beside the chimney.
- **Flashing pulling away** from the brick where the chimney meets a low-pitch roof.
- **Loose or missing brick** at the top of the stack, or brick fragments on the roof or in the gutter.
- A **leaning chimney** or a visible gap where it meets the house.
- **No chimney cap**, letting rain, birds and debris straight down the flue.
- **Recessed mortar joints** you can see from the ground with binoculars.

## Risks of waiting

Chimney damage accelerates. Once water gets into the brick, each freeze-thaw cycle expands the cracks, and a crown repair that would have been simple becomes a rebuild of the top of the stack. Loose brick at roof height is a falling hazard over the driveway and walk, and a flashing leak left for a season usually means ceiling and drywall repairs too.

## Our chimney repair process

1. **Inspect from the roof**, not the ground — crown, cap, top courses, joints, flashing and the condition of the flue opening.
2. **Explain what we find** with photos, including whether a liner question should go to a sweep or HVAC contractor first.
3. **Repair or rebuild the masonry**:
   - Cut out and **repoint** failed mortar joints.
   - **Replace spalled brick** with matched units.
   - **Rebuild the stack above the roof line** where the top courses are too far gone.
   - Form a **new sloped crown** with a proper overhang and drip edge.
4. **Re-flash**: new counterflashing cut into fresh mortar joints, coordinated with your roofer where the step flashing needs replacing.
5. **Cap the flue** and, where appropriate, apply a breathable masonry water repellent.
6. **Clean up** and hand over before-and-after photos.

## Materials

Type N or Type S mortar depending on exposure; brick matched for size and colour to your home; a reinforced, sloped mortar or cast crown with a drip edge; stainless or galvanized chimney cap; counterflashing set into reglets or joints; and breathable, vapour-permeable masonry sealer — never a film that traps moisture inside the brick.
