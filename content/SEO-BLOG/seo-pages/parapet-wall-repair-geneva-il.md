## Why Parapets Fail First

The wall below your roofline has two advantages: it is exposed on one face only, and it has a heated interior behind it that helps drive moisture out. A parapet has neither.

- **Weathered on both faces.** Street side and roof side — roughly double the wetting, with no dry side to migrate toward.
- **No interior heat.** Nothing dries it between storms, so it stays saturated far longer.
- **It carries the roof's water.** Everything leaving the roof edge passes at or near the parapet.
- **Horizontal surfaces on top.** Coping sits at the highest, most exposed point of the building, and horizontal masonry holds water rather than shedding it.
- **Thermal movement.** The parapet expands and contracts more freely than the restrained wall below, opening joints and cracking corners.

Then Kane County freeze-thaw does the rest — dozens of crossings of the freezing point each winter, each expanding trapped water inside saturated masonry.

## The Item Most Scopes Skip

Most parapet proposals cover repointing and coping. Fewer address **through-wall flashing** — and it is frequently the actual fix.

A parapet takes on some water no matter how good the joints are. Through-wall flashing is the membrane inside the wall that catches that water and directs it back out. **A parapet without functioning flashing has nowhere to send the water it absorbs**, so it sends it down inside the wall and into the upper floor.

This is why buildings keep leaking after the roof is replaced and the joints are repointed. The water was never entering at the roof.

## Signs Your Geneva Parapet Needs Work

- **Cracked, loose, displaced or missing coping stones** along the top of the wall.
- **Open, sandy or receded mortar joints**, on the roof side as often as the street side.
- **Spalling brick or delaminating limestone** on either face.
- **Efflorescence** on the parapet or the wall below it.
- **A visible lean, bow or bulge**, sighted along the wall from a corner.
- **Interior water staining** on the upper floor near exterior walls.
- **Step cracks** at parapet corners — the classic thermal-movement signature.
- **Vegetation growing** out of the joints, proving persistent saturation.
- **Debris on the roof or sidewalk** below the wall.

On Third Street, that last one is not a maintenance note. **It is the item that requires action the same day.**

## Historic vs. Modern Mortar

Geneva's building stock splits cleanly, and the mortar decision follows.

**19th-century downtown blocks** were laid with **soft lime mortar**, softer brick and quarried limestone coping and trim. **The governing rule is that mortar must be softer than the units it holds**, so these walls need a lime-based mix matched to the original. A hard modern Portland mortar inverts the relationship, drives freeze-thaw stress into the brick faces, and spalls them within a few winters — permanent loss of original material on a historic facade.

**Modern commercial buildings** — brick veneer, CMU, precast — take **Type N or Type S** as conditions warrant, with cast stone coping.
