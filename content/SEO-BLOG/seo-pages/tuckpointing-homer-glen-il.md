## Veneer vs. Solid Masonry — Why It Matters

| | Solid multi-wythe masonry | Brick veneer |
|---|---|---|
| **Structure** | The wall itself is structural | Decorative skin over framed or block backup |
| **Thickness** | Several courses deep | One course, with an air cavity behind |
| **Water strategy** | Absorbs and dries outward | Drains down the cavity and out |
| **Depends on** | Mortar and unit condition | Joints **plus** ties, flashing, weep holes |
| **Hidden failure points** | Few | Corroding ties, blocked weeps, bad flashing |
| **Typical era here** | Pre-1940 | 1960s onward — most of Homer Glen |

**A brick veneer wall is designed to leak.** That sounds wrong, but it is the design: water gets past the brick, runs down the back face, hits **through-wall flashing**, and exits through **weep holes** in the bottom course. The system works as long as that drainage path is clear.

Which leads to the single most common way veneer repointing goes wrong: **a crew mortars the weep holes shut** because they look like missing mortar. That converts a drained wall into a bucket. We leave weeps open, and we check that they are actually open before we finish.

## What Tuckpointing Does

Mortar is engineered as the **sacrificial** part of a masonry wall — deliberately softer than the brick, so it absorbs movement and moisture stress and wears out first. Replacing mortar is cheap; replacing brick is not.

**Repointing is the structural half: grinding or raking failed mortar out to a sound depth, then packing fresh mortar in layers.** Tuckpointing, as most homeowners use the term, includes tooling that joint so the repair matches the profile the house was built with. We do both as one operation.

Once joints open, water runs behind the brick face instead of off it. In a Will County winter it freezes, expands about nine percent, and works the wall apart from the inside.

## Signs Your Homer Glen Home Needs Tuckpointing

- **Mortar you can dig out with a screwdriver** — the fastest honest test.
- **Receded joints** sitting behind the brick face, having lost the depth needed to shed water.
- **Sandy grit** on sills, steps and along the foundation.
- **Step cracks** running diagonally through joints, often at corners.
- **Efflorescence** — chalky white bloom — which on veneer usually means water is not draining properly.
- **Blocked or missing weep holes** in the bottom course.
- **Spalling brick faces**, flaking or popping.
- **Rust staining** below a window — a corroding steel lintel.
- **An open-jointed chimney**, exposed on four sides and always the fastest-failing masonry on the property.

## Why Wooded Lots Change the Picture

Homer Glen's large, mature-tree lots are the village's best feature and they are hard on masonry.

**Shade means slow drying.** North and east elevations under heavy canopy stay damp for days after a storm, going into every freeze-thaw cycle saturated. It is common here to find one elevation with sound joints and the shaded side visibly failing.

**Canopy means clogged gutters.** Leaf load overwhelms gutters, they overflow, and the water runs down the wall — which is why so much local damage is concentrated in a vertical band beneath one gutter section.

**Organic growth follows the moisture.** Algae and moss on shaded brick are a symptom of the same wetness that is working on the joints.

**And the clay moves.** Will County's heavy clay swells and shrinks seasonally, which shows up as step cracking at corners and openings.
