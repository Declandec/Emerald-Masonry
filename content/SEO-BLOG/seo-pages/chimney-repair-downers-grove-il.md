Downers Grove is unusual because it contains two entirely different chimney populations, and homeowners rarely know which one is on their roof.

**Population one: true masonry chimneys.** The pre-war housing near downtown, the historic streets around Maple Avenue, and the enormous stock of 1950s and 60s ranches and split-levels. Solid brick, firebrick firebox, clay flue tiles, poured concrete crown.

**Population two: brick chase veneers.** The subdivisions built from the 1970s onward. What looks like a brick chimney is a **wood-framed chase clad in brick veneer**, wrapped around a factory-built metal fireplace with a round metal flue. There is no crown — there is a **sheet metal chase cover**. There is no masonry firebox — there is a steel one with a manufacturer's plate inside.

They look nearly identical from the driveway. They fail completely differently. **And the wrong diagnosis produces the wrong estimate.**

## How to Tell Which One You Have

Two places to look, both free:

**At the top.** A masonry chimney terminates in a poured concrete crown, several inches thick, sloped to shed water, with clay flue tiles projecting through it. A chase terminates in a flat metal lid — the chase cover — usually galvanized, often rusting through and streaking the brick below with orange, with a round metal cap sitting on it.

**At the firebox.** Open the doors and look inside. Firebrick laid in courses with mortar joints, and a masonry throat above: real masonry. Smooth steel or refractory panels, a metal frame, and a UL label on a plate: factory-built.

**Why it matters:** on a chase, the brick is a **cladding**, not a structure. Water getting behind it reaches wood framing, not more masonry. A rusted-through chase cover on a Downers Grove subdivision home is not a cosmetic issue — it is water pouring straight down the inside of a wood chase, and it is the single most common cause of chimney-related rot in that housing stock. It also costs a fraction of what a homeowner braces for, because it is not a rebuild.

## The Three Water Paths

Whichever type you own, water gets in one of three ways, and they present identically from inside the house — a stain on the ceiling near the chimney, a damp smell, discoloration on the firebox wall.

1. **Through the top.** A cracked crown or a rusted chase cover lets water straight into the structure. Crowns crack because they were poured thin, poured flat instead of sloped, or poured tight against the flue tiles with no expansion joint.
2. **At the roof intersection.** Step flashing and counter flashing have to interlock and be let into a mortar joint. Failed, caulk-only, or surface-smeared flashing is probably the most common leak source we find in DuPage County, and it is often mistaken for a masonry problem entirely.
3. **Through the stack itself.** Open mortar joints and spalled, porous brick absorb water directly. Above the roofline the stack is exposed on all four sides with no wall behind it to buffer temperature swings, so it always deteriorates before the house does.

**Diagnosing which path is active — before anyone quotes a rebuild — is the entire job.** A rebuild that does not correct failed flashing leaks exactly as much as the old chimney did.

## Signs Your Downers Grove Chimney Needs Work

- **White staining or efflorescence** on the exterior brick — water is moving through it.
- **Rust streaks running down the brick** from the top — a failing chase cover, or corroding metal at the crown.
- **Crumbling, missing or receding mortar joints** on the stack above the roof.
- **Spalled or flaking brick faces**, especially on the north and canopy-shaded sides.
- **A cracked, chipped or eroded crown**, or gaps between the crown and the flue tiles.
- **Moss or dark green growth** on a stack that stays wet under heavy tree cover.
- **Ceiling stains near the chimney chase**, or a musty smell in the fireplace.
- **Flue tile chips in the firebox** — pieces of clay in the ash means the liner is deteriorating.
- **A visible gap** between chimney and house wall, or a stack that reads out of plumb.
- **Loose or fallen brick** at the base — treat as urgent.

## Why Downers Grove Chimneys Deteriorate the Way They Do

**The tree canopy.** Downers Grove takes its urban forest seriously, and mature trees are one of the village's real assets. They are also, from a masonry standpoint, a moisture problem. A chimney under heavy canopy dries slowly, stays damp for days after rain, grows moss on shaded faces, and enters every freeze cycle already saturated. That is why the north side of a Downers Grove stack routinely looks a decade older than the south side.

**Overhanging limbs** add abrasion and drop debris on the crown and cap, and they keep flashing seams wet.

**Freeze-thaw.** DuPage County crosses the freezing point dozens of times each winter. Every crossing that catches saturated brick expands the water inside it by roughly nine percent, and each cycle takes a little more of the face. The mechanism is covered in [freeze-thaw masonry damage in Chicago](/blog/freeze-thaw-masonry-damage-chicago).

**Age spread.** A 1925 chimney near downtown needs soft, lime-rich mortar matched to soft original brick; a 1958 ranch chimney was built with denser brick and Portland-cement mortar; a 1994 chase is a different animal entirely. **Mortar must always be softer than the brick it holds** — get that backwards on a pre-war stack and the brick faces spall while the joints stay perfect.

**Unused chimneys deteriorate faster.** Many Downers Grove homes converted to gas or stopped using the fireplace entirely. A stack that never gets a hot fire never dries out internally, and the flue stays cool and damp year-round.

## Our Chimney Repair Process

1. **Free on-site assessment**, including roof-level inspection where safe, identifying chimney type, crown or chase cover condition, flashing, joints, brick and plumb.
2. **Identify the active water path** — top, flashing, or stack face — and say so in writing.

4. **Repointing** — joints cut back to sound mortar, matched for hardness, color and sand, packed in lifts and tooled to the original profile.
5. **Brick replacement** for spalled units, using salvaged or matched brick.
6. **Crown repair or replacement** — a new cast crown, properly sloped, with an overhang drip edge and a bond break at the flue tiles so thermal movement does not crack it. On chases, a new custom-fabricated chase cover in stainless or aluminum, cross-broken to shed water.
7. **Flashing** — new step and counter flashing let into the joints and sealed correctly, not smeared with roof cement.
8. **Cap installation** to keep rain, snow and animals out of the flue.
9. **Rebuild where warranted** — taken down to sound masonry and rebuilt with matched units and correct mortar.
10. **Water testing and walkthrough** before we call it finished.
