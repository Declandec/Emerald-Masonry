Here is the honest framing: **cutting out brick and setting new brick is the straightforward part of this job.** Any competent mason can do it.

**Matching is the part that separates a repair you cannot find from one you notice every time you pull into the driveway** — and on Shorewood's housing stock, matching is genuinely harder than people expect.

## Why Matching Is Hard Here

Shorewood grew rapidly from the 1990s through the 2000s, on the Will County side of the Joliet area. Its housing is overwhelmingly **brick veneer from that build era**.

That creates a specific problem. Much of the brick used in 1990s and 2000s subdivision construction came from **production runs that no longer exist**. Manufacturers discontinue colors, blends change, and a wall laid in 1998 may have no current-production equivalent at all.

So "just get more of the same brick" is frequently not available — and the alternatives all require judgment.

## Four Things a Replacement Unit Must Match

| What must match | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Size** | Brick dimensions vary by era and manufacturer; an off-size unit throws the coursing and joint widths |
| **Color and texture** | The visible match — what you notice from the street |
| **Absorption** | How much water the unit takes on relative to its neighbors |
| **Hardness** | How it behaves under freeze-thaw stress beside the original units |

Most crews think about the first two. **Get those right and the last two wrong, and the repair looks perfect on day one**, then behaves differently from the wall around it through every wet-freeze cycle — a denser unit set among softer ones does not absorb or move the same way.

## When No Exact Match Exists

Three legitimate answers, in order of preference:

**Blend.** Distribute two or three close units across the repair area. The eye reads natural variation far more forgivingly than it reads a uniform patch that is slightly the wrong tone.

**Relocate original material.** Take sound original brick from a hidden elevation — behind the garage, a rear wall, below grade — use it for the visible repair, and put the imperfect modern match where nobody looks. **This is standard restoration practice and usually the best-looking result.**

**Salvage.** Reclaimed units from the same era, where the yards have them.

What we will not do is set an obviously wrong unit on a front elevation and hope you stop noticing.

## Spalling vs. Joint Failure — Which Do You Have?

The distinction determines the whole scope.

**Mortar failure is repointing. Brick failure is replacement.**

Mortar is the sacrificial layer in a masonry wall — deliberately softer than the brick, so it absorbs the stress and wears out first. When only the joints have gone, [tuckpointing](/services/tuckpointing) restores the wall and the units are untouched.

Once water has been entering long enough to saturate the **brick itself**, freezing inside it breaks the face off in layers. That is **spalling**, and a spalled brick cannot be brought back — **the fired outer skin is what made it weather-resistant, and it does not grow back.**

Most Shorewood jobs involve some of both, plus fixing whatever let the water in.

## Signs You Need Brick Replacement

- **Spalling faces** — flaking, shelling, popping or crumbling.
- **Cracked brick**, particularly cracks running through the unit rather than following the joints.
- **Loose brick** that moves under hand pressure.
- **Deep pitting**, where units have lost real depth.
- **Rust staining** below a window or garage opening — a corroding steel lintel that will need addressing alongside the brick.
- **A patch of damage** under a gutter run, below a sill, or at splash height near grade.
- **Brick debris** collecting at the base of the wall.
- **Efflorescence** on the surrounding wall, showing water is moving through.

## Our Replacement Process

1. **Free on-site assessment.** We count the failed units, take a sample for matching, and — critically — find where water is entering.
2. **Fix the cause first.** Replacing brick while a rusting lintel, failed sill or overflowing gutter keeps running guarantees a repeat.
3. **Source the match.** Current production where one exists, salvage or a blend where it does not. We show you options before proceeding.
4. **Cut out carefully.** The mortar around each unit is cut to free it, rather than attacking the brick, so surrounding units and their edges stay intact.
5. **Set the new units**, properly bedded and aligned to the existing coursing.
6. **Repoint** with mortar matched for hardness, color and sand — **Type N** for most veneer, **Type S** where conditions warrant.
7. **Tool the joints** to match the original profile.
8. **Verify weep holes** are open on veneer walls, and clean down gently. Never sandblasting or high-pressure washing.

## What Drives Cost

- **Number of units** and whether the damage is localized or spread.
- **Match difficulty** — a discontinued color is the single biggest variable on newer homes, and sourcing can affect the schedule as much as the price.
- **Height and access** — ground level versus staging or a lift.
- **Underlying cause** — a rusting lintel or failed sill adds scope beyond the brick itself.
- **Extent of accompanying joint work.**
- **Whether original material can be relocated** from a hidden elevation.

We do not quote brick replacement sight unseen — the match is most of the job, and it cannot be assessed from a description.

## What Waiting Costs

Spalling is progressive. Once a brick loses its fired face, the porous body underneath absorbs water far faster, so that unit deteriorates more quickly and **starts wetting its neighbors**. A dozen brick becomes fifty. And because spalling means water is already inside the wall, the steel lintels and ties in there are corroding on the same timeline.

There is also a sourcing argument for acting sooner: **the longer you wait, the more units you need**, and the harder it becomes to find enough matching material for a larger repair.
