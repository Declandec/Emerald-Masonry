Palos Hills is largely a **1960s and 1970s village** — split-levels, ranches, bi-levels and a substantial amount of multi-unit brick stock, built when almost every house vented its furnace and water heater into a masonry chimney.

Almost none of them do now. Over the last two decades those mid-efficiency furnaces were replaced with **high-efficiency units that vent out a side wall through PVC**, and the water heater often followed. The chimney stayed where it was, and stopped having a job.

Here is the part homeowners rarely expect:

> A chimney that has been taken out of service deteriorates **faster** than one still working. Warm flue gas used to help drive moisture out of the masonry after every rain. Without it the stack stays saturated longer, goes into each freeze-thaw cycle wetter, and loses brick faster — while nobody inspects it, because nobody uses it.

That is why on a great many Palos Hills streets the chimney in the worst condition on the block is attached to a house with a perfectly good new furnace.

## Where Palos Hills chimneys actually fail

**The crown, first and worst.** The crown is the sloped concrete or mortar cap at the top. On 1960s and 70s construction it is frequently a thin mortar wash rather than a proper crown with an overhang and a drip edge, and once it cracks, water goes straight down inside the stack. [Chimney crown versus chimney cap](/blog/chimney-crown-vs-chimney-cap) and [why the top of your chimney fails first](/blog/why-the-top-of-your-chimney-fails-first) cover the distinction.

**Spalled brick below the crown.** Once the top has been admitting water for a few years, the brick beneath saturates and starts losing faces in plates. On an unused stack this progresses noticeably faster.

**At the roof line.** Step flashing, counterflashing and the lap between them is a chronic leak source because it sits between the mason's work and the roofer's. On the wide, shallow-pitched roofs common here, a chimney on the up-slope side without a **cricket** behind it ponds water against the masonry every time it rains. See [chimney flashing leaks](/blog/chimney-flashing-leaks-chicago).

**Inside the stack, invisibly.** An abandoned flue with a cracked crown above it collects water in a space that cannot dry. That is how a chimney rots from the middle outward while the outside still looks acceptable — and how staining appears on a ceiling nowhere near the fireplace.

**Out of plumb.** Less common, but real on this housing stock. A stack that has shifted or pulled away from the wall is a structural question rather than a cosmetic one — [why brick chimneys lean and pull away](/blog/why-brick-chimneys-lean-pull-away-from-house) covers what that means.

## Repair it, shorten it, or take it down

This is the decision most Palos Hills homeowners are actually facing, and all three answers are legitimate. What matters is choosing deliberately rather than by default.

| Option | When it is the right call |
|---|---|
| **Repair** — crown, repointing, unit replacement, flashing | The chimney still vents something, or it matters to how the house reads from the street, and the stack is fundamentally sound |
| **Rebuild above the roof line** | Units spalled through, joints sandy in depth, crown failure has taken courses, stack out of plumb |
| **Reduce to below the roof line and close the roof** | Nothing vents through it any more, and it contributes nothing you want to keep |
| **Full removal** | Nothing vents through it, and the chase is also taking up interior space you want back |

The non-negotiable first step is confirming **what still uses the flue.** A furnace, a water heater, a fireplace, a wood stove — any one of them changes the answer, and this is a question for a chimney sweep or a qualified heating contractor rather than for a mason. [Removing a chimney instead of repairing it](/blog/should-i-remove-my-chimney-instead-of-repairing) covers the economics honestly, and [chimney sweep versus masonry contractor](/blog/chimney-sweep-vs-masonry-contractor) covers who assesses which half of the structure.
