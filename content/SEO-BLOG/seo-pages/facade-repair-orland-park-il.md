What is failing is everything that has been **attached to the wall since it was built.**

## The real leak path on a retail façade

> On an Orland Park retail or medical office building, most water entry we trace does not come through the masonry. It comes through discrete openings in it — canopy and awning anchors, sign-band fasteners, abandoned conduit sleeves, wall-mounted mechanical and light fixtures, and the holes left behind by tenants who moved out years ago.

Every tenant turnover is a small construction project performed on somebody else's exterior wall. Signage goes up, an awning goes on, a security camera and a card reader get run through the brick, a condensate line is punched out the back. When that tenant leaves, the hardware comes down and **the holes stay.** Over twenty years and four tenants, one bay can accumulate dozens of unsealed penetrations, each one sitting directly above a ceiling somebody will eventually complain about.

This is why sealing the wall does not stop the leak. A breathable sealer treats the brick face; it does nothing about a half-inch hole. Worse, a film-forming coating over a wall full of open penetrations traps the water that got in behind it.

So on every Orland Park façade we assess, **penetration and anchor closure is a scoped, counted line item** — not an incidental. Our guide to [anchoring into brick for awnings, handrails and signs](/blog/anchoring-into-brick-awnings-handrails-signs) covers how it should be done in the first place.

## Three more conditions specific to this stock

**Long unjointed runs.** A big-box or strip elevation can run a hundred feet or more without a movement joint. Fired brick expands irreversibly over its first decades, and an unjointed run relieves that growth at a corner or an opening. That is why these buildings crack in the same place after every repointing. See [why brick buildings need expansion and movement joints](/blog/masonry-expansion-joints-why-brick-buildings-need-them).

**Dissimilar-material transitions.** Brick to EIFS, brick to cast stone, brick to metal panel. Each pair moves at a different rate and was detailed with sealant on day one. Sealant has a service life; the transition does not stop moving when it expires. We re-detail these with proper joint geometry and backer rod rather than caulking over the previous bead. Where thin manufactured stone is involved, [manufactured stone veneer failure](/blog/manufactured-stone-veneer-failure-repair-chicagoland) is worth reading first.

**Parapet and coping on a flat roof.** Same as everywhere else in Chicagoland, and still the most under-inspected element on the building, because you cannot see it from the parking lot.

## What an assessment has to produce

An Orland Park façade assessment is worth paying attention to only if it produces a **document**, because an owner, a lender, a board or a regional manager cannot act on a walk-and-talk. Ours produces:

1. **A mapped condition record** — conditions located on an elevation sketch, not described in general terms
2. **Located, dated photographs** tied to that map
3. **Quantities with the assumptions stated** — linear feet of joint, unit counts, penetration counts, coping footage
4. **A risk-ranked priority order** — overhead hazard, then active water entry, then accelerating deterioration, then appearance
5. **An explicit can-wait list**, so deferral is a decision on the record rather than an omission
6. **Access and staging assumptions**, because that is where two bids stop being comparable

[How to read a masonry assessment report](/blog/how-to-read-masonry-assessment-report) covers what to look for in anyone's version of that document, including ours.

## Inspecting from the wall, not the parking lot

A façade cannot be assessed from grade with a pair of binoculars. Brick has to be **sounded** to find units that have delaminated behind an intact face. Joints have to be **probed** to find out whether the mortar is sandy at a quarter inch or solid, which is the difference between full repointing and spot work. Coping joints have to be opened. Penetrations have to be counted at arm's length.

That means access — a lift over the parking field, or staging on one elevation at a time. [Scaffolding versus lifts on commercial masonry](/blog/scaffolding-vs-lifts-commercial-masonry) covers the trade-offs, and we resolve the choice at the assessment so it is priced in the scope instead of appearing later as a change order.
