Here is the useful way to think about it.

**Efflorescence is a receipt.** Water entered your masonry, dissolved mineral salts inside it, travelled to the surface, and evaporated — leaving the salts behind as a chalky white bloom. Every deposit is documentation that **water moved through that specific part of the wall.**

Which means the stain is genuinely helpful. It is telling you where to look.

## Why Cleaning It Off Solves Nothing

Homeowners scrub it, and it comes back next spring, and they scrub it again.

That loop continues because **the deposit is downstream of the actual issue.** Until the water path changes, the wall keeps performing the same process: absorb, dissolve, migrate, evaporate, deposit.

Worse, the water doing that is the same water that drives **freeze-thaw damage** — dozens of crossings of the freezing point each DuPage winter, each one expanding moisture trapped inside saturated masonry. So a wall that keeps efflorescing is a wall that is also, quietly, spalling its brick faces and opening its joints.

**Treat recurrence as a diagnosis, not a chore.**

## Reading Where the Water Is Coming From

| Where the efflorescence is | Most likely source |
|---|---|
| **A vertical band down one section** | Leaking or overflowing gutter seam above |
| **Directly below a window** | Failed sill that has lost slope or joints |
| **Bottom two or three courses** | Splash-back at grade; winter road salt |
| **Around a chimney** | Cracked crown or failed flashing |
| **Interior basement wall** | Grade, downspout discharge, water in soil |
| **Spread across a shaded elevation** | Slow drying plus deteriorated joints |
| **At the base of veneer** | Blocked weep holes trapping cavity water |
| **On new mortar after a repair** | Curing water — usually temporary |

That last row matters: **fresh mortar commonly blooms during curing** and typically diminishes over the first months. If it persists past that, it has stopped being a curing artifact.

## Signs Worth Acting On

- **Recurring white bloom** in the same place year after year.
- **Efflorescence alongside spalling brick faces** — water has been in there a while.
- **Interior basement walls** with powdery deposits.
- **Damp or musty basement air** with no plumbing source.
- **Mortar joints receded or raking out** on the affected elevation.
- **Staining that appears after every heavy rain** rather than persistently.
- **Blistering paint** on a coated wall — trapped moisture.
- **Heavy crusty deposits low on the wall** — often salt from winter de-icing.

## Sealing: When It Helps, and When It Hurts

We should be direct, because this is the service most often sold to people who do not need it.

**A breathable, penetrating sealer** reduces how much water the brick face absorbs while still letting vapor escape outward. Used correctly, on a wall whose joints are sound, it is genuinely useful.

**A film-forming coating or paint** creates a surface skin that blocks vapor. Moisture that gets in behind it — through a failed sill, a chimney, a gap at a window — cannot dry, so it freezes inside the brick and **spalls the face off from behind.**

| | Breathable penetrating sealer | Film-forming coating / paint |
|---|---|---|
| **How it works** | Penetrates, repels liquid water | Skin on the surface |
| **Vapor** | Passes through — wall still dries | Blocked — moisture trapped |
| **On Chicagoland masonry** | Appropriate where warranted | **Causes the spalling it was meant to prevent** |
| **Failure mode** | Gradually stops repelling; reapply | Blistering, peeling, brick spalling from behind |

**And no sealer of any kind fixes open joints.** Water enters primarily through failed mortar, not through the brick face. Sealing a wall with deteriorated joints seals the water in.

## Our Process

1. **Free on-site assessment.** We map where the deposits are, read what that pattern says about the water path, and check joints, sills, flashing, chimney, gutters, downspouts, grade and weep holes.
2. **Correct the water source.** Repointing, sill repair, flashing, caulking, gutter and grade correction — whatever the assessment identifies.
3. **Clean appropriately.** Dry brushing for light deposits; masonry-specific cleaners for heavier ones, with the wall pre-wetted and thoroughly rinsed. **Never sandblasting or high-pressure washing**, which strip the fired face off brick and make absorption permanently worse.
4. **Let it dry and reassess.** Salts already inside the wall can keep surfacing for a while after the source is closed.
5. **Seal only if warranted**, with a vapor-permeable product, after a test patch.

## Prevention

- **Keep gutters clear and downspouts extended** well away from the wall — the highest-return item by a wide margin.
- **Slope grade away** from the foundation and control splash at the base.
- **Repair sills, chimney crowns and flashing** promptly.
- **Repoint failing joints** with mortar matched softer than the brick.
- **Keep weep holes open** on veneer.
- **Never paint absorbent brick** or apply a film-forming coating.
- **Watch where the bloom appears** — it is a free annual diagnostic.
