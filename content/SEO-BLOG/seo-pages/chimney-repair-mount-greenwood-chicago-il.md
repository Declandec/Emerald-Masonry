## The Problem Nobody Mentions: Gas Conversions and Unused Flues

Here is the issue we see most often in 60655 and almost never see discussed.

A very large share of Mount Greenwood fireplaces have been **converted to gas inserts or logs**, and a large share of the rest simply are not used anymore. Both situations quietly accelerate chimney deterioration, for two reasons.

**Gas burns cooler and wetter.** A gas appliance produces flue gases with far more water vapor and much less heat than a wood fire. Those gases cool as they rise and **condense inside the flue**, wetting the masonry from the inside. On a chimney sized for a wood fireplace, the flue is often oversized for the gas appliance, which makes the cooling and condensation worse.

**Nothing dries it out.** An actively used chimney gets periodically heated, which drives moisture out of the masonry. A converted or abandoned chimney never gets that. It absorbs water from rain, snow and condensation and simply stays damp — going into every one of the dozens of freeze-thaw cycles Chicago delivers each winter.

**The result: unused and converted chimneys in Mount Greenwood frequently deteriorate faster than the ones people are still burning wood in.** If your fireplace has been gas or unused for fifteen years, that stack deserves a look regardless of how the rest of the house is doing.

## Why Chimneys Fail Before the Walls Below Them

Every other brick wall on your house has a roof over it and a heated interior behind it. A chimney has neither. It stands above the roofline, exposed on four sides, taking wind-driven rain from every direction with nothing warm behind it to dry it out.

Water enters at the top — through a cracked crown or an open flue — soaks into the masonry, freezes, expands roughly nine percent, and works the joints and brick faces apart from the inside. Repeat that dozens of times a winter for sixty-odd years, which is exactly how old most Mount Greenwood chimneys now are, and the outcome is predictable.

## The Chimney Repair Ladder

Knowing which rung your chimney is on is most of the decision. Each step up costs meaningfully more than the one below.

| Repair | What that part actually does | When it is the right call |
|---|---|---|
| **Chimney cap** | Covers the flue opening — keeps rain, snow and animals out | Any open or unused flue; nearly always worth it |
| **Crown repair or rebuild** | The sloped slab on top that sheds water off the masonry | Hairline to moderate crown cracking |
| **Flashing replacement** | Seals the joint where the chimney passes through the roof | Ceiling stains near the chimney chase |
| **Repointing** | Restores the mortar joints holding the stack together | Receded or sandy joints, sound brick |
| **Brick replacement** | Swaps out spalled and flaking units | Localized face damage on a sound stack |
| **Rebuild above roofline** | Takes the stack down and relays it | Lean, deep joint failure, widespread spalling |

An honest contractor tells you which rung you are on. One who quotes the top of the ladder every time is selling rather than diagnosing.

## Signs Your Mount Greenwood Chimney Needs Attention

- **White efflorescence staining** on the stack — direct evidence water is moving through the masonry.
- **Spalling brick faces**, flaking or popping, worst on the north and west sides.
- **Mortar joints that rake out** under a screwdriver, or that have receded behind the brick face.
- **A cracked or crumbling crown** at the top.
- **Rusted, lifted or tar-patched flashing** at the roofline — tar patching is a temporary fix somebody applied years ago.
- **Water stains** on ceilings or in the attic near the chimney chase.
- **A visible lean**, or a gap opening between the chimney and the house.
- **Brick or mortar debris** in the gutters, on the roof, or in the yard.
- **Rust or staining inside the firebox**, or a damper that no longer moves — often the first interior sign of a wet flue.
- **Daylight visible** through the stack from the attic.

## Mortar Matters: Matching the Era

Mount Greenwood's post-war brick was laid with **harder Portland-based mortar** and denser, machine-made face brick. That is the opposite situation from a pre-1930 chimney built with soft lime mortar and softer brick.

The governing rule is the same either way: **mortar must be softer than the brick it holds**, so the joints take the stress and the brick does not. But the correct mix to satisfy that rule is completely different on a 1958 ranch than on a 1908 Victorian. A mix too soft for post-war brick erodes out of the joints early; a mix too hard for older brick spalls the faces off. We match hardness first, then color, sand and joint profile.
