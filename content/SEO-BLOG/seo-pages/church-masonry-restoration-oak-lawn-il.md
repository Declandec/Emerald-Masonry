Most masonry advice for religious buildings is written for a single, very old, very tall church. **That is not what a parish in Oak Lawn usually owns.**

What you own is a **campus** — a church, a school, a rectory, often a hall or convent — and on a great many Oak Lawn parishes those buildings went up inside a narrow post-war window as the village built out. Same era, same brick, same mortar, same details, same exposure. Two consequences follow, and they shape everything about how the work should be scoped.

## Your buildings age as a cohort

When four buildings are constructed within a few years of each other using the same materials, they do not fail one at a time in a convenient rotation. **They reach the end of their mortar's service life together.**

That is why a parish that has deferred masonry for a decade suddenly seems to need everything at once, and why a single-building estimate is almost never the right document. It also reframes the question a council should be asking. Not *"is something wrong with the church"* — but *"what stage is this whole campus at, and in what order do we take it."*

Practically, a cohort campus produces:

- **The same failures on every building**, which means quantities from one building predict the others
- **A shared efficiency** — one mobilization across multiple buildings costs less per building than four separate visits
- **A single mortar and brick match** that can be established once and carried across the campus
- **A phasing problem**, because everything qualifies at once and the budget does not

## The school calendar is a hard constraint, not a preference

This is the part that separates an Oak Lawn parish scope from a commercial one, and it is the most common scheduling failure we see in other contractors' proposals.

**Work over an occupied school is a different job than work over an empty one.** Overhead masonry near doors, windows, walkways and the yard means controlled access, protected routes, dust and noise during instruction, and a much tighter safety envelope. All of it is doable. None of it is cheap or comfortable.

So the practical sequencing on a parish campus is:

- **School building scopes are written to the summer window** — mobilized, executed and demobilized inside it, with the scope sized honestly to what fits rather than optimistically
- **Church, rectory and hall work fills the school year**, where access is easier and the schedule is dictated by services and events rather than by classes
- **Weekend and holiday liturgical schedules** are mapped before staging is planned, not after
- **Anything urgent overrides all of it** — a parapet shedding material over a school entrance does not wait for June

A proposal that schedules school masonry for October has either not asked or not listened.

## What actually fails on a post-war parish campus

**Towers and steeples.** The tallest, most exposed masonry you own, weathered on every side with no adjacent building mass sheltering it. Towers need attention well before the walls beneath them, and access is the dominant cost driver.

**Parapets and copings.** Flat and low-slope roofs across school and hall buildings mean long parapets weathered on both faces and the top. The coping is the roof of that wall; open coping joints let water straight into the core.

**Steel lintels.** Long spans over school windows and gymnasium or hall openings. Corroding steel expands and lifts the courses above it, drawing a horizontal crack across the opening. Rust staining down the brick is the early warning.

**Limestone and cast stone.** Sills, water tables, entrance surrounds, cornerstones and trim. Stone fails at the wash when it loses pitch and drip edge and starts feeding water back into the wall.

**The base courses.** Splash line, snow banked against the wall, de-icing salt from walks that get salted heavily because children use them. On a school building this band is frequently the worst masonry on the campus.

**Entrance surrounds.** The most-used, most-photographed, most-salted masonry on the property, and usually the most deferred.

## How we scope a campus

1. **Walk every building**, at grade and from a ladder or lift, and sound the masonry rather than reading it from the parking lot.
2. **Record it building by building and elevation by elevation**, with quantities.
3. **Rank the whole campus by risk** — overhead hazard, then active water entry, then accelerating deterioration, then appearance.
4. **Establish one match** for mortar color, texture and joint profile, and one brick and stone match, using a sample panel.
5. **Write separately priced phases** aligned to the school calendar and to the funding cycle, so a council votes on a real number rather than an allowance.
6. **Carry quantities forward** so phase three is not re-estimated from scratch in three years.

Where the campus is repetitive, we will often scope one building completely and use it to calibrate the rest — real opened-wall quantities instead of a guess.

## Materials and method

**Mortar stays softer than the unit it holds.** On older masonry that means soft, lime-rich mixes; a hard modern mortar in a soft wall pushes stress into the face of the brick and spalls it. On post-war brick, **Type N** for most of the work, **Type S** only where exposure or structural conditions genuinely call for it.

Joints are **cut out to roughly two and a half times their width** and packed full — never skimmed over failed material. Color and profile are matched on a cured sample. Cleaning, where it is in scope, uses the **gentlest method that works**; sandblasting and high-pressure washing permanently strip the weathered skin off limestone and soft brick and make the wall more absorbent than it started.

## What drives the cost

**Height and access** first, especially on a tower or steeple. Then the **proportion of limestone and cast stone to brick**. Then **parapet and coping condition**, **how much steel** is in the scope, whether the work must **compress into a summer window**, and what is found once the first wall is opened. We price scope, state exclusions plainly, and put in writing how hidden conditions are handled before anyone signs.
