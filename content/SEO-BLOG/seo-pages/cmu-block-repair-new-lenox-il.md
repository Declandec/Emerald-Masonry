## Block is not brick, and it does not fail like brick

Three properties drive everything.

**It is porous.** Concrete masonry units absorb far more water than fired face brick. **Split-face block** — the textured finish on most 1980s-and-later commercial elevations — is worse, because splitting the unit exposes its open aggregate structure to the weather. Water does not need a crack to enter a split-face wall; it moves through the field of the block.

**It shrinks.** Fired brick expands slightly over its life. Concrete block does the opposite — it shrinks as it cures and dries. That is why block walls are built with **control joints**: deliberate vertical breaks that let the wall move without cracking wherever it chooses. Brick needs expansion joints, block needs control joints, and they are not interchangeable.

**It is frequently structural.** On a warehouse or flex building the block is often load-bearing rather than a veneer on a frame. That changes what unit replacement involves and raises the stakes on anything resembling movement.

## What fails on New Lenox block buildings

### Control joint sealant

The first thing to check, every time. Hardened, torn, or debonded sealant turns a designed movement joint into a designed leak. Cheap to fix, disproportionately damaging when ignored.

### Joint deterioration

Bed joints erode on exposed elevations the same way they do on brick, and the south and west faces go first.

### Spalling and face loss

Freeze-thaw taking the face off units, concentrated at the base of the wall where splash and de-icing salt collect, and around openings.

### Cracking

**Vertical or stepped** cracking generally points to movement or shrinkage the control joints did not absorb — often because there were too few, or badly placed. **Long horizontal** cracking running with the coursing is a different signal: the wall is being pushed, and that is a structural question before it is a masonry one.

### Coatings that trap moisture

Many block buildings have been painted. A film-forming coating on a porous wall blocks vapour from leaving, so moisture that gets in behind it — and it always does — has nowhere to go but into the freeze-thaw cycle. Blistering paint on a block elevation is a symptom, not a paint problem.

### Dock and grade-level damage

Impact from trailers and bumpers, plus salt and plow damage at the bottom courses every winter. Predictable, and far better planned for than reacted to. See [vehicle impact and loading dock masonry damage](/blog/vehicle-impact-bollards-loading-dock-masonry-damage).

## How we repair it

1. **Find the water path before pricing the wall.** Control joints, bed joints, the field of the block, penetrations, coping and roof edge above, flashing. On a block building the entry point is frequently above the stain and never inside it.
2. **Repoint deteriorated joints** with mortar matched in type and profile.
3. **Repair and reseal control joints** — remove the failed material entirely, clean the joint faces, install correctly sized backer rod, and tool a new bead. Caulking over an old bead does not adhere and fails within a season.
4. **Replace spalled and failed units**, matched as closely as block allows.
5. **Repair cracks appropriately to their cause**, and escalate to a structural engineer where the pattern indicates movement.
6. **Address trapped-moisture coatings** where that is what is happening.
7. **Seal the field of the wall** with a breathable penetrating product where it genuinely benefits — after the repairs, not instead of them.
