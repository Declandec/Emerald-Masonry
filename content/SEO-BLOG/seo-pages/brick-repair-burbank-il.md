## The Same Band on Every Block

Stand at the end of a Burbank street and look at the bottom of the walls. You will very often find a horizontal band of spalled, flaking brick starting near grade and stopping a foot or two up, with sound brick above it.

That band has three overlapping causes, and every one of them is about water and where it lands.

**Splash-back.** Rain running off a roof edge, or out of an overflowing gutter, lands on the walk, driveway, stoop or patio and bounces. That spray wets the lowest courses over and over, and because those courses are closest to the ground they dry the slowest.

**Road and walk salt.** Southwest Cook County uses a lot of de-icer, and Burbank's tight lot spacing puts drives, walks and stoops right against the masonry. Salt dissolves in meltwater, enters the brick, and crystallizes inside the pore structure as it dries. Those crystals exert pressure that pops the face off from within.

**Ground moisture.** Soil, mulch and turf against the wall wick moisture upward into the lowest courses continuously through the wet season.

Then Chicagoland freeze-thaw finishes it. Water in saturated brick freezes, expands about nine percent, and lifts the face off in flakes. Repeat that dozens of times a winter for six decades and you get exactly the band you are looking at.

**The band is at a consistent height because the splash line is at a consistent height.** Same era, same setbacks, same roof overhangs, same result.

## The Other Burbank Pattern: Hard Mortar

There is a second failure signature common on this housing stock, and it is worth knowing because it changes the repair.

Many Burbank houses have been repointed at least once since the 1980s, frequently with a mortar mix considerably harder than the original. **Mortar is supposed to be softer than the brick it sits against** — it is the sacrificial component, designed to fail first, because a joint can be replaced and a brick face cannot.

When the joints are harder than the brick, that relationship inverts. Movement, moisture and freeze pressure that should be relieved in the joint get forced into the brick faces instead. The tell is unmistakable: **crisp, intact grey mortar joints standing slightly proud of brick faces that are crumbling and spalled.** If you see that, replacing the brick alone will produce the same failure again.

## Signs You Need Brick Repair

- Brick faces flaking, sheeting, or crumbling — spalling
- A horizontal band of damage near grade, at the splash line
- Damage concentrated beside a downspout, or under a gutter joint
- Cracked or broken individual units, often from impact
- Brick you can move, or that sounds hollow when tapped
- Mortar joints standing proud of receded brick faces
- White efflorescence — proof water is moving through the wall
- Rust staining and a horizontal crack above a window or garage door, which means a corroding [steel lintel](/services/lintel-repair)
- Stair-step diagonal cracking, which means movement rather than weather
- Stoop and step treads spalling at the nosing, or courses separating

## Risks of Waiting

A spalled brick has lost its hardest, most weather-resistant layer. What is exposed behind it is more absorptive, so it takes on water faster and the following winter does more damage than the last. Damage spreads outward through neighbouring units for exactly this reason.

Water passing through open faces and joints also reaches whatever steel is in the wall and any structural framing behind it. And the cost curve is steep in a specific way: scattered unit replacement is straightforward, but once a section has lost bond it becomes a rebuild, and a rebuild on an upper elevation brings staging costs that dwarf the masonry itself.

## Our Brick Repair Process

1. **Diagnose the cause** — splash and salt, impact, corroding steel, or structural movement. These are four different jobs.
2. **Sound the surrounding masonry.** Tapping finds units that have lost bond behind faces that still look acceptable; those come out too, and this is what makes an estimate honest.
3. **Check the existing mortar hardness**, because a hard-mortar wall needs a different plan.
4. **Source the match** — size, colour range, texture and face treatment, verified against the wall in daylight.
5. **Cut out damaged units** by removing surrounding mortar and extracting the brick without disturbing the courses above.
6. **Correct what caused it** — downspout and splash management, grade, blocked weep holes, or corroded steel.
7. **Set replacement brick** in matched mortar, keyed to the existing coursing and bond.
8. **Blend rather than block** — scatter replacements through the surrounding field so the eye reads a wall, not a rectangle.
9. **Repoint the transition and any hard-mortar joints** that are driving the failure.
10. **Clean down** and review the finished work with the owner.
