The important thing about sealant is knowing what it is *not*.

**Sealant is not mortar.** A mortar joint holds masonry units together and is not supposed to move. A sealant joint exists precisely because two things *will* move relative to each other — differently, seasonally, forever. Those are opposite jobs, and swapping one for the other is the single most common masonry cover-up we find on Park Ridge homes: **a bead of caulk troweled into deteriorating brickwork** so it looks repaired.

It is not repaired. It is sealed shut, with the water still inside.

## Where Sealant Genuinely Belongs

- **Window and door perimeters** — the gap between the frame and the masonry opening.
- **Control and expansion joints** — deliberate vertical gaps in a masonry wall that let it expand and contract without cracking.
- **Material transitions** — masonry to wood trim, masonry to siding, masonry to metal.
- **Around penetrations** — hose bibs, electrical, gas lines, dryer and exhaust vents, light fixtures.
- **Coping and cap joints**, where a stone or metal cap meets what is under it.
- **At the top of a sill** where it meets the window unit — but not underneath it, where the water needs to get out.

## Where Sealant Is the Wrong Answer

- **In a mortar joint.** That is a [tuckpointing](/services/tuckpointing) repair.
- **Over a crack in a brick or stone unit.** That is a unit repair or replacement.
- **Across weep holes.** Those are drainage, not gaps.
- **Under a sill's drip edge**, where it blocks the drainage path.
- **Over an active leak** whose source has not been found — sealing the exit does not stop the entry.

## Why Most Sealant Fails Early

Sealant failure is usually installation, not product. Four causes account for nearly all of it.

### No backer rod
This is the big one. Backer rod is the foam that goes in behind the sealant, and it does two things: it sets the depth, and it **prevents the sealant from bonding to the back of the joint.** A bead bonded on three sides cannot stretch — it tears the first time the joint moves.

**A correct sealant joint is bonded on two faces only, and is thinner in the middle than at the edges** — the hourglass profile. That shape is what lets it stretch.

### Wrong depth-to-width ratio
Too deep and it cannot flex. Too shallow and it has no material to work with. Backer rod is what sets this correctly.

### Caulking over old caulk
The new bead is only as good as what it is stuck to. Old sealant has to come out.

### A dirty or damp joint
Dust, chalked masonry, or moisture in the joint all prevent adhesion, and the bead releases in sheets a season later.

## Signs Your Park Ridge Home Needs Sealant Work

- Sealant that is **cracked, split down the middle, or pulled away** from one face.
- Beads that have gone **hard, chalky or brittle** and no longer compress.
- **Gaps you can see daylight or a draft through** at a window perimeter.
- **Water staining** on interior sills, or drafts at the perimeter of windows in winter.
- **Open vertical control joints** in the field of a brick wall.
- Sealant **smeared across brick faces** or into mortar joints — a prior cover-up.
- Failed sealant around **vents, hose bibs and other penetrations**.

## Our Process

1. **Assess the joint** — determine whether it is genuinely a sealant joint, and what failed.
2. **Full removal** of old sealant; no beading over.
3. **Clean and prepare** the substrate so the new sealant can bond.
4. **Backer rod** sized to the joint, installed to the correct depth.
5. **Prime** where the substrate requires it.
6. **Install the sealant** in a continuous bead, no voids.
7. **Tool it** to the hourglass profile — this is what makes it durable, not just neat.
8. **Verify the water path** afterward, so we have not sealed drainage shut.

## What Drives Cost

- **Linear footage** and the number of openings.
- **Joint size** — wider and deeper joints use more material and larger rod.
- **Removal effort** — old, hard, over-layered sealant is slow to get out.
- **Access and height** — ground level versus ladder versus lift.
- **Substrate prep** — chalked or previously painted masonry needs more work.
- **Color matching** and whether multiple colors are needed on one elevation.
- **Whether it is combined** with other masonry work on the same mobilization, which lowers the per-foot cost.
