## What commercial tuckpointing in Evergreen Park, IL actually involves

**Tuckpointing** — properly, **repointing** — is cutting deteriorated mortar out of the joints to a sound depth, typically two to two and a half times the joint width, and packing new mortar back in lifts before tooling it to match the existing profile. The mortar is meant to be the sacrificial, replaceable part of the wall. Leave it too long and the damage moves into the brick, then the steel.

The building stock here makes it a commercial job rather than a big house job. Evergreen Park's apartment buildings — vintage **courtyard** buildings, three-storey **walk-ups**, and corner blocks with shops at grade along **95th Street** and the **Kedzie** and **Western** corridors — share the same construction as their neighbours across the city line in Beverly and Mount Greenwood: multi-wythe solid brick, face brick on the street, softer **Chicago common brick** on the sides and rear, parapets, steel lintels over every opening, and limestone sills and copings. [Chicago common brick and why it crumbles](/blog/chicago-common-brick-why-it-crumbles) explains why the rear wall is usually a cycle ahead of the front.

## The scope: quantities, not adjectives

A proposal that says "tuckpoint building as needed" is a number with no job attached. A commercial scope should let you check the price. Ours states:

| Line item | How it is measured |
|---|---|
| Wall area | Square feet, **per elevation** |
| Joint cut-out | **Percentage** of joints per elevation, or **lineal feet** where failure is localised (parapets, piers, under sills) |
| Cut-out depth | Minimum depth stated — 2 to 2½ × joint width |
| Mortar | Type (usually Type N or a lime-rich O on soft common brick), colour and sand match, sample panel |
| Brick replacement | **Unit count**, with salvage or matched new brick named |
| Lintels, sills, coping | Itemised separately — never buried in "repointing" |
| Access | Swing stage, mast climber, lift or pipe scaffold, and where it stands |

That table is also how you make three bids comparable. ### Spot repointing or full elevation

The honest test is a probe, not a sales pitch. If the mortar is sound a quarter inch in and failure sits in bands — the top courses, under a leaking scupper, along a rear stair — **spot repointing** by the lineal foot is legitimate. If a screwdriver goes in easily across most of an elevation, you are paying for a full elevation whether you buy it now or in pieces. See [spot repointing versus full tuckpointing](/blog/spot-repointing-vs-full-tuckpointing).

## Working on an occupied building

Nobody moves out for tuckpointing. The job has to run around people, and on a courtyard building the courtyard *is* the front door. Our standard on an occupied Evergreen Park property:

- **Written tenant notice** before work reaches each elevation — dates, hours, what to clear from balconies, porches and window sills, and a contact who answers
- **One protected entrance open at all times**; when the courtyard entry is in the work zone, we schedule around it or build covered passage
- **Dust and noise windows**: grinding within agreed hours, the active elevation's windows closed, vacuum-shrouded grinders, and debris bagged daily
- **Storefront hours respected** on mixed-use blocks — ground-floor work early or after close, sidewalk protection kept clear of shop doors
- **Parking and alley access** coordinated so rear-loading tenants and garbage collection are not blocked

## Phasing commercial tuckpointing in Evergreen Park by elevation

Deterioration is never even. On most of these buildings the **south and west** faces, the parapets and the rear common-brick walls are a full cycle ahead of the street elevation. We price each elevation as its own line so an owner can fund the weather faces and parapets this season and **defer the rest as a documented decision** — with a note of roughly how long it can wait — instead of guessing. A phase plan also keeps one face of the building quiet while another is staged.

## What drives the price

No honest contractor can give a number without walking the wall. These are the variables that move it:

1. **Square footage and storey height** — a three-storey courtyard building has a lot of wall around a small footprint
2. **Percent of cut-out** — 20% and 80% are different jobs on the same wall
3. **Access** — tight gangways, courtyards and alley setbacks dictate swing stage, mast climber or scaffold
4. **Brick replacement count** and how hard matching brick is to find
5. **Hidden steel** — rusted lintels and corroded parapet anchors found while cutting out
6. **Mortar specification** and whether a sample panel is required
7. **Working-hour restrictions** and protection on an occupied, mixed-use block

## Between jobs: what an owner should actually do

Tuckpointing is the cheapest masonry repair there is, and the cheapest version is the one done before it spreads.

- **Walk the building every spring and fall** — look for receding joints, sand at the base of walls, efflorescence and rust bleeding from lintels
- **Keep gutters, scuppers and downspouts working**; most localised joint failure sits right under a water problem
- **Check parapets and copings** after any hard winter — they fail first and leak into top-floor units
- **Log tenant leak complaints by elevation and unit**; the pattern tells us where to probe
- **Never let anyone caulk over mortar joints** — it traps water and makes the real repair harder
