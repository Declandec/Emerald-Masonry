## What church masonry restoration actually covers on a Berwyn campus

On this building stock, church masonry restoration is a set of related repairs rather than one service:

- **Tuckpointing and repointing** of failed mortar joints, specified to the existing wall rather than to a standard mix
- **Tower and belfry work** — the tallest, most exposed, most access-driven masonry on the property
- **Parapet and coping repair or rebuilding**, including the stone or terra-cotta caps that are usually the first real leak path
- **Limestone and cast-stone trim** — sills, water tables, entry surrounds, buttress caps
- **Lintel repair and replacement** over tall nave and classroom window openings
- **Brick repair and replacement** where faces have spalled through, matched by unit and colour
- **Stabilization scopes** for closed or partly used buildings

## Why a Berwyn parish tower fails before the walls beneath it

A bell tower has no neighbours. The nave wall below it is sheltered by the roof mass, shaded part of the day and protected at the base. The tower is weathered on four faces, takes the full driving rain off the west and south, and carries the campus's most complicated stonework at its least reachable point.

> On nearly every Berwyn parish property we assess, the tower and its coping are a full repair cycle ahead of the church walls below it — which is why a campus scope that starts at ground level in the order the buildings are numbered will spend its first phase in the wrong place.

The tower is also the element most likely to shed material onto a public walkway. That combination — worst condition, highest consequence — is why it usually leads the ranking even though it is the most expensive elevation to reach.

## The mortar rule that decides whether the repair lasts

Berwyn's parish buildings are, for the most part, **solid masonry with soft brick and lime-rich mortar** — not the veneer-over-frame assembly of a 1990s building. On that wall the joint is a designed sacrificial element, meant to be *softer than the brick it holds* so that movement, salt and moisture relieve themselves through the mortar, which can be replaced, rather than through the brick face, which cannot.

Repoint that wall with a hard, Portland-heavy modern mix and the relief path closes. The next few freeze-thaw seasons push the stress into the brick instead, and faces begin popping off in sheets around joints that still look perfect.

So the specification comes before the price. We identify the existing mortar's character, specify a mix compatible with the brick in front of us, record it in the written scope, and hold it across every later phase so the work still matches in year six. ## Stabilization is a real scope, not a euphemism for neglect

This is the part no ranking competitor page in Berwyn addresses at all, and it is the decision most parishes here are actually facing.

If a school building is closed, partly leased, or used for storage and a few meetings, restoring its masonry to match the church is often indefensible stewardship. Leaving it alone is worse — a neglected building deteriorates on an accelerating curve, and a parapet nobody is looking at is the one that drops brick onto the walk. Our guide to [vacant and partly used masonry buildings](/blog/vacant-boarded-buildings-masonry-deterioration-chicago) covers that curve.

A proper **stabilization scope** sits between the two and is written, priced and documented like any other:

1. **Stop the water at the top.** Parapets and copings made genuinely watertight, because almost all accelerating deterioration in a mothballed masonry building enters from above.
2. **Remove the hazard.** Anything loose, undermined or displaced over an entrance, walkway or public sidewalk comes down or gets secured now, not in a later phase.
3. **Close the openings properly.** Boarded openings detailed so they shed water and allow the wall to dry, rather than trapping moisture behind them.
4. **Repoint selectively.** Only the elevations and bands where joint loss is actively admitting water — typically the weather elevations and the bottom courses.
5. **Document the condition.** Dated, located photography and a written condition record, so in three years the council is re-checking a baseline instead of starting over.

That keeps a building from becoming an emergency while a congregation decides its future. It is a legitimate answer, and it belongs in writing where a council can vote on it.

## Access is the cost driver in Berwyn, and it belongs in the scope

Berwyn is dense. Parish buildings sit on or near the lot line, share walls or narrow gaps with neighbours, face the public sidewalk directly, and are frequently reachable only from an alley or the parking apron. Cicero, just east, is the same. That has three consequences a bidder has to price:

- **Staging plan.** Pipe staging, a lift, or a swing setup — and where it can physically stand on a site with no side yard.
- **Pedestrian overhead protection.** Where an elevation is directly above a public walk, protection is not optional and is not cheap.
- **Sequencing around use.** These buildings are in use every weekend, and often on weekdays too.

We resolve all three at the assessment and write them into the scope, because **access assumptions are where two masonry bids on the same church stop being comparable.** If one contractor priced a lift off the alley and another priced full pipe staging with a sidewalk canopy, the cheaper number is not the better deal — it is a different job.
