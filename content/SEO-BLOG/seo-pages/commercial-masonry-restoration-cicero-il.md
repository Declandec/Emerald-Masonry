## The Constraints That Actually Set the Schedule

| Site condition | What it changes |
|---|---|
| **Zero lot line, no setback** | Pipe staging off the wall or the walk; a boom lift often will not fit or cannot be positioned |
| **Alley-only rear access** | Staged deliveries, no bulk drops, and the alley must stay passable for tenants and refuse pickup |
| **Sidewalk under the work** | Overhead protection and a protected walkway are the first thing built, before any cutting |
| **Shared party walls** | Ownership and access agreed before scheduling — the repair frequently involves both buildings |
| **Occupied units above retail** | Phase by elevation, notice to residents, noisy work scheduled around tenants where daylight allows |
| **Courtyard plan** | The courtyard becomes the staging yard, which means resident access is planned around it |
| **On-street parking only** | Permitting, staging footprint and material handling all take longer |

None of that changes what the wall needs. It changes how long it takes and what it costs to get to it — which is exactly the part most estimates leave out until it is too late to plan around.

## Parapets and Lintels: Where Cicero Scopes Concentrate

Two elements drive most commercial scopes here, and both are directly over a public way.

**Parapet walls** are exposed on both faces and the top, get no heat from the building behind them, and take water straight into the top of the wall once the coping joints open. On a storefront that wall stands over the sidewalk, which makes its condition a safety question rather than a maintenance one. The decision is always repair or rebuild, and it turns on how far down the deterioration runs — repointing a parapet whose top courses have lost their bond is money spent on a wall that needs to come down.

**Storefront and window lintels** on this stock are bare steel that has been quietly corroding behind a sign band for fifty years. Corroding steel expands to several times its volume, which lifts the masonry above it — the horizontal crack over an opening is the symptom, the steel is the cause.

## Responding to a Municipal Notice

If a facade notice or a failed inspection is what brought you here, the sequence that works is straightforward:

1. **Get the masonry assessed and documented in writing**, with photographs and measured extents.
2. **Confirm with the Town exactly what your notice requires** and by when — those requirements are theirs to define, not a contractor's to assume.
3. **Address the safety items first.** Anything that could shed masonry over a sidewalk or entrance is both the cited risk and the right first spend.
4. **Phase the rest** where a full facade is not fundable in one season, with the phasing plan in writing.

What we provide is the accurate condition record and a scope that maps to it. What we will not do is tell you a repair satisfies a requirement we have not read.

## Our Restoration Process

1. **Survey every elevation, the parapet and the roof edge** — joints, units, lintels, sills, coping, flashing, sealant, weeps and any signs of movement.
2. **Sound and probe.** Drummy brick and hollow areas do not appear in photographs taken from the sidewalk.
3. **Plan the access first** — staging method, overhead protection, alley and walk management, resident and tenant notice.
4. **Rank the scope** by public safety, then active water entry, then advancing deterioration, then preventive work.
5. **Repoint and rebuild** — joints cut to sound depth, repointed in mortar matched for color, texture and hardness; failed units replaced with matched brick; parapets rebuilt where deterioration has gone through.
6. **Lintels, sills and openings** — corroded steel replaced and flashed, sills restored to shed water.
7. **Block and dock repairs** — control joints, impact damage, jambs and corners.
8. **Sealant joints**, then **seal exposed elevations last**, once mortar has cured.

## Materials

Soft, lime-rich mortars for pre-1930s common brick; Type N, and Type S only where the units genuinely warrant it, on harder modern brick and block; matched sand and pigment for color; salvaged and reclaimed units where a match is not currently produced; non-staining sealants where limestone or cast stone is involved; breathable penetrating repellents rather than film-forming coatings that trap moisture in a wall.
