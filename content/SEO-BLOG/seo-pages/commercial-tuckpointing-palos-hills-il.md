## Your wall is a veneer over block, and that changes the scope

The multi-unit stock built across Palos Hills through the 1960s and 1970s is overwhelmingly **brick veneer over a concrete block backup wall.** From the parking lot it reads as a solid brick building. It is not.

What you actually have is a **drained cavity assembly**:

- A single four-inch **brick veneer** on the outside
- An air **cavity** behind it
- **Wall ties** anchoring the veneer back to the CMU backup
- **Through-wall flashing** at the base, at floor lines and over openings
- **Weep holes** — the small open head joints letting caught water back out
- **Shelf angles** — steel ledges carrying the veneer at each floor line on anything over one story

That assembly is **designed to let water through the brick.** Wind-driven rain passes the face and the joints, runs down the back of the veneer, hits flashing, and exits through the weeps. Water in, water out.

Which means the honest answer to "why is my building still leaking after we tuckpointed it" is usually this: **the brick was never the barrier, so restoring the brick did not restore the barrier.**

### The four failures that live behind the veneer

**Shelf angles.** Steel corrodes, and rust occupies several times the volume of the metal it came from. Trapped between the veneer and the floor structure, that expansion lifts the courses above it. The result is a **horizontal crack running a long distance at one consistent height** — one at the second floor line, another at the third. Owners are told this is settlement. It is not. Settlement does not draw a level line across an entire elevation.

**Through-wall flashing.** On buildings of this era it is frequently short, unturned, or absent at the floor lines entirely. Water that reaches it drains **into** the wall rather than out of it.

**Weep holes.** We find them mortared shut by a previous repointing crew who thought open joints looked unfinished, buried under decades of raised mulch beds, or packed solid. A blocked weep converts the cavity from a drain into a reservoir.

**Wall ties.** Corroded or missing anchors let the veneer begin to bow away from the block behind it. Bowing is not a repointing condition — it is a rebuild condition, and it needs to be caught while it is still measured in fractions of an inch.

## Balconies and open stair towers: the leak nobody scopes

This is the second thing that separates a Palos Hills multi-unit building from a house, and it is the single most common source of interior complaints we are called about.

**Exterior balconies and open stair towers penetrate the veneer at dozens of points.** Every railing post, every slab edge, every stringer connection, every threshold is a hole through a drained wall, and each one depends on sealant and flashing rather than on masonry. Sealant has the shortest service life of anything on the building — commonly fifteen to twenty years — and on a great deal of this stock it has never been replaced once.

So the pattern is: residents on the balcony side report water; the association tuckpoints the elevation; the complaints continue; everyone concludes masonry work does not fix leaks.

A commercial tuckpointing scope for a building like yours should explicitly say what happens at:

- Balcony and walkway **slab edges** where they meet the veneer
- **Railing and post penetrations** through masonry
- **Stair tower** stringer and landing connections
- **Door thresholds** and frame perimeters at open-air corridors
- The **sealant joints** at every one of them

If a proposal you are holding does not mention those, it priced a wall, not a building.

## What we actually scope on a Palos Hills multi-unit property

1. **Walk every elevation of every building**, from grade and from a ladder or lift, and sound the veneer rather than eyeballing it.
2. **Map the failure**, elevation by elevation and building by building, with quantities — not "the complex needs tuckpointing."
3. **Find the floor lines.** Locate shelf angles, check for the horizontal cracking that says the steel is working, and probe where flashing should be.
4. **Check the weeps.** Open, blocked, or never installed determines whether this is a joints job or a drainage job.
5. **Inventory the penetrations** at balconies, stairs, thresholds and equipment.
6. **Write a scope a manager or board can bid.** Line items, quantities, unit pricing for the repeated work, and an explicit statement of what is excluded.
7. **Pilot one building** where the complex is repetitive, then apply calibrated quantities to the balance.

That last step matters more than anything else on a repetitive property. When ten buildings went up in the same eighteen months with the same brick, the same mortar and the same details, **one building tells you what the other nine will cost** — with real opened-wall quantities instead of an allowance.

## The repointing itself

Where the joints genuinely are the work, it is done properly or it is done twice:

- **Cut out to depth** — roughly two and a half times the joint width — with grinders on bed joints and hand tools where the units are soft. Never a skim over failed material.
- **Match the mortar** to the wall. **Type N** for most veneer of this era; **Type S** only where exposure or structural conditions genuinely call for it. Mortar stays softer than the unit it holds.
- **Match color, texture and joint profile**, tested on a sample panel and reviewed dry before the elevation proceeds.
- **Re-establish weeps** — open or re-drill at proper spacing rather than leaving the cavity sealed.
- **Cure and protect.** Kept damp in heat, protected and heated in cold, or scheduled for a workable window.
