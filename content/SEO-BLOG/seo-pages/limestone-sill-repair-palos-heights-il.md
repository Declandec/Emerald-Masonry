## What a sill is actually doing

A sill has two jobs, and both are about moving water away from your wall.

**It sheds.** The stone is pitched outward so water runs off rather than sitting on it or tracking back toward the window.

**It breaks the flow.** A properly detailed sill has a **drip edge** — a groove or projecting lip on the underside — that stops water wrapping around and running back onto the brick. Water will not cross a clean drip edge. It lets go and falls clear.

Lose either function and every drop hitting that window starts going somewhere it was never meant to go.

## How sills fail here

**Delamination.** Limestone is a sedimentary stone laid down in layers. Water gets between them, freezes, and lifts the stone apart in sheets — a flaking, layered face rather than a clean break.

**Spalling.** The face pops off in patches, typically where water sits longest.

**Cracking through the section.** Often across the middle of a longer sill, sometimes accelerated by a steel lintel corroding nearby or by movement below.

**Lost slope.** Old sills settle, and well-meant repointing with a fat bed of mortar can level one out entirely. A flat sill is a shelf that holds water.

**Bridged drip edge.** Repeated painting, a heavy parge coat, or a bead of caulk run along the underside will bridge the groove and route water straight back to the brick. This one is common and completely avoidable.

**Open end joints.** Where the sill meets the brick jamb at either end is the single most common water entry point on the assembly, and the easiest to overlook.

## How we repair a stone sill

We do the smallest repair that genuinely solves it.

1. **Assess the stone and the water path.** Is the pitch intact, is the drip edge clear, are the end joints open, and what has the brick below been taking?
2. **Repoint** the end joints and bed joint with matched mortar, where the joints are the failure.
3. **Patch** localised spalling with a stone-appropriate repair mortar, tinted and textured on site, where the unit is otherwise sound.
4. **Dutchman repair** where one portion has failed but the rest is good — the damaged section is cut out and a matched piece let in.
5. **Replace the sill** where it has cracked through or delaminated across its depth, setting the new unit with proper pitch and a real drip edge.
6. **Repair the brick below** that the failed sill has been feeding — usually the reason you called.
7. **Repoint and clean down.**
