## Four Eras, Four Different Chimneys

| Era | Typical stack | What it needs |
|---|---|---|
| **19th century** | Tall, often multi-flue, soft brick, lime mortar | Soft lime-based repointing; hard mortar destroys it |
| **Early 1900s** | Victorian and Foursquare stacks, corbelled tops | Lime-modified mortar, corbel rebuilding |
| **Post-war** | Shorter single-flue, Portland mortar, denser brick | Type N; crown and flashing work dominates |
| **Modern** | Veneer chimneys, prefab chases | Flashing, cap, crown; sometimes chase-cover issues |

**The governing rule never changes: mortar must be softer than the brick it holds**, so the joints take the stress instead of the brick faces. But the mix that satisfies that rule on an 1880s stack is not the mix for a 1985 one. Get it backwards on old brick and the faces spall within a few winters — permanent loss of original material.

## Why Chimneys Fail First

Every other brick wall on your house has a roof over it and a heated interior behind it. A chimney has neither. It stands above the roofline exposed on four sides, takes wind-driven rain from every direction, and has nothing warm behind it to dry it out between storms.

Water enters at the top — through a cracked crown or an open flue — soaks into the masonry, freezes, expands roughly nine percent, and works the joints and brick apart from the inside. Kane County delivers dozens of those freeze-thaw crossings each winter, with no lake buffer to soften them.

## The Repair Ladder

| Repair | What that part does | When it applies |
|---|---|---|
| **Chimney cap** | Covers the flue opening against rain, snow, animals | Any open or unused flue |
| **Crown repair or rebuild** | Sloped slab shedding water off the masonry | Crown cracking or crumbling |
| **Flashing replacement** | Seals the chimney-to-roof joint | Ceiling stains near the chase; rusted or tar-patched metal |
| **Repointing** | Restores the mortar joints | Receded or sandy joints, sound brick |
| **Brick replacement** | Swaps spalled units | Localized face damage |
| **Corbel / top rebuild** | Restores decorative top courses | Damage concentrated in projecting brickwork |
| **Rebuild above roofline** | Takes the stack down and relays it | Lean, deep joint failure, widespread spalling |

Knowing which rung you are on is most of the decision, and each step up costs meaningfully more than the one below.

## Signs Your Batavia Chimney Needs Work

- **White efflorescence staining** on the stack — water is moving through the masonry.
- **Spalling brick faces**, flaking or popping, worst on the north and west sides.
- **Mortar joints that rake out** under a screwdriver, or have receded behind the brick.
- **A cracked or crumbling crown** at the top.
- **Rusted, lifted or tar-patched flashing** at the roofline.
- **Water stains** on ceilings or in the attic near the chimney chase.
- **A visible lean**, or a gap opening between the chimney and the house.
- **Brick or mortar debris** in gutters, on the roof, or in the yard.
- **Deteriorated corbelling** on an older stack — missing or crumbling units in the decorative top courses.
- **Daylight visible** through the stack from the attic.

A lean or falling debris on a tall stack over a walkway is a safety matter, not a spring project.

- **Which rung of the ladder** the stack is actually on.

- **Number of flues** — a wide multi-flue stack is substantially more masonry.
- **Decorative corbelling** — rebuilding stepped brickwork to the original profile is slower, more skilled work.
- **Mortar matching** — a custom lime blend takes more work than a bagged mix.
- **Extent of spalling** and salvaged brick sourcing.
- **Season** — cold-weather work requires protection and adds time.
