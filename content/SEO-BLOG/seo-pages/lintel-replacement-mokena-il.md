## Rust does not thin steel. It expands it.

This is the mechanism, and it is the reason lintel failure is so destructive relative to how little steel is involved.

Corroding steel converts to iron oxide, which occupies **several times the volume** of the metal it came from. A lintel sits under a load of masonry with a window or door frame beneath it, so as it swells it cannot go down. It goes **up**, lifting the wall it was installed to carry.

The trade calls it **rust jacking**, and it leaves an unmistakable signature.

### What it looks like

- A **straight horizontal crack** in the mortar joint directly above an opening
- **Rust staining** bleeding down the masonry below that joint
- The **course above the opening lifted** out of plane with the rest of the wall
- **Cracked or displaced units** at the corners of the opening, sometimes stepping diagonally away
- A **visible steel edge** flaking or delaminating in layers at the head
- On wide spans, a **measurable sag** across the opening
- Doors and windows gone **out of square** at the head

## Why commercial openings fail worse

Residential lintels span a window. Commercial lintels span **storefronts, overhead doors, and dock openings** — often twelve, sixteen, or twenty feet.

Three consequences follow:

**More load.** A wide span carries far more masonry, so section loss shows up as visible movement sooner.

**More consequence.** The masonry above a sixteen-foot overhead door is a substantial quantity of material sitting above a route people and equipment use constantly.

**More water.** Storefront and dock openings are typically the wettest details on a building — gutter discharge, splash-back off a walk or apron, snow melt piling against the head, and canopy and signage penetrations directly above.

## How the work is done

1. **Assess the whole elevation, not the one crack.** Every opening on that wall is the same age, from the same steel, in the same exposure.
2. **Shore the masonry** above the opening before anything is removed.
3. **Take down the masonry above the lintel** carefully, salvaging units for reuse.
4. **Cut out and remove the corroded steel.**
5. **Set new protected steel**, correctly sized for the span and properly bedded at both bearing ends.
6. **Install flashing and a drip edge** above the opening where the detail allows, with end dams so water leaves the wall rather than running into it.
7. **Relay the masonry**, reusing salvaged units and blending replacements for size, colour and texture.
8. **Repoint** with matched mortar, seal where sealant belongs, and clean down.

## Materials

Correctly sized, protected steel. Mortar matched in type — **Type N** or **Type S** as the wall genuinely calls for — and matched in colour and joint profile so the repaired head does not read as a patch. Salvaged brick reused wherever sound, because nothing blends with an existing wall like the units already in it. Flashing detailed continuous with end dams, because flashing that stops short simply drains into the wall.

## Why it should not be deferred

A lintel is a structural member. Once the steel has lost section, the masonry above the opening is being carried by something that is no longer what it was designed to be — and it is directly above a door.

Deferral also grows the scope. A lintel caught early is a lintel, shoring, and a few courses. A lintel left five more winters is frequently a lintel plus a substantially larger rebuilt section, and on a storefront that means more of the elevation coming down and going back up.
