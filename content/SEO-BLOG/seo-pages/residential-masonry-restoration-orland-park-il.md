Restoration is not an upsell — done properly it is usually **cheaper across five years** than four separate repairs, because setup, access and staging get paid for once instead of four times, and because repairs stop being undone by problems nobody looked at.

## The Order That Matters

If you take one thing from this page, take this sequence. It is how we prioritize every scope we write.

**1. Anything actively letting water in.** Chimney crown and flashing, failed sills, open mortar joints on weather-beaten elevations, bad caulking at window perimeters. Water is the mechanism behind essentially every other item on the list.

**2. Corroding steel.** Rust staining below a window means a **steel lintel** is corroding and expanding inside the wall. It gets worse every winter and it eventually lifts the brick above the opening.

**3. Damage the water already caused.** Spalled brick, deteriorated joints, failed sills. Real repairs — but pointless before steps 1 and 2.

**4. Protection.** Breathable sealing where it is warranted, after joints are sound.

**5. Cosmetic.** Matching, cleaning, appearance work.

**Phasing by what is most visible is the expensive way to do this.** The spalled brick on the front elevation is what you notice; the chimney crown you cannot see from the ground is what is causing it.

## What Orland Park Homes Actually Need

Orland Park's housing is mostly **1970s through 2000s brick veneer** — subdivisions across 60462 and 60467, larger homes toward the west and south, with an older core around **Old Orland** near 143rd and Beacon.

**Veneer fails at points, not across the field.** A single decorative brick layer over a framed or block backup, drained by flashing and weep holes. So on a 1990s Orland Park home the field brick is often perfectly sound while these specific elements are failing:

- **The chimney** — exposed on four sides above the roofline, no heated interior drying it. Always the fastest-failing masonry on the property.
- **Lintels over wide openings** — the era's large picture windows, sliders and two- and three-car garage doors are long spans carrying real load.
- **Sills** — horizontal surfaces that hold water once they stop shedding it.
- **Flashing and weep holes** — poorly detailed during construction, or later mortared shut by a crew that thought weeps were missing mortar.
- **Isolated joint sections** under a leaking gutter or on the shaded north elevation.

**In the Old Orland core**, the masonry is genuinely older and needs soft **lime-based mortar** rather than a hard modern mix. The governing rule is that **mortar must be softer than the brick it holds** — a hard mortar on soft older brick drives freeze-thaw stress into the brick faces and spalls them within a few winters.

## Signs Your Home Needs a Full Assessment

- **Rust staining** running down from a window, door or garage opening.
- **A horizontal crack** in the joint directly above an opening.
- **Spalling brick faces** — flaking, popping or crumbling.
- **Mortar you can rake out** with a screwdriver, or joints receded behind the brick face.
- **White efflorescence** on the brick — water is moving through the wall.
- **A cracked chimney crown**, rusted or tar-patched flashing, or staining on the stack.
- **Water stains inside** near exterior walls or the chimney chase.
- **Blocked or missing weep holes** at the bottom course.
- **Step cracks** running diagonally through joints at corners.
- **More than one of the above** — which is the actual signal that you need a scope, not a repair.

## What Waiting Costs

The compounding problem with masonry is that every deferred item makes the next one worse. Open joints wet the brick; wet brick spalls; the same water corrodes the lintels and ties inside the wall; a failed sill soaks everything below it. By the time a homeowner calls about the thing they finally noticed, the scope is usually three times what it would have been two winters earlier.

That is the argument for assessing the whole envelope once, even if you only fix part of it this year. **Knowing the list is free. Discovering it one item at a time is not.**
