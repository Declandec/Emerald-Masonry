Almost all the storm-claim advice available in this market was written about roofs. That is not a criticism — roofs get hit more often and the process is well established. But it produces a real problem, because **a masonry claim does not work like a roof claim**, and following roof advice on a brick chimney loses claims.

Here is the difference in one sentence.

> On a roof claim the argument is usually whether the event caused the damage. On a masonry claim the argument is almost always whether the wall was **already failing** — and in Chicagoland, on a brick wall, the carrier's default assumption is that it was.

That assumption is frequently fair. Most brick problems around here genuinely are cumulative: freeze-thaw, open joints, water. Which means the burden sits on the evidence, and the evidence has to be gathered before anybody tidies up.

## What storm damage masonry repair actually covers

- **Chimneys and chases** — displaced or toppled stacks, cracked crowns, torn flashing, impact damage
- **Wall damage** from fallen limbs, wind-driven debris or vehicle strike
- **Parapets and coping** displaced by wind on flat-roofed buildings
- **Limestone and cast stone** — cracked, spalled or dislodged sills, bands and caps
- **Fire and smoke damage** to masonry
- **Emergency stabilisation** — removing or securing anything overhead that could still fall
- **Matched rebuilding** with units and mortar that read as original

## What actually distinguishes event damage from deterioration

This is the section that decides claims, and it is the one the roofing-led pages have no reason to write.

A mason reading a wall for a claim is looking for **whether the failure surface is fresh**:

- **Fracture faces.** A brick broken by impact shows a clean, bright, unweathered interior. A brick that spalled over five winters shows a face that has been exposed, dirtied and eroded.
- **Mortar on displaced units.** Brick knocked loose by a limb carries mortar that broke cleanly and is still sharp at the edges. Brick that fell because its joints turned to sand comes away with mortar you can crumble in your hand.
- **Directional pattern.** Wind and impact damage has a direction and a focus — a cone of damage, a line of displacement, a strike point. Freeze-thaw damage is distributed by exposure and moisture, concentrating at the base of the wall, below sills and around downspouts.
- **Impact evidence.** Bark, abrasion, paint transfer, crushed arrises, a scar on the coping.
- **What the rest of the elevation looks like.** If the whole wall is spalling and one area is displaced, both stories are true — part of the loss is the event and part is not. Saying so plainly is more credible than claiming everything.

## The first hour matters more than the first week

1. **Secure underneath it.** Cordon generously — the next piece may not land where the first one did. Close the entrance or the walk if it is beneath the damage.
2. **Photograph before anything moves.** Wide and tight, from several positions, dated. Nobody sweeps up until this is done.
3. **Keep the fallen material.** The brick itself is evidence, and its fracture face is the single most useful thing in the file.
4. **Call your carrier** and open the claim before repairs begin.
5. **Get a mason out** the same day if anyone walks beneath the damage. Day one is about hazard, not repair.
6. **Then stabilise** — hazard removal and protection, which on masonry is not a tarp.

## Matching is a sourcing problem, not a purchase

On a roof, like-kind replacement means the same shingle in the same colour. On masonry it frequently means a brick that has not been manufactured for forty years.

That has to be solved in the scope, before the rebuild is priced. There are three routes and they are not equivalent in cost:

| Route | When it works |
|---|---|
| **Salvage brick** from a reclamation source | Common Chicagoland units, where age and colour can be matched |
| **Close-match current production** | Where size, colour range and texture line up acceptably |
| **Relocate original brick** from a hidden elevation, filling that area with new | The best visual result on a prominent elevation, and the most labour |

[Brick matching and replacement material](/blog/brick-matching-replacement-material) and [salvaged brick sourcing in Chicago](/blog/salvaged-brick-sourcing-matching-chicago) cover how each is done. A scope that says "replace damaged brick" without naming the matching route is a scope that will produce either an argument or a patch you can see from the street.

The same applies to mortar. The new joints must match in colour, sand texture and profile, and — the governing rule on any older wall — the mix must be **softer than the brick it holds**.

## Code and ordinance: ask before the rebuild is priced

When a chimney, parapet or wall section is substantially rebuilt rather than patched, current requirements may apply to the replacement. That can add scope that the loss estimate did not anticipate, and whether a policy responds to it is a coverage question worth asking your carrier **early** rather than discovering at invoicing.

We flag it in the scope whenever a rebuild crosses that line. It is not our call to make, but it should never be a surprise.
