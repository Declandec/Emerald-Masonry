## The first question is not "what does it cost"

It is **"who is responsible for what."**

On most Illinois associations the exterior masonry — walls, parapets, lintels, chimneys — is a **common element**, funded by the association and controlled by the board. Balconies, patios, and sometimes exterior doors and windows are frequently **limited common elements**, where responsibility splits between the association and the unit owner in whatever way the declaration says.

That split is not standard. It genuinely varies building to building, and it is the single most common source of friction on association masonry projects. Read the declaration first, and where the language is ambiguous, get an opinion before the scope is written rather than after an invoice arrives. Our guide to [HOA versus unit owner responsibility for masonry repair](/blog/hoa-vs-unit-owner-who-pays-masonry-repair) covers the usual arrangements.

## What actually fails on Downers Grove association buildings

**Mortar joints on the weather elevations.** South and west walls run years ahead of north and east on the same building — which is why a sensible plan often treats two elevations now and two later rather than wrapping every building at once.

**Lintels and shelf angles.** The steel above windows, doors and garage openings corrodes, expands, and lifts the masonry it carries. On brick-veneer buildings from the 1970s onward, a continuous **shelf angle** behind the wall does the same thing invisibly. See [brick veneer ties and shelf angles](/blog/brick-veneer-ties-shelf-angles-hidden-steel).

**Balcony slab edges and guardrail anchorage.** Overhead, load-bearing on people, and deteriorating from the inside out as embedded steel corrodes. This outranks everything cosmetic. Our guide to [balcony, walkway and guardrail masonry](/blog/balcony-walkway-masonry-condo-multifamily-chicago) goes through the inspection.

**Parapets and roof edges** on the flat-roofed buildings, weathered on three surfaces with no heated space behind them.

**Grade-level courses**, where splash, road salt, and raised landscaping grade do their work every winter.

**Chimneys and flues** on the older garden-style courtyard buildings.

## Downers Grove, specifically

Downers Grove has one of the deeper association housing stocks in DuPage, and it splits into recognisable groups.

**The 1960s–80s condominium and townhome communities** along the Finley Road, Butterfield, and 63rd Street corridors — brick veneer over frame or block, steel lintels now well past their service life, balconies and walkways, and flat or low-slope roofs with parapets. This is the bulk of the work.

**Garden-style courtyard buildings** in the older neighbourhoods nearer downtown and the BNSF line — solid masonry, tall chimney stacks, and mortar that is genuinely due.

**Newer townhome and rowhome developments**, where the issues are sealant renewal, expansion joints, weep holes, and flashing rather than wholesale repointing.

**Mixed-use and commercial condominium** along Ogden Avenue and near the downtown, where a commercial unit owner and a residential board share a building.

Four different repair vocabularies, and a scope that treats them the same is a scope that will produce surprises.

## What we give a board

The deliverable is a document, not a number.

1. **A free on-site assessment**, elevation by elevation and building by building, with photographs.
2. **A written scope** with stated square footages, the repoint-versus-rebuild split, access method, materials, and **explicit exclusions**.
3. **A priority order** the board can defend to owners: overhead and structural, then active water entry, then deterioration, then appearance.
4. **A phasing proposal** grouped by access rather than by category — because the cost of upper-elevation work is largely the cost of getting up there, and two items on one mobilisation beat the same two items in different years.
5. **Unit prices for contingent work**, fixed during the bid rather than negotiated once staging is up. Our guide to [unit price versus lump sum](/blog/unit-price-vs-lump-sum-masonry-contract) explains why that matters on masonry specifically.
6. **A mock-up requirement**, so mortar colour, joint profile and brick match are approved as a physical standard before production. See [mock-ups and test panels](/blog/mock-ups-test-panels-masonry-contract).
7. **COI, W-9 and lien waivers** on request, before mobilisation.
8. **A resident communication plan** — notice, affected parking and balconies, duration, and a named contact.

## Reserve studies, honestly

Most boards arrive with a reserve study that carries a masonry or tuckpointing line, and treat it as a budget.

It is better understood as a **planning placeholder**. Reserve studies generally estimate from building age, square footage, and typical service lives — not from a condition survey of your actual walls. The real scope, and more importantly the real *sequence*, comes from looking at the buildings.

Sometimes the study is high and the work can be staged further out. Sometimes it is low because a lintel condition nobody had catalogued moved a whole elevation up the priority list. Either way, an assessment before the money is committed is worth considerably more than it costs, which is nothing. See [HOA masonry maintenance budgets](/blog/hoa-masonry-maintenance-budgets).

## Materials and technique

Mortar matched in **type, hardness, colour, sand, and joint profile** — with softness relative to the surrounding units treated as the governing constraint on older buildings. Type N where the wall calls for it, Type S where it genuinely does. Salvaged brick reused where sound, replacements matched in size, colour and texture. Backer rod and correctly tooled elastomeric sealant at expansion joints and perimeters — sealant where sealant belongs, mortar where mortar belongs. Flashing detailed continuous with end dams so water leaves the wall rather than draining into it.

## What drives the cost

Number of buildings and elevations. Height and access — ground reach, lift, or scaffolding are three different cost structures. The ratio of repointing to rebuilding. Lintel, balcony and parapet scope. Resident protection and overhead coverage. Whether the work is phased and therefore mobilised more than once. Weather windows, since mortar needs workable temperatures to cure.
