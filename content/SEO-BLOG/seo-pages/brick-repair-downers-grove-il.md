## Mortar Failure vs. Brick Failure

The distinction that determines the whole scope:

**Mortar failure is repointing. Brick failure is replacement.**

Mortar is the sacrificial layer in a masonry wall — deliberately softer than the brick, so it absorbs the movement and moisture stress and wears out first. Replacing mortar is cheap; replacing brick is not. When only the joints have gone, [tuckpointing](/services/tuckpointing) restores the wall and the units are untouched.

Once water has been entering long enough to saturate the **brick itself**, freezing inside it breaks the face off in layers. That is **spalling**, and a spalled brick cannot be brought back — it has to be cut out and replaced with a match.

## Matching: Why It Is Harder Here

A replacement brick has to match on **four** dimensions, and most crews think about two.

| What must match | Why |
|---|---|
| **Size** | Brick dimensions changed across eras; an off-size unit throws the coursing |
| **Color and texture** | The visible match |
| **Absorption** | How much water it takes on relative to its neighbors |
| **Hardness** | How it behaves under freeze-thaw stress beside the original units |

Get the first two right and the last two wrong and the repair looks perfect on day one, then behaves differently from the wall around it through every wet-freeze cycle.

**On pre-1940 Downers Grove masonry, salvaged brick is usually the better answer than new** — not for authenticity but for physics. Salvaged units from the same era share the original's softness and absorption.

And the mortar follows the brick: **soft, lime-modified mixes on older soft brick; Type N on post-war; Type N or S on modern veneer as conditions warrant.** A hard modern mortar on soft old brick inverts the wall's design and spalls the faces within a few winters.

## Signs Your Downers Grove Brick Needs Repair

- **Spalling faces** — brick flaking, shelling or crumbling, worst near grade, below sills and on shaded elevations.
- **Cracked brick**, particularly cracks running through the units rather than following joints.
- **Loose brick** that moves under hand pressure.
- **Step cracks** climbing diagonally through the mortar joints.
- **Rust staining** below a window or door — a corroding steel lintel expanding inside the wall.
- **Receded or sandy mortar joints** that rake out under a screwdriver.
- **Efflorescence** — the white chalky bloom proving water is moving through.
- **A bulge or bow** in the wall plane — structural, needs evaluation rather than a patch.
- **Brick debris** collecting at the base of the wall.

## Why Brick Fails in Downers Grove

**Freeze-thaw is the engine.** Not extreme cold, but repeated crossings of the freezing point with water present — dozens each DuPage winter, plus more in the shoulder seasons. Each cycle expands trapped water about nine percent inside the brick.

**The tree canopy keeps walls wet.** Downers Grove has genuinely heavy mature tree cover, and shaded north and east elevations stay damp for days after rain — going into each freeze cycle saturated. It is why one elevation fails while the sunny side looks untouched.

**Canopy also means clogged gutters.** Heavy leaf load overwhelms gutters, they overflow, and damage concentrates in a vertical band under one section.

**Clay soils move.** DuPage's heavy clay swells and shrinks seasonally, producing step cracking at corners and around openings.

**And the older stock is soft.** Pre-1930 brick absorbs far more water than modern face brick and needs matching soft mortar to survive.

## What Waiting Costs

Spalling is progressive. Once a brick loses its fired face, the porous body underneath absorbs water far faster, so that unit deteriorates more quickly and starts wetting its neighbors. A dozen brick becomes fifty; fifty becomes a section rebuild. And because spalling means water is already inside the wall, the steel lintels and ties in there are corroding on the same timeline.
