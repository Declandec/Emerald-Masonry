## What Actually Fails on These Buildings

### Parapets — first, and most misdiagnosed
The section of wall above the roofline is **weathered on both faces with no heated interior drying it**, and it carries the water leaving the roof edge. That makes it the fastest-deteriorating masonry on almost every flat-roofed commercial building.

**And it is the leak owners blame on the roof.** Failed coping joints, open parapet mortar or missing **through-wall flashing** send water down *inside* the wall, where it appears on the top floor. The membrane gets replaced, the leak continues, and everyone is frustrated — because the water was never entering at the roof.

### Lintels and shelf angles
Corroding steel over storefront and window openings expands to several times its original volume, lifting the brick above and cracking the joint line. **Rust staining is the early warning**; displaced masonry over a public sidewalk is the late one.

### Coping
Cracked, displaced or wrongly sloped caps at the top of the wall let water into everything below.

### Joints and sealant
On veneer, precast and panel buildings the **sealed joints are the weather barrier**, and sealant is a wear item with a life far shorter than the structure.

### Ornamental stone and cornices
On downtown blocks and churches, the most exposed and most anchored elements — and the ones that come down onto sidewalks.

## Signs Your Elgin Building Needs Attention

- **Cracked, loose or displaced coping** along the top of the wall.
- **Open, sandy or receded mortar joints**, on the roof side as often as the street side.
- **Spalling brick or delaminating stone** on either face.
- **Rust staining** below a lintel or bleeding from a joint.
- **Efflorescence** on the facade or the wall below a parapet.
- **A visible lean, bow or bulge** in the parapet, sighted from a corner.
- **Interior water staining** on the top floor near exterior walls.
- **Failed sealant** — split, pulled away, hardened or missing.
- **Debris on the roof or sidewalk** below the wall.

Loose masonry at a roof edge above a public sidewalk is a life-safety and liability matter, not deferred maintenance.

## Mortar by Era

**The governing rule is that mortar must be softer than the units it holds**, so the joints take the stress instead of the brick or stone faces.

- **Downtown Elgin's 19th- and early-20th-century blocks** — soft **lime-based** mortar. A hard Portland mix here spalls the brick faces within a few winters, which on a historic facade is permanent loss of original material.
- **Early-1900s institutional and church masonry** — lime-modified mixes, often with ornamental stone requiring dutchman or matched patching.
- **Post-war and modern** — **Type N** or **Type S** as conditions warrant, with cast stone coping and sealant-dependent joints.

## Budgeting: Treat the Envelope Like the Roof

The buildings that handle this well fund the envelope on a cycle instead of reacting to leaks. Inspect on a schedule, prioritizing the parapet and the south and west elevations. Put joint sealant and facade repair in the reserve study as recurring capital items. Walk the building after hard freezes and storms. **Treat rust staining as urgent** — it is the earliest sign embedded steel is corroding, and steel is what turns a masonry bill into a structural one.

Deferring converts a repointing project into a repointing-plus-steel-plus-interior-restoration project.
