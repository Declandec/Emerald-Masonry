## The Four Foundations Under Downers Grove

**Rubble limestone.** The oldest stock, roughly pre-1900, common in and around the historic core near the downtown and the older tree-lined blocks. Irregular quarried stone laid up in soft, lime-rich mortar. It is heavy, it is durable, and its mortar is *supposed* to be soft — that is what lets the wall move without cracking the stone.

**Brick.** Early twentieth century. Multiple wythes of common brick below grade, often the same Chicago common brick used above it. Brick foundations do well until water reaches them repeatedly, then the below-grade faces spall and the joints wash out.

**Hollow clay tile.** Roughly 1910s through 1930s. Large hollow blocks with internal webbing. Structurally fine when dry and intact, but brittle, and once water gets inside the cells it freezes and cracks the webs. Hollow tile is the material most often misdiagnosed as block.

**Concrete block (CMU).** Post-war through modern. Uniform eight-by-sixteen units. Its characteristic failures are horizontal cracking at mid-height from lateral soil pressure, step cracking at corners, and mortar joint deterioration where water sits.

**Each of those four wants a different repair.** That is the whole point of this page.

## Where Downers Grove Foundations Get Into Trouble

Downers Grove sits on heavy DuPage clay under a mature tree canopy, and the Village has spent years on stormwater management for good reason. For masonry foundations that combination produces a specific set of problems:

- **Clay soil that swells and shrinks** with moisture, loading and unloading foundation walls seasonally.
- **Mature root systems** near century-old homes, drawing moisture unevenly under one side of a footing and driving differential settlement.
- **Downspouts discharging at the wall** — still the single most common cause of foundation masonry failure we see anywhere in Chicagoland.
- **Negative grade**, where decades of landscaping and mulch have raised soil against the house and reversed the slope.
- **Window wells without drainage**, holding a column of water directly against the wall.
- **Cold joints at additions**, where a newer foundation meets an original and the two settle differently.

Freeze-thaw finishes the job. Water saturates the masonry, freezes, and expands, which is how a damp joint becomes an open joint and an open joint becomes a missing one.

## Signs You Need Foundation Masonry Repair

- Mortar you can rake out of the joints with a finger or a screwdriver
- Sand or grit accumulating on the floor along the base of the wall
- Stones or blocks that have shifted, dropped, or rotated out of plane
- A wall that bulges inward when you sight along it
- Horizontal cracking at mid-height on a block wall
- Step cracking through the joints at a corner
- Efflorescence, the white powdery deposit left when water moves through masonry and evaporates
- Spalled, flaking below-grade brick
- Parge coat blistering, hollow-sounding, or falling off in sheets
- Active water entry at a specific joint or crack

## Risks of Waiting

A foundation wall is holding back soil and carrying the building. Eroded joints reduce the wall's ability to distribute that load, and once individual stones or blocks begin to move, the wall's geometry — not just its mortar — is compromised. Water that runs through a foundation wall also carries fines out with it, undermining the material behind the wall and accelerating movement.

The practical escalation: repointing becomes section rebuilding, section rebuilding becomes excavation and full rebuilding, and a wall that has begun to bow may require structural reinforcement that was entirely avoidable a few years earlier.

## Our Foundation Masonry Repair Process

1. **Identify the material** — stone, brick, hollow tile, or block — and the original mortar type.
2. **Find the water.** Downspouts, grade, window wells, sump discharge, gutters, irrigation, and any interior source.
3. **Assess movement.** Is the wall stable and simply eroded, or is it actively moving? These get different scopes.
4. **Correct the water source first** where it is ours to correct, and identify it plainly where it is not.
5. **Rake and clean the joints** back to sound mortar without over-cutting soft stone or fracturing hollow tile webs.
6. **Repoint with a compatible mix** — soft and lime-rich on historic stone, appropriate Type N or Type S where the material and loading call for it.
7. **Rebuild deteriorated sections**, resetting displaced stone or block on a sound bed.
8. **Parge only where the material warrants it**, using a coating compatible with the substrate rather than a hard cement skin over soft stone.
9. **Address the transition** at additions, stoops, window wells and utility penetrations.
10. **Review the finished work** and document what still needs to be watched.

## Materials and the Rule That Governs Everything

The rule is the same one that governs every masonry repair, and it is violated most often below grade: **the mortar must be softer than the masonry unit it sits against.** A high-Portland mortar or a hard cement parge on a soft rubble limestone or century-old brick foundation does not strengthen it. It concentrates stress and moisture in the stone and the brick, which are the parts you cannot replace.
