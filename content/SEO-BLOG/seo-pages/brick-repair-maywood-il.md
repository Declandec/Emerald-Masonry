## The Damage a Bad Repair Does

There is one rule behind almost all of it: **mortar must be softer than the brick it holds.**

Mortar is meant to be the sacrificial part of a wall. Masonry moves — it expands in summer, contracts in winter, absorbs water and freezes. If the mortar is softer than the brick, the joint absorbs that stress, and joints can be replaced cheaply forever. If the mortar is harder, the **brick faces** absorb it instead, and they spall, flake and shear off.

Century-old Maywood brick is soft. Modern Portland-heavy mortar out of a bag is hard. Put them together and you get the signature we see constantly on these blocks:

**A patch that looks perfect, surrounded by brick faces that are falling off.**

Other repairs we routinely have to undo:

- **Smeared face pointing** — mortar buttered over the surface of failing joints instead of the joints being cut back to sound material. It has no depth, no bond, and it traps water behind it.
- **Overcut joints** from an aggressive angle grinder, which takes the arrises off soft brick and permanently widens every joint on the wall.
- **Caulk in mortar joints** — flexible sealant used where mortar belonged, sealing water in rather than out.
- **Painted or coated brick**, applied to hide spalling, which stops the wall drying outward and accelerates the damage underneath. See [why painted brick traps moisture](/blog/painted-brick-traps-moisture-masonry-repair-chicagoland-il).
- **Mismatched replacement brick** — modern units set into a historic wall, differing in size, color and hardness, so the wall reads as patched forever and the new units behave differently in a freeze.
- **Filled weep holes**, closing off the wall's drainage. Covered in [weep holes in masonry walls](/blog/weep-holes-masonry-walls).

If any of that sounds like your building, the wall is not beyond help. It just means the scope starts one step earlier than you expected. More on the pattern in [why masonry repairs fail: contractor shortcuts](/blog/why-masonry-repairs-fail-contractor-shortcuts).

## Signs Your Maywood Building Needs Brick Repair

- **Brick faces flaking, popping or shearing off** — spalling, and the single clearest signal.
- **Bricks that crumble or powder** when pressed with a key.
- **Cracked units**, especially cracked straight through the face.
- **Step cracks** running diagonally through the mortar joints.
- **Loose or displaced brick** you can move by hand — treat as urgent.
- **Deep, receding or missing mortar joints**.
- **White efflorescence** blooming on the surface after wet weather.
- **A visibly patched area that looks worse than the wall around it.**
- **Damage concentrated in the lowest few courses**, near grade.
- **Bulging or bowing** in a wall face — a structural signal, not a cosmetic one.

## Why Maywood Walls Deteriorate the Way They Do

**Age, first.** Much of Maywood's housing and its commercial stock along Fifth Avenue and Madison Street predates 1930, with a meaningful share going back to the 1880s and 1890s. That masonry was built with soft, hand-formed or early machine-made brick and lime-rich mortar — durable materials, but ones that demand matching materials when repaired.

**Ground moisture.** Maywood sits low, with the Des Plaines River along its western edge and the forest preserve corridor beside it. Lower ground, a higher water table, and flood-plain conditions on parts of the village mean the base of a wall is working harder here than it does two towns east. That shows up as damage concentrated in the bottom three courses — the mechanism is explained in [why the bottom three courses of brick fail first](/blog/why-the-bottom-three-courses-of-brick-fail-first).

**Freeze-thaw.** Cook County crosses freezing dozens of times each winter. Saturated brick that freezes expands from within, and each cycle takes a little more of the face.

**Salt.** Sidewalk and street de-icer splashes onto the lower wall all winter, crystallizes inside the brick, and does mechanical damage independent of frost. See [road salt and de-icer brick damage](/blog/road-salt-deicer-brick-damage-illinois).

**Deferred maintenance cycles.** Maywood has a lot of long-tenured owners and budget-conscious institutions, which means repairs have often been staged over decades by different hands — hence the layers.

**Institutional and church masonry.** Between the medical campus on the west side, the schools, and a large number of older churches, Maywood has significant institutional brick and stone. That work almost always requires lime-based mortar, hand preparation and salvage matching rather than production methods.

## Repair or Replace? The Honest Rule

- **Repoint** when brick faces are sound and the mortar joints have eroded. This is maintenance, and it is the cheapest place to be.
- **Replace individual units** when a brick has lost its face, cracked through, or gone soft. Once the hard fired outer skin is gone, the brick absorbs water like a sponge and cannot be restored by coating it.
- **Rebuild a section** when displacement, bowing or widespread unit failure means the wall no longer holds together — repointing a wall that has moved does not put it back.

Most Maywood jobs are a mix: a few dozen replacement units, several hundred feet of repointing, and one small area of rebuild. We map it that way in the estimate rather than pricing the whole wall at its worst spot.

## Our Brick Repair Process

1. **Free on-site assessment**, elevation by elevation, identifying failed units, joint condition, previous repairs and — critically — where the water is coming from.
2. **Address the water source first.** Gutters, downspout discharge, grading, a failed sill, an open coping joint. Replacing brick without correcting the water means replacing it again.
3. **Remove failed units by cutting the joints, not the brick.** Neighboring units stay undamaged; sound brick gets salvaged for reuse on visible elevations.
4. **Remove damaging previous repairs** — hard mortar, smeared pointing, sealant in joints — with hand tools where the brick is soft.
5. **Source matched brick.** Salvaged period units first, matched for size, color range, texture and hardness.
6. **Match the mortar.** Hardness first, then color, sand and texture — always softer than the surrounding brick.
7. **Lay in matching bond**, with joints packed in lifts and tooled to the original profile so the repair disappears into the wall.
8. **Repoint the surrounding joints** so the new work is not an island in failing mortar.
9. **Clean down before the mortar cures hard**, protecting brick faces.
10. **Walkthrough**, with an explanation of curing — fresh mortar reads light and darkens over the following weeks.

## The Cost of Waiting

A spalled brick is an open door. The fired outer skin is what makes a brick weather-resistant; once it is gone the unit absorbs water freely, freezes, and takes its neighbors with it. One failed brick becomes a patch of six, then a course, then a section where the wall has lost enough material that repointing is no longer the answer.
