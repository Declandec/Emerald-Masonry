## Three Chimneys, Three Repairs

### The nineteenth-century stack

Aurora's oldest neighbourhoods — the near-downtown blocks on both sides of the Fox River, the historic districts, the churches, the mill-era buildings and the Victorians around them — carry chimneys built with **soft, hand-made or early machine-made brick laid in lime-rich mortar.** Often they are tall, slender, corbelled at the top, and serving flues built for coal.

These stacks fail from **hard mortar and aggressive repair** at least as often as from weather. The rule that governs them is absolute: **mortar must be softer than the brick it holds.** A Portland-heavy Type S mix on 1890s brick does not strengthen the chimney; it makes the brick the sacrificial element and spalls the faces off within a few winters. Repointing and rebuilding here means lime-rich mortar, salvaged brick where possible, and reconstructing corbelled detailing by hand.

### The post-war stack

The 1950s through 1970s build-out east and west of the river produced denser, machine-made brick laid in Portland-based mortar on ranches, Cape Cods and Georgians. These chimneys are more weather-tolerant, and they mostly fail from the top down — **crown first**, then the exposed top courses.

They also carry a specific modern problem. Many were sized for a coal or early gas furnace and now vent nothing but a water heater, or nothing at all, because a high-efficiency furnace was installed and vented out a sidewall. A cooler, wetter, much smaller exhaust in an oversized flue **condenses inside the chimney**, wetting the masonry from the inside while weather wets it from the outside. That is why an under-used chimney often deteriorates faster than a hard-working one.

### The modern chase

Subdivision construction from the 1980s onward brought **brick-veneer chimney chases** — a framed chase clad in a single wythe of brick, often with a sheet-metal chase cover rather than a masonry crown. These fail at the cover (which rusts and streaks the brick below it), at the bottom courses, at the flashing, and at joints that had drainage sealed shut.

**A repair quoted without identifying which of these three you own is a guess.**

## Why Chimneys Fail Faster Than Walls

Whatever the era, a chimney is the hardest-working masonry on a building. Above the roofline it has **four weather faces plus the top**, no roof over it, and no heated interior behind it to drive moisture out. It is the most wind-exposed masonry on the property, so it takes more driven rain and loses heat faster than any wall.

Chicagoland freeze-thaw does the rest: water in saturated brick and mortar freezes, expands roughly nine percent, and levers the material apart, dozens of times each winter. That is why deterioration on a chimney is almost never uniform — it is worst at the top and improves on the way down, and that gradient tells a mason how deep the repair has to go.

## Signs of Chimney Damage

Use binoculars from the ground:

- A **cracked, spalled or missing crown**, or a thin mortar wash sloped off the flue instead of a real crown
- **Mortar joints receding, dark or open** at the top courses
- **Spalled brick** — faces flaked or sheeted off
- **Missing brick or visible gaps** in the upper courses
- A **missing, rusted or dislodged cap**
- **Clay flue tile** cracked, chipped, or sitting proud of a broken crown
- **Dark saturation staining** or white efflorescence on the stack
- **Rust staining** at a chase cover or embedded steel
- A **visible lean**, or a gap opening between chimney and house
- **Debris in the firebox** — sand, mortar crumbs or tile fragments

Inside, check the top-floor ceiling and the attic where the chimney passes through, which is usually where a leak shows long before finished ceilings stain.

## Risks of Waiting

Chimney damage accelerates itself. Once the crown cracks, water enters the top of the stack directly instead of running off it, saturating the masonry and speeding up freeze-thaw. Open joints admit more water, which opens them further. Meanwhile that water reaches the flashing, the framing and the interior.

Structurally, a chimney that has lost its mortar is loose brick standing several feet above your roof in Fox Valley wind. Functionally, a cracked or spalled flue is no longer safely containing combustion gases from anything still venting into it — a carbon monoxide question that warrants a qualified inspection, not a wait-and-see.

## Our Chimney Repair Process

1. **Inspect from the roof**, plus the flue and the interior — not from the driveway.
2. **Identify the era and the materials**, because that sets the mortar and the method.
3. **Sound the masonry** to find where sound material actually begins.
4. **Establish the rebuild depth** and confirm it with the owner before demolition.
5. **Set up access and protection** — staging or roof jacks, roof membrane and ground protection.
6. **Take down the deteriorated section**, salvaging brick worth reusing.
7. **Inspect the flue** and advise where relining is warranted.
8. **Rebuild in matched brick and compatible mortar**, holding original coursing, bond and any corbelled top.
9. **Form a proper crown** — sloped, with a bond break at the flue tile and an overhang with a drip edge past the brick face.
10. **Repoint the sound masonry below** so the whole stack is weather-tight.
11. **Address flashing and counterflashing**, coordinating with roofing where needed.
12. **Install a cap** sized to the flue, and review the finished work.

## Materials

- **Lime-rich and Type O mixes** for pre-1900 Aurora brick
- **Type N** for most twentieth-century chimney brick
- **Type S** only where conditions genuinely warrant it
- **Salvaged original brick** wherever it is sound, since it is a better match than anything new
- **A formed concrete crown** with overhang and drip edge, not a troweled mortar wash — this is a small part of the cost and most of the service life

## What Drives the Cost

- **How far down sound masonry is found** — the single largest variable
- **Roof pitch, height and access**, and whether staging or a lift is required
- **Chimney size** — a tall multi-flue stack is a different job than a single flue
- **Crown and cap** scope
- **Flue condition** and whether relining is needed
- **Brick matching**, especially on historic or unusual material
- **Flashing coordination** with roofing
- **Season**, since mortar has curing temperature limits
