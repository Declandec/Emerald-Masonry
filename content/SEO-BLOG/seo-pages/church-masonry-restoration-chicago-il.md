## Why Church Masonry Deteriorates Ahead of Everything Around It

**Height and exposure.** Steeples, bell towers, gables and parapets stand well above the surrounding roofline, unheated, weathered on every face. They take more wind-driven rain and more freeze-thaw cycling than any masonry on the block.

**Inspection difficulty.** Nobody gets close to a tower without staging or a lift. Deterioration that would be obvious at eye level runs unnoticed for decades.

**Deferral.** Masonry competes with the roof, the boiler, the parking lot and the programme budget every single year — and it is the one that shows the least urgency until suddenly it does not.

**Original materials.** Chicago's churches are largely **soft brick and limestone laid in lime-rich mortar**, and that assembly is unforgiving of hard modern repointing. A Portland-heavy mix on hundred-year-old stone forces every freeze cycle into the stone face. Spalled mortar can be replaced; spalled 1890s limestone cannot.

## Where the Work Concentrates

- **Steeples and bell towers** — the most exposed and highest-consequence element
- **Parapets, gables and cornices** — coping joints open, water enters the top of the wall
- **Limestone bands, sills, tracery and window surrounds** — lost wash, worn drip edges, cracked and sugaring stone
- **Roofline and flashing transitions**, where masonry meets roofing and two trades meet
- **Stained-glass surrounds**, where sealant and stone have both aged past service
- **Entry steps, porticos and stoops** — high traffic, salt, and public exposure
- **Rectories, parish halls and school buildings** on the same campus, usually a different era and a different specification

## Phasing a Restoration a Council Can Actually Fund

| Phase | What it covers | Why here |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Safety** | Anything shedding masonry over a walk, entrance or roof; unstable tower or parapet elements | A public building; this is not a budget question |
| **2 — Active water entry** | Coping joints, flashing, cracked stone, open joints on exposed elevations | Every wet season enlarges the next phase |
| **3 — Advancing deterioration** | Repointing sound-but-eroding elevations, limestone repair | Far cheaper than replacement later |
| **4 — Preventive** | Sealant renewal, drainage, gentle cleaning | Protects the phases above |
| **5 — Interior and cosmetic** | Plaster and finish repairs downstream of the envelope | Only once the building is dry |

Recording the **mortar specification, sand and stone source** in phase one is what makes phase four match it four years later. Without that record, a long restoration reads as a patchwork of separate jobs.

## Our Restoration Process

1. **Assess from staging or a lift**, including the tower — not from the ground.
2. **Sound and probe** to separate what is weathered from what has lost its bond.
3. **Document by elevation** with photographs tied to locations.
4. **Rank and phase** into a written scope the council can approve and bid.
5. **Correct the water paths first** — coping, flashing, drainage.
6. **Repoint in compatible mortar**, softer than the units, matched for colour, sand, texture and profile.
7. **Repair limestone** — reform the wash, re-cut drip edges, Dutchman patches where localised, replacement only where the stone is spent.
8. **Rebuild what is past repointing** — tower courses, parapets and sections that have lost their bond.
9. **Clean gently**, never abrasively, and seal only sound masonry after curing.
