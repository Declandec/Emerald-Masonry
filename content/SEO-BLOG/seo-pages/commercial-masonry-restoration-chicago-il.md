## In Chicago, deferred masonry is a compliance problem, not just a budget one

This is the fact that changes the calculus here and does not apply the same way in the suburbs.

**The City of Chicago actively enforces exterior wall conditions.** A deteriorating façade is not only a maintenance item on a reserve schedule — it is a condition the Department of Buildings can cite. From there it can escalate toward administrative hearings and penalties that compound while the wall sits unrepaired. Separately, buildings above a certain height and construction type carry **periodic exterior wall examination** obligations, on cycles the Department sets.

Two practical consequences:

**One: confirm what applies to your specific building.** Requirements turn on height, construction and use, and they change. Ask the Department of Buildings about your address rather than relying on what a neighbor was told.

**Two: if an examination or a citation is in play, the masonry scope has to produce a record, not just a repaired wall.** That means dated pre-existing-condition photography, a written scope tied to specific elevations and quantities, documented repair methods, and a completion record. An owner who can show a dated assessment and a scheduled, written repair plan is in a materially different position from one holding a verbal quote.

We write scopes that way as a matter of course, because the same documentation that satisfies a compliance file is what protects an owner in an insurance or tenant dispute later.

## What actually fails on a mid-size Chicago building

**Parapets.** The highest-risk masonry you own — weathered on both faces and the top, with the coping acting as its roof. Open coping joints let water straight into the parapet core, where it freezes and works the wall apart from inside. Parapets are also the element most likely to shed material onto a public sidewalk, which is exactly the failure that stops being a repair and becomes an incident.

**Steel lintels and shelf angles.** Corroding steel expands as it rusts — **rust jacking** — lifting the courses above it. On a storefront that draws a horizontal crack across a display window. On a multi-story building it draws a level line the length of an elevation at a floor line, which owners are routinely told is settlement. Settlement does not draw a level line.

**Mortar joints on the weather elevation**, receded behind the face of the units.

**Spalling brick** at the base courses, where salt, splash and snow concentrate — and on Chicago common brick, which is soft, porous and unforgiving of a hard modern mortar.

**Limestone, cast stone and terra cotta** at sills, water tables, cornices and entrance surrounds — failing at the wash when they lose pitch and drip edge and begin feeding water back into the wall.

**Sealant joints** at storefront framing, windows and movement joints, decades past a fifteen-to-twenty-year service life.

**Wall ties**, where a veneer has begun to bow away from its backup. That is a rebuild condition, not a repointing condition, and catching it early is the whole game.

## Phasing by risk, not by appearance

Almost nobody funds a full façade in one year, and nobody should have to. What matters is that the order follows risk.

| Phase | What belongs in it | Why it leads |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Public safety | Loose parapet units, failing coping, spalled stone or brick above an entrance or public walk | Falling material is the only failure that becomes an incident rather than a line item |
| 2. Active water entry | Open coping, failed flashing, corroded lintels, open joints on the weather elevation | Water in a wall converts repointing into rebuilding |
| 3. Accelerating deterioration | Elevations where joints have receded past the face of the units | Quantities grow every winter this waits |
| 4. Appearance | Sound but stained, patched or mismatched areas | Real value, no urgency |

Each phase is priced separately in the written scope, with quantities carried forward so phase three is not re-estimated from scratch in three years.

## How we scope and execute

1. **Walk every elevation** — from grade, from a lift, and from the roof — and **sound** the masonry rather than reading it from the sidewalk.
2. **Resolve access first.** On a Chicago lot, staging is often the largest single cost driver and sometimes the binding constraint. Alley, gangway, sidewalk, swing stage or lift gets decided before anything is priced.
3. **Document existing conditions** with dated photographs tied to elevations.
4. **Write the scope** with quantities, exclusions, unit prices for hidden conditions, and a stated staging method.
5. **Correct the water source** — coping, through-wall flashing, weep holes, sealant, roof drainage — before or alongside the face work.
6. **Cut failed joints to depth**, roughly two and a half times the joint width, never skimming over failed material.
7. **Repoint with matched mortar** — soft, lime-rich mixes on soft historic brick and stone; **Type N** for most commercial work; **Type S** only where exposure or structure genuinely calls for it. Mortar always stays softer than the unit it holds.
8. **Match color, texture and joint profile** against a **cured** sample panel, not a wet one.
9. **Replace failed units** matched by size, color, texture and era, salvaging from concealed elevations where an exact match cannot be bought.
10. **Cure and protect**, then seal only if warranted — with a breathable penetrating sealer, never a film-forming coating that traps moisture in the wall.

On cleaning: the **gentlest method that works**, tested on an inconspicuous area. **No sandblasting, no high-pressure washing.** Both permanently strip the weathered outer skin from limestone, terra cotta and soft Chicago common brick, leaving a more absorbent surface that deteriorates faster than the one you started with.

## What drives the cost

**Height and access**, first and often largest. **Overhead protection** over a public way. **Proportion of stone and terra cotta to brick**, since stone work is slower and more skilled. **How much steel** is in the scope, because opening a wall to replace a shelf angle is a different order of work than repointing it. **Occupancy** and off-hours restrictions. **Permit and consent timelines.** And **what is found once the wall is opened** — which is why unit prices for hidden conditions belong in the bid rather than in an argument later.
