## Why Hillside Lots Fail on One Wall

On a flat lot, a foundation deals with rain that falls on the yard around it. Manageable, and it fails fairly evenly.

**On a sloped lot, the uphill wall deals with water arriving laterally through the soil** — everything that fell uphill of you, moving downhill through the ground, pressing against that wall on its way past.

Three consequences follow:

**The uphill wall stays wetter, for longer.** It is receiving water continuously during and after a rain event, not just during it.

**It carries more soil pressure.** Saturated clay on a slope pushes harder than saturated clay on the flat, and it pushes consistently from one direction.

**Damage concentrates.** Which is why so many Carpentersville homeowners describe the same thing: **one foundation wall with problems and three that look fine.**

If that is your house, the uphill wall is where to look — and the fix involves what happens to the water before it reaches that wall, not just the masonry itself.

## Reading the Cracks

- **Hairline vertical cracks.** Often shrinkage from curing; usually minor.
- **Stair-step cracks** climbing diagonally through the mortar joints. Differential settlement or soil movement — worth evaluating, especially if widening.
- **Horizontal cracks** partway up a block wall. **The one to take seriously here.** Lateral soil pressure pushing the wall inward — precisely what saturated clay on a slope does.
- **Cracks wider at one end.** Rotational movement.
- **Bowing or bulging**, visible when sighting along the wall. The wall is deflecting under load; this needs professional evaluation before anything else is scoped.
- **Cracks with displacement**, one side proud of the other. Active movement.

## Signs Your Carpentersville Foundation Needs Work

- **Mortar crumbling or washing out** from between blocks, brick or stone.
- **Step cracking** through the joints.
- **Horizontal cracking or bowing** in a block wall — especially on the uphill side.
- **Spalling and flaking** in the first courses above grade.
- **Parging falling away in sheets**, exposing the wall behind it.
- **Water entering the basement** at the wall–floor joint or through visible joints.
- **Efflorescence** — white powdery deposits on interior basement walls.
- **Doors and windows** on the level above sticking or going out of square.
- **A damp or musty basement** with no plumbing source.
- **Soil visibly saturated** against one wall long after rain has stopped.

## Why Foundations Deteriorate Here

**Slope-driven water.** Covered above, and the defining local factor.

**Clay soil movement.** Kane County's heavy clay swells substantially when saturated and shrinks when dry, pressing laterally against foundation walls seasonally.

**Freeze-thaw.** Dozens of crossings of the freezing point each winter with no lake buffer. Water inside a saturated joint expands as it freezes and works the mortar apart, concentrated at the wettest part of the building.

**Water at grade.** Splash-back, downspouts discharging beside the wall, and grade sloping the wrong way — the ordinary causes, amplified on a slope.

**River-corridor moisture.** Proximity to the Fox keeps soils and masonry damper for longer.

## Our Process

1. **Free on-site inspection.** Inside and out, reading crack patterns and checking grade, downspouts, uphill runoff and drainage — determining whether the wall is deteriorating, actually moving, or both.
2. **Diagnosis before scope.** Water management problems get identified up front, because repairing masonry while the water keeps arriving guarantees a repeat.
3. **Joint preparation.** Failed mortar raked and cleaned out to sound material.
4. **Repointing.** Mortar matched to the wall — **Type S** or **Type M** where structurally appropriate on block and below grade, softer **lime-modified** mixes on old brick and stone.
5. **Crack repair and rebuilding.** Repaired appropriately to cause; failed block or brick replaced with matched units; deteriorated sections rebuilt.
6. **Parging and protection** of the exposed portion where appropriate.
7. **Drainage recommendations** — grade, downspout extensions, and on sloped lots, intercepting water uphill of the wall.

Where a wall shows genuine structural movement rather than masonry deterioration, we say so plainly and tell you what evaluation it needs. **Not every foundation problem is a masonry problem**, and we would rather tell you that than sell repointing on a wall that is moving.

## What Drives Cost

- **Linear footage and wall height** of the section needing work.
- **Above grade versus below grade** — excavation is the single largest cost multiplier, and on a slope, access for excavation is harder.
- **Deterioration versus movement** — a bowing wall is a different scope from eroded joints.
- **Foundation type** — resetting old stone is slower than repointing block.
- **Site slope and access** — steep lots complicate equipment, spoil handling and staging.
- **Related water work** — grading, downspout extension, uphill interception, parging.
- **Interior finish** — a finished basement complicates interior-side access.
