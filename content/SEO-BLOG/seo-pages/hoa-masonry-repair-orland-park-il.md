## Start With a Survey of Every Building, Not a Price for One

Deterioration across an association is never uniform. On a typical Orland Park property, one or two buildings are meaningfully worse than the rest — usually the ones with the most exposed west elevations, the worst drainage, or a chimney that has been leaking quietly for a decade.

So the first deliverable is a **condition survey building by building**, ranked by risk rather than by address order. That gives a board three things it cannot get from a lump-sum bid:

- **A defensible order of work** — which building is phase one, and why
- **A basis for the reserve conversation** — what is coming, and roughly when
- **A comparable bid document** — every contractor pricing the same quantities

## How We Rank an Association Scope

| Priority | What it covers | Why it comes first |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Safety** | Loose or displaced brick, unstable chimney or parapet, failing lintels over entries | Anything over a walkway, entry or patio is the first dollar spent |
| **2 — Active water entry** | Open joints, failed flashing, cracked sills, blocked weeps | Water reaching a unit becomes an owner complaint and a claim |
| **3 — Advancing deterioration** | Spalling brick, eroded joints not yet leaking | Cheaper now than as unit replacement later |
| **4 — Preventive** | Sealant renewal, breathable sealing, drainage corrections | Extends everything above it |
| **5 — Cosmetic** | Cleaning, staining, appearance blending | Once the envelope is sound |

Boards get into trouble when phase one is chosen by what looks worst from the street rather than by what is actually failing. Those are frequently different buildings.

## What Fails on Orland Park Association Property

Orland Park's association stock runs from **1970s and 1980s townhome and condo developments** through **1990s-and-later brick and stone veneer** buildings, mostly two and three storeys on open suburban sites.

- **Chimneys and stacks** — the most exposed masonry on any building, and the most commonly deferred because no single owner looks at them
- **Lintels over garage and entry openings** — bare steel corroding and lifting the brick above it
- **Base courses and grade-level walls** — splash-back, snow, de-icing salt and foundation plantings holding moisture
- **Veneer details** — blocked or caulked weep holes, short flashing, failed sealant joints at windows and dissimilar materials
- **West and south elevations** — driving rain with nothing to break it up on an open site
- **Patio and balcony transitions**, where masonry meets another material and nobody owns the joint

On newer veneer buildings the brick itself is rarely the problem. The **details** are, which is why a survey has to look at flashing, weeps and sealant rather than only at mortar joints.
