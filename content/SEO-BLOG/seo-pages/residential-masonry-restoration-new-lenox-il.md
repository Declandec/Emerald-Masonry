New Lenox did not grow gradually. It grew in **waves** — a modest older core along the Lincoln Highway corridor, then subdivision after subdivision from the 1970s through the 2000s, with the heaviest building through the 1990s and early 2000s.

That matters, because masonry ages on a clock set by the year it was built. And the largest cohort of brick-veneer homes in this village is now arriving at the end of its **first service life** — not the brick, which has decades left, but the components around it that nobody thinks of as masonry at all.

## Your house is a drained cavity wall, not a brick wall

This is the single most useful thing to understand about a New Lenox house, and most homeowners have never been told it.

You do not have a brick house. You have a **framed house with a brick skin.** A single four-inch veneer stands about an inch off a sheathed, papered, wood-framed wall and is anchored back to it with metal ties. Behind it is an air cavity. At the base of that cavity, and above openings, sits **through-wall flashing** that catches water and returns it outside through **weep holes** — the small open head joints you can find every two feet or so along the bottom course.

Which means: **your wall is designed to leak.** Wind-driven rain passes the brick face and the joints. That was always the plan. The system works by catching that water and draining it back out.

So on a veneer house the failures that cause interior damage are almost never in the brick:

**Weep holes mortared shut.** We find this constantly, usually from a previous repointing crew who thought open joints looked like sloppy work and filled them. The cavity is now a reservoir.

**Weeps buried.** Twenty years of mulch beds, regrading and landscape fabric raising the soil above the flashing line.

**Sealant joints past service life.** Perimeter sealant at windows and doors, and movement joints in the veneer, commonly last fifteen to twenty years. On a 1998 house that has never been redone, every one of them is an open slot into a drained wall.

**Corroded shelf angles.** The steel ledge carrying the veneer at a floor line. Rust occupies several times the volume of the steel it came from, so as it corrodes it lifts the courses above — drawing a **horizontal crack running level across the elevation at one consistent height.** Homeowners are routinely told this is settlement. Settlement does not draw a level line.

**Corroded lintels** over windows, patio doors and garage openings, with rust staining weeping down the brick as the early warning.

**Wall ties** failing, letting the veneer begin to bow away from the framing behind it. That is a rebuild condition and it needs catching early.

**A restoration that only addresses the face of the brick has not addressed your house.**

## Will County clay, and why drainage is the cheapest fix you own

New Lenox sits on **heavy clay soil**. Clay swells when it takes on water and shrinks when it dries out, so a wet spring following a dry late summer is a full cycle of soil movement against your foundation and your masonry — every single year.

You cannot change the soil. You can change how much water reaches it next to the house, and that is consistently the highest-return work on any property here:

- **Downspouts** extended well away from the foundation, discharging onto ground that falls away
- **Gutters** kept clear, because overflow sheets water down a wall all season
- **Grade** falling away from the house for the first several feet
- **Soil and mulch** kept below the base course so weeps stay open and brick is not in contact with wet clay
- **Sump discharge** running away from the house, not back toward it
- **Sprinkler heads** not aimed at the brick

That list costs a fraction of any masonry scope and protects everything in it.

## Scoping and phasing a whole house

Almost no New Lenox house needs everything at once. Masonry fails by exposure, and the honest priority order is:

1. **Anything letting water in** — a failed lintel, an open chimney joint, blocked weeps, failed sealant at a window that is already staining drywall inside
2. **The chimney**, which is exposed on four sides and the top and weathers far faster than the walls below it
3. **The base courses and splash line**, where snow, de-icing salt and rain splash concentrate
4. **The weather elevation** — usually west or northwest facing, where joints have receded behind the brick face
5. **The shaded elevation**, which dries slowest
6. **Everything else**, which on many houses genuinely does not need doing yet

We write it elevation by elevation with each phase priced separately, so a homeowner can do the urgent work this fall and carry a real number into next year rather than an allowance.

## How the work is done

1. **Walk all four elevations** at grade and from a ladder, and sound the veneer rather than eyeballing it.
2. **Find the water source** before writing anything — roof drainage, grade, sills, lintels, sealant, chimney.
3. **Check the weep line.** Open, blocked, or never installed changes the entire scope.
4. **Correct the drainage path** — clear or re-drill weeps, correct flashing where it is reachable, replace failed sealant with backer rod and proper joint sealant.
5. **Cut failed joints to depth** — roughly two and a half times the joint width — never skimming over failed material.
6. **Repoint with matched mortar**, **Type N** for most veneer of this era, always softer than the brick it holds, matched for color and texture against a **cured** sample and tooled to the original profile.
7. **Replace failed brick** matched by size, color, texture and era, salvaging from a concealed elevation where an exact match cannot be bought.
8. **Address the steel** — lintels and shelf angles cleaned and coated where sound, replaced where section is lost, with flashing and weeps installed as the masonry goes back.
9. **Cure and protect** — damp and shaded in heat, heated and covered in cold, or scheduled for a workable window.

## What drives the cost

**How many elevations** are genuinely involved. **Height and access** — a two-story rear elevation over a deck is not a front wall off a driveway. **Whether the drainage details need correcting** or only the face work. **How much steel** is in the scope, since opening a wall to replace a shelf angle is a different order of work than repointing it. **How difficult the brick is to match.** And **what is found once the first wall is opened.** We assess it, price the scope, and put exclusions in writing.
