We should say the uncomfortable part first, because this is the service most often sold to people who do not need it.

**Sealing a wall with open mortar joints does not waterproof it. It seals the water in.**

Water gets into masonry primarily through **failed joints**, not through the face of the brick. Coat the surface while the joints are still open and you have changed nothing about the entry path — but you have made it harder for the wall to dry. That is worse than doing nothing.

## Brick and Block Behave Differently

This matters in Crest Hill more than in most towns, because the local building stock is genuinely mixed — post-war residential brick alongside a substantial amount of **concrete block** in the commercial, industrial and institutional properties along the Route 30 and Weber Road corridors.

| | Brick | Concrete block (CMU) |
|---|---|---|
| **Surface** | Fired skin resists water | **Absorbent through the whole face** |
| **Main water path** | Mortar joints | **Joints and the unit itself** |
| **Absorption rate** | Lower | Considerably higher |
| **Sealer coverage** | Standard rate | **Higher rate; often two passes** |
| **Movement** | Expansion joints | **Control joints sealed with flexible sealant** |
| **Common coating error** | Painting | Painting, and sealing over cracked block |

**Block is the one people get wrong.** It drinks water in a way brick does not, so it appears to need sealing more urgently — and it is also the material most likely to have been painted repeatedly, trapping moisture in an already absorbent unit.

On a block wall, sealing is genuinely useful **after** cracks are repaired and control joints are properly sealed with flexible sealant. Applied before that, it is cosmetic.

## Breathable Sealer vs. Film-Forming Coating

| | Breathable penetrating sealer | Film-forming coating / paint |
|---|---|---|
| **How it works** | Penetrates, repels liquid water | Skin on the surface |
| **Vapor** | Passes through — wall still dries | Blocked — moisture trapped |
| **Appearance** | Essentially invisible | Visible sheen or color change |
| **On Chicagoland masonry** | Appropriate where warranted | **Causes the spalling it was meant to prevent** |
| **Failure mode** | Gradually stops repelling; reapply | Blistering, peeling, faces spalling from behind |

A masonry wall in this climate has to **dry outward**. Trap moisture behind a film and water that inevitably gets in — through a failed sill, a chimney, a gap at a window — has nowhere to go. It freezes inside and breaks the face off from behind.

## When Sealing Actually Helps

- **After repointing**, on a wall with sound joints, as added protection on a weather-beaten elevation.
- **On concrete block**, once cracks and control joints are addressed.
- **On highly absorbent masonry** that takes on water readily.
- **On chimneys**, exposed on four sides with no interior drying them.
- **At grade and splash zones**, where road salt and splash-back keep masonry saturated.
- **On horizontal stone** — sills, water tables, caps — that hold water rather than shedding it.
- **On a south or west elevation** taking the most driving rain.

## When Sealing Is the Wrong Answer

- **When the joints have failed.** Repoint first. Always.
- **When you have efflorescence** and have not found the water source.
- **When brick is already spalling.** Replace the failed units first.
- **When a chimney crown, flashing or sill is the actual leak.**
- **Blanket-sealing modern brick veneer**, which is designed to drain through flashing and weep holes.
- **On cracked block** where the crack, not the surface, is the path.
- **When somebody proposes it instead of repointing.** That is the clearest signal to get another opinion.

## Our Process

1. **Free on-site assessment.** Joint condition, unit absorption, existing coatings, efflorescence, cracks, control joints, and where water is entering.
2. **Repair first.** Joints, brick or block, sills, chimney, flashing as the assessment requires.
3. **Address control joints** on block walls with backer rod and flexible sealant — not mortar.

5. **Test patch** on an inconspicuous area to confirm appearance and absorption.
6. **Application** at the correct rate for the substrate — block generally takes more than brick — in appropriate temperature and moisture conditions.
7. **Cure and inspection.**
