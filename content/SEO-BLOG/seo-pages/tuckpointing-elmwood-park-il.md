Here is the thing almost nobody tells an Elmwood Park homeowner:

**Your house is not made of one kind of brick.**

Walk to the curb and look at the front. Hard, dense, uniform face brick, laid with tight tooled joints, chosen to be looked at. Now walk down the gangway and look at the side wall, or go into the alley and look at the back. Different brick entirely — softer, more porous, often salmon or orange, laid quickly, never meant to be seen.

That is **Chicago common brick**, and it drinks water in a way the front of your house does not.

**Two materials. Two failure schedules. Two different mortars.** Price the job as one number per linear foot and that distinction disappears — which is exactly how a repointing job ends up destroying the rear wall it was supposed to save.

## The Rule That Governs Everything

There is one principle behind every good repointing job, and it is worth memorizing because it lets you evaluate any contractor's answer:

**Mortar must be softer than the brick it holds.**

Mortar is the sacrificial element. A brick wall moves — it expands in July, contracts in January, takes up water and freezes. Something has to absorb that stress. If the mortar is softer than the brick, the joint takes the damage, and joints are replaceable. If the mortar is harder than the brick, the **brick faces** take it instead, and they pop, flake and shear off. Brick is not replaceable in the same easy way.

Now apply that to an Elmwood Park house:

- **Front face brick** — hard, dense, low absorption. Tolerates a firmer mix.
- **Side and rear common brick** — soft, absorbent, comparatively weak. Needs a distinctly softer mix.

Use the front mortar on the back wall and you have installed something harder than the brick around it. It will look fine for two or three years. Then the salmon faces start flaking off in sheets while the joints between them sit there perfectly intact — the tell-tale signature of mortar that outranked its brick.

## Signs Your Elmwood Park House Needs Tuckpointing

- **Joints recessed behind the brick face** — more than about a quarter inch means water is being held against the wall.
- **Mortar you can rake out with a fingernail**, or that powders when scratched.
- **Sand at the base of the wall** after rain — that is your joints, arriving on the ground.
- **Step cracks** running diagonally through joints, especially near corners and openings.
- **Spalling or flaking brick faces**, most often low on a common-brick side wall.
- **White chalky efflorescence** blooming on the brick — salts carried out by water moving through the wall.
- **Damp patches or staining on interior walls** after a long rain.
- **Open joints at the chimney** above the roofline, where exposure is worst.
- **The rear elevation looking a decade older than the front.** Normal here — and diagnostic.

Not sure? Take a key to a joint on the back of the house and then a joint on the front. If the back gives and the front does not, you have just proved the point of this page.

## Our Tuckpointing Process

1. **Free on-site assessment.** We look at every elevation separately, identify each brick type, and test joint hardness by hand. You get a written scope that says what is happening on each wall.
2. **Mortar analysis and matching.** We match hardness first, then color, sand and texture. Where the front and rear brick differ, we batch two mixes. This is the step that determines whether the job lasts 30 years or 5.
3. **Joint preparation.** Failed mortar is cut back to sound material — generally two to two and a half times the joint width — using the right tool for the brick. Soft common brick gets hand tools or a light touch, not an aggressive grinder that chews the arrises.
4. **Cleaning and dampening.** Joints are cleared of dust and pre-wetted so the brick does not pull water out of the fresh mortar before it can cure.
5. **Repointing in lifts.** Mortar is packed in layers and compacted, not buttered over the surface. A joint filled solid is what stops water; a skim coat is decoration.
6. **Tooling to match the original profile.** Concave, weathered, grapevine, struck — the existing profile is matched so the repair reads as part of the wall, not a stripe across it.
7. **Cleaning down.** Faces cleaned before the mortar sets hard, because removing cured mortar smears later means damaging brick.
8. **Curing and final walkthrough.** Fresh mortar cures lighter and darkens over the following weeks; we tell you that up front so the first week does not alarm you.

## What Drives the Cost

We do not publish a per-linear-foot rate, and here is the honest reason: **on an Elmwood Park house it is the wrong unit.** A rate per foot of joint implies every foot is the same work, and on a building with hard face brick out front and soft common brick behind it, it is not.

What actually drives the number:

- **How many elevations need work**, and how much of each — spot repointing versus a full wall.
- **Brick type per elevation**, because soft common brick takes slower, gentler preparation.
- **Wall height and staging** — ground-floor reach versus scaffolding a three-story rear elevation.
- **Access** — gangway width, alley access, protecting the neighboring property.
- **Joint condition** — how deep the erosion goes, and whether previous bad repointing has to come out first.
- **Mortar matching complexity**, including custom color blending.
- **Related repairs found in the same wall** — displaced brick, a rusting lintel, a failed sill.

We assess in person and itemize by elevation, so you can see what the front costs, what the rear costs, and phase the work across seasons if you want to.

What starts as a joint you can rake with a fingernail becomes spalled brick faces, then displaced units, then a section of wall that needs rebuilding instead of repointing.
