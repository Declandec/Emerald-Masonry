**Why is one sill shedding in sheets while the identical sill six feet away is untouched?**

Same building. Same stone. Same age. Same weather. And one of them is losing its face while the other looks nearly new.

## The Answer Is Bedding Planes

**Limestone is a sedimentary rock.** It formed in horizontal layers over an enormous span of time, and those layers — **bedding planes** — are still there inside every piece of it, whether or not you can see them from the street.

They are the stone's grain, and they are its weakness.

**Set naturally bedded**, with the layers running horizontally as they formed, a sill sheds water across the top of the layers. Water runs off. The stone lasts a very long time.

**Set face-bedded**, with the layers turned vertical and facing out, the exposed edges of every layer face the weather. Water gets *between* the layers, freezes, and splits them apart. The face comes off in **sheets rather than crumbs** — that is delamination, and it is the signature of face-bedded stone.

**Which way any given piece was laid was decided a century ago at the quarry and the jobsite.** It is why two sills in identical conditions age completely differently, and it is the first thing worth understanding before deciding what to do about it.

## What Fails on Evanston Stone

### Delamination
Face shedding in flat sheets or plates. Bedding-plane failure, accelerated by any water that reaches the stone.

### Spalled edges and corners
The exposed arrises of a sill or water table take the most weather and the most physical contact.

### Cracks across the span
Sills span an opening. A settling foundation, a corroding steel lintel below, or a hard mortar bed can crack one straight across.

### Erosion of carved profiles
Drip edges, moldings and carved detail wear away, and once the **drip edge** is gone the sill stops throwing water clear and starts running it down the wall below.

### Dropped or displaced units
When the mortar bed under a stone fails, the unit settles, opens a joint at the top, and starts admitting water directly.

### Damage from the wrong repair
Hard Portland-heavy mortar against soft limestone, or a film-forming sealer over it, both drive moisture into the stone and cause the exact failures they were meant to prevent.

## Why Horizontal Stone Fails First

Every piece of stone on your building that is **horizontal** — sills, water tables, steps, coping, belt courses — fails before the vertical stone does, for one reason: **it holds water instead of shedding it.**

A vertical wall sheds. A sill collects. That is why sills, treads and copings are consistently the most deteriorated stone on an Evanston greystone or brick-and-limestone home, even when the walls are sound.

## Signs Your Stone Needs Attention

- **Flat plates or sheets** lifting off the face of a sill or stone unit.
- **Spalled, rounded or broken edges** where a crisp arris used to be.
- **A crack across a sill**, particularly at midspan or near one bearing.
- **Rust staining** on stone below a window — steel corroding under or behind it.
- **Open or washed-out joints** around stone units.
- **Efflorescence** blooming on the stone or the masonry below it.
- **A sill sitting visibly lower** at one end than the other.
- **Water staining on the wall directly below a sill** — the drip edge has eroded away.
- **Hard gray mortar** packed around soft stone from a previous repair.

## Our Stone Repair Process

1. **Assess the stone** — identify bedding orientation, the extent of delamination, and whether the unit is structurally sound.
2. **Check what is under and behind it.** A cracked sill frequently has a corroded lintel or a failed bed beneath it, and repairing the stone without that is temporary.
3. **Decide unit by unit** — patch, dutchman, repin, reset, or replace. Preservation of the original is the default.
4. **Dutchman repairs** — cut the failure out to a clean geometric shape, fit a matched stone insert, set and pin it.
5. **Matched patching** with stone-specific compounds where a full insert is not warranted, colored and textured to the original.
6. **Reset displaced units** on a correct, appropriately soft mortar bed.
7. **Repoint around the stone** with lime-based mortar softer than the stone itself.
8. **Restore the drip edge and drainage** so the sill throws water clear of the wall again.
9. **Gentle cleaning and breathable protection only** — no sandblasting, no high-pressure washing, no film-forming sealers.

## What Drives Cost

- **Number of units** and how many need dutchman work versus patching.
- **Repair versus replacement** — replacement requires sourcing and cutting matched stone.
- **Access and height** — grade-level water tables versus third-floor sills.

- **Carved or profiled detail**, which is slow, skilled work to reproduce.
- **Stone sourcing** and matching difficulty on unusual or discontinued material.
- **Undoing prior repairs** in hard mortar or sealer, often the largest single variable.
