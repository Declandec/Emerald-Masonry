Here is the question that determines your scope, and it almost never gets asked:

**Is your wall holding the building up, or is it hanging on the building?**

Melrose Park's commercial stock runs from 1920s load-bearing industrial brick to 1970s brick veneer on steel frame, frequently on the same block and occasionally on the same property. **They look similar from the street and they are structurally unrelated.** A scope written without knowing which one you have is a guess.

## Load-Bearing vs. Veneer: How to Tell, and Why It Matters

| | Load-bearing multi-wythe | Brick veneer on frame |
|---|---|---|
| **Typical era here** | 1900s–1950s | 1960s–present |
| **Wall at a window reveal** | 8–16 inches deep | ~4 inches + framing |
| **Header courses** | Present — bricks laid end-on | Absent |
| **Weep holes at base** | None | Present (should be) |
| **What carries load** | The masonry itself | The steel or stud frame |
| **Hidden steel** | Lintels over openings | Lintels **plus** shelf/relieving angles |
| **Primary failure** | Mortar erosion, inner-wythe saturation, bulging | Tie corrosion, shelf-angle rust, displacement |
| **Worst case** | Loss of section in a bearing wall | Veneer separating from the structure |

**On a load-bearing wall, the masonry is the structure**, so eroded joints and saturated inner wythes are a capacity question, not just an appearance one. Water that gets past the outer wythe has nowhere to drain — there is no cavity — so it moves through the wall and shows up as efflorescence and damp on the interior face.

**On veneer, the masonry is a rain screen.** It is designed to leak, and to drain what leaks through a cavity and back out the weep holes. The structural risk is not the brick; it is the **corrugated ties and the shelf angles** holding it to the frame. When those rust through, the wall is sound-looking and unsupported at the same time.

## Signs a Melrose Park Commercial Building Needs Restoration

- **Displaced or open coping** at the parapet, or mortar washed out of the coping joints.
- A **parapet leaning, bowing or separating** at the corners.
- **Rust staining and horizontal cracking** above windows, dock doors and storefront openings.
- A **horizontal crack running the length of a floor line** on a veneer building — the classic shelf-angle signature.
- **Spalled, eroded or impacted block** at the dock apron and drive aisle.
- **Efflorescence or damp** on the inside face of an exterior wall.
- **Stepped cracking** at building corners or where an addition meets the original structure.
- **Weep holes mortared shut**, painted over, or absent entirely on a veneer wall.
- **Sealant failure** at panel joints, window perimeters and control joints.

## Risks of Waiting on Commercial Masonry

Commercial deferral costs more than residential deferral, for three reasons.

**Scale.** A residential wall is a few hundred square feet. An industrial elevation is thousands, so the same rate of deterioration multiplies.

**Access.** Once damage climbs above the reach of a lift, you are into swing stage or scaffold, and access frequently costs more than the masonry.

**Liability.** Falling masonry from a parapet or a facade over a sidewalk, a dock or an employee parking area is a different category of problem than a cosmetic one — and on taller buildings, facade condition is increasingly a documented obligation rather than a discretionary maintenance item.

## Our Commercial Restoration Process

1. **Survey and classification.** Determine wall construction, locate hidden steel, identify prior repairs, photograph and map conditions by elevation.
2. **Prioritize by risk and water path.** Parapet and coping first, then openings and steel, then field walls, then grade — because that is the order water travels.
3. **Written scope by elevation**, with quantities, so you can phase it across budget years if you need to.
4. **Access plan** built around your operations — lift, scaffold or swing stage, with dock doors and drive aisles kept usable.
5. **Steel first.** Expose corroded lintels, relieving and shelf angles, treat or replace, and install **through-wall flashing above the steel** so it stays dry.
6. **Rebuild and repoint** — joints cut to sound mortar, mortar matched to the units, tooled to the original profile.
7. **Parapet and coping** reset or rebuilt with proper flashing and positive drainage.
8. **Sealant and control joints** renewed with the correct product and backer rod.
9. **Coatings only where appropriate**, breathable, and only after the joints are sound.

## What Drives Cost

- **Wall construction** — veneer with shelf-angle work is a different project than repointing a solid wall.
- **Height and access** — ground-and-lift work versus scaffold or swing stage.
- **Steel involved** — every corroded lintel or angle adds shoring, removal and rebuild.
- **Parapet condition** — resetting coping is minor; rebuilding a parapet is major.
- **Repair versus rebuild ratio** across each elevation.
- **Operational constraints** — night work, phased access, keeping docks live.
- **Protection requirements** over entries, docks, sidewalks and parked vehicles.

**Phasing is normal here.** A written scope broken out by elevation lets you take the parapet this year and the field walls next, in the order that actually protects the building.
