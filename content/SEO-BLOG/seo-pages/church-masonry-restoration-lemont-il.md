Lemont is one of the few places in Chicagoland where the churches were built **from the ground they stand on**. The dolomitic limestone quarried along the Des Plaines valley and the canal — sold to Chicago as Athens Marble — went into the town's own parish buildings, rectories, convents, school buildings and cemetery walls through the second half of the nineteenth century. Many of them are still in use, still laid in their original lime mortar in places, and still being maintained by volunteers and a parish budget.

For the stone itself — how Lemont dolomite weathers and why the mortar decides its fate — see our page on [historic masonry restoration in Lemont](/locations/historic-masonry-restoration-lemont-il). ## What church masonry restoration in Lemont actually involves

On a nineteenth-century limestone parish campus, a restoration is usually a combination of five kinds of work:

- **Repointing** the stone walls in a lime-based mortar matched to the original in colour, aggregate and — most importantly — softness
- **Stone repair** — compatible repair mortar, a **dutchman**, or full replacement of individual blocks
- **Sills, copings and water tables** — the horizontal stones that take the weather first; see [limestone sill repair](/services/limestone-sill-repair)
- **Tower and steeple masonry** — the highest exposure, the hardest access, and frequently the oldest repairs
- **Site masonry** — rectory foundations, cemetery and boundary walls, stoops and steps

## Signs your Lemont church needs attention

- Mortar recessed or washed out of joints, especially on the west and south walls and the tower
- **Stone faces delaminating** — thin layers peeling off along the bedding plane
- Hard grey cement pointing from a past repair, with the stone around it spalling
- Staining on interior plaster below windows or at the base of the tower
- Open joints on sills and copings, or sills tipping toward the wall
- Cemetery or boundary walls leaning, with capstones loose or missing

## The patch, dutchman or replace decision

This is where most parish money is either saved or wasted.

### Leave or patch

Shallow surface loss on an otherwise sound block often needs nothing, or a lime-based repair mortar tooled to the surrounding stone. It is the least invasive option and, done correctly, is reversible.

### Dutchman

A **dutchman** is a piece of new matching stone cut and set into a prepared pocket in the damaged block, pinned and bedded so the original stone stays in the wall. It keeps original fabric, avoids dismantling the course above, and is usually the right answer for a deep defect in one corner of a large ashlar or a broken arris on a quoin.

### Replace

Replacement is for stone that has failed through its depth, has lost its bearing, or where a dutchman would leave too little original stone to be worth keeping. New stone is matched for colour, bed direction and finish — dolomite set on its natural bed lasts; dolomite set on edge peels.

The order matters: **least invasive first**. A good scope tells you, stone by stone, which category each defect falls into and why.

## Why lime mortar is not optional

The single most expensive mistake on Lemont's old stone is hard cement mortar from a past repair. The joint was designed to be the weak, breathable part of the wall, so that moisture leaves through the mortar and freeze-thaw stress is spent in a joint that can be repointed. Replace it with Portland mortar and the stone becomes the weak part. We specify a **natural hydraulic lime or lime-rich mortar**, matched by sample panel, and remove incompatible cement pointing carefully rather than with a grinder that widens the joint. [Lime versus Portland mortar](/blog/lime-vs-portland-mortar) explains the chemistry.

## Steeples, towers and access

A tower is usually the highest, most exposed and least inspected masonry on the campus — and often the place where a bell frame, louvres and decades of pigeon guano have been working on the stone unseen. Access is frequently **the largest single cost** on a church project, so we assess it first: lift, partial scaffold or full scaffold, how loads bear on stairs and lawns, and whether the work can share staging with roofing or carpentry already planned. Our guide to [steeple and bell tower masonry](/blog/church-steeple-bell-tower-masonry-restoration) goes deeper.

## Working inside the parish calendar

We do not treat a church as a building site that happens to have services in it. Before mobilising we agree with the parish office:

- Which elevations are worked around weekend and weekday Masses
- Protected, clear routes for processions, weddings and funerals
- A **stand-down procedure for funerals** at short notice
- Quiet periods with no cutting or grinding
- Protection for stained glass, doors and memorial plantings

## Scoping for the finance council

Parish masonry is usually approved by a **finance council** or building committee, often with diocesan review above a threshold. That approval works best on a document that separates:

1. **Safety and water-entry items** that cannot wait
2. **Deterioration items** that will cost more next year
3. **Appearance and long-term items** that can be phased across budget years or capital campaigns

Each phase is priced on its own with quantities, so the council can approve phase one against real numbers. [Tuckpointing for churches and institutions](/blog/tuckpointing-church-institutional-masonry) covers how to keep later phases consistent.

## What drives the cost

- Linear feet of joint and the condition of past cement repairs to be removed
- Number of stones to dutchman versus replace, and the source of matching stone
- Tower, steeple and gable access
- Sills, copings and water tables needing reset or replacement
- Calendar constraints and the number of mobilisations
