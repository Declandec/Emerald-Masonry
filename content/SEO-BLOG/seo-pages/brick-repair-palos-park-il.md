## The problem is not water. It is drying.

Every brick wall in Chicagoland gets wet. Brick and mortar are porous, they absorb water, and wind-driven rain pushes moisture through the face. That is normal and it always has been.

**A wall stays healthy because it dries out again between wettings.** The moisture it takes on during a storm evaporates back out over the following days, and as long as it dries faster than it wets across a season, the wall lasts a century.

Solar exposure and airflow are what do that drying. And a Palos Park wall under fifty feet of oak canopy gets very little of either.

So the local pattern is consistent and predictable:

- The wall stays **saturated for days** after rain rather than hours
- Every one of Chicagoland's dozens of annual **freeze-thaw cycles** catches it wet
- Water inside the brick and mortar **freezes, expands**, and works the material apart a fraction at a time
- **Green film, moss and algae** appear — which only grow where masonry stays damp, making them an accurate map of the problem
- **Mortar joints go soft**, then recede behind the face of the brick
- Then **the brick itself begins to spall** — faces flaking and popping off

That last step is the one that brings people to a mason, and by then the joints have usually been finished for a while.

## Reading your own walls

Walk the house and look for these, in this order:

**Green or black film on the brick.** Not cosmetic — diagnostic. It marks the elevations that never dry.

**Receded mortar joints.** Sight along the wall at a low angle. Mortar should sit at or just behind the brick face. Joints set back a quarter inch or more, so the units read as individually raised, have lost real section. Push a house key into a joint: sound mortar resists, failed mortar crumbles.

**Spalling.** Faces flaking, sheeting or popping off, exposing the softer interior of the brick. Common at the base courses and anywhere water concentrates.

**Efflorescence.** White mineral bloom on the surface — proof that water is actively moving through the masonry and carrying salts out with it.

**Step cracks.** Diagonal cracking running through the mortar joints in a stair pattern, usually indicating movement rather than weathering.

**Displaced or loose units**, and any brick you can move by hand.

**Rust staining** running down from above a window or door — a corroding steel lintel expanding inside the wall.

## Where Palos Park masonry actually fails

**The north and canopy-shaded elevations**, for all the reasons above. On a great many properties here, one wall is dramatically worse than the other three.

**The base courses.** Splash off the ground, snow banked against the wall, and on wooded lots a persistent layer of leaf litter and mulch that holds moisture directly against the brick and often creeps above the base course over the years.

**Chimneys.** Exposed on four sides and the top, and on wooded lots frequently collecting leaves and seed debris on the crown and at the flashing, which holds water exactly where it does the most harm.

**Garages, sheds and outbuildings.** Usually set further back under heavier canopy, rarely inspected, and often the worst masonry on a property.

**Freestanding walls, piers and entrance columns.** Weathered on every face with no building mass to shelter them, and common on Palos Park's larger lots.

**Anywhere trees are involved.** Vines on masonry hold moisture against the wall and their tendrils work into joints. Roots near a foundation, a chimney footing or a garden wall are worth looking at before they become a structural conversation rather than a maintenance one.

## Repair or replace an individual brick

| Condition | What it needs |
| --- | --- |
| Sound face, failed joints around it | **Repoint** — the unit is fine |
| Hairline crack, face intact | Usually **monitor**; repoint and correct the water source |
| Face spalled or popped off | **Replace** — the exposed interior is soft and absorbent |
| Unit loose or displaced | **Replace and re-bed**, and find out what moved it |
| Several courses displaced or bulging | **Rebuild** the section; this is movement, not weathering |

The rule underneath all of it: **replacing brick without correcting the water source guarantees a repeat.** The new units are only as durable as the conditions you put them back into.

## Our process

1. **Walk every elevation and sound the wall** with a hammer handle — spalling is often audible before it is visible.
2. **Find the water.** Gutters, downspout discharge, grade, mulch and leaf litter height, canopy, sills, lintels, chimney, vines.
3. **Mark the failed units** and quantify honestly — usually far fewer than a homeowner fears, occasionally more.
4. **Correct the water source first**, because everything else depends on it.
5. **Cut out failed units** carefully, preserving the surrounding brick, shoring where the section carries load.
6. **Match the replacement brick** by size, color, texture and era — reclaimed period brick where production units will not match, salvage from a concealed elevation where nothing else is close.
7. **Set in matched mortar**, always **softer than the brick it holds** — soft, lime-rich mixes on older soft units, **Type N** for most residential work, **Type S** only where genuinely warranted.
8. **Repoint the surrounding joints** to depth, roughly two and a half times the joint width, never skimming over failed material.
9. **Match color, texture and profile** against a cured sample, and **cure and protect** the work.

On cleaning: the **gentlest method that works**. **No sandblasting, no high-pressure washing** — both permanently strip the weathered outer skin off brick and leave a more absorbent surface that fails faster than what you started with. Biological growth comes off with low-pressure washing and appropriate cleaners, not force.

## Prevention that actually works here

- **Thin the canopy** over the roof and walls where you can, and keep growth off the masonry
- **Remove vines** from brick entirely
- **Pull mulch and leaf litter back** below the base course and keep it there
- **Extend downspouts** well away from the foundation onto ground that falls away
- **Keep gutters clear** — on a wooded lot that is a seasonal job, not an annual one
- **Clear the chimney crown and flashing** of leaves and seed debris
- **Inspect the shaded elevation and the outbuildings** deliberately, because nobody does
