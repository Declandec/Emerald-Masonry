## The Test: Does Your Brick Actually Absorb?

Spray a small, representative area of wall with plain water and watch what happens.

- **Water beads, or sits and runs off** → the masonry is still shedding. A repellent adds little right now.
- **The brick darkens slowly over 10–30 seconds** → moderate absorption. A repellent is reasonable on exposed elevations once the wall is sound.
- **The brick darkens almost immediately and drinks it in** → high absorption. This wall is a genuine candidate — but first find out *why* it is that thirsty.

That last point matters. High absorption usually means the fired face of the brick has been compromised — most often by **past pressure washing or sandblasting**, which strips the hard outer surface permanently and cannot be reversed. A repellent helps such a wall, but it is treating an injury rather than maintaining a healthy wall.

Test more than one spot. South and west elevations take the driving rain here and are frequently in a different condition than the sheltered sides.

## The Rule That Decides Whether Sealing Helps

**Repair first. Seal second. Never the reverse.**

A penetrating repellent is designed for one job: shedding wind-driven rain off sound masonry. It cannot bridge an open mortar joint, a cracked brick, a failed sill, a corroding lintel, or a gap at a window. Water entering through any of those goes past a sealer as though it were not there — and a sealed face slows how fast the wall can dry back out afterward.

So the honest sequence on a New Lenox house is:

1. **Diagnose the water path** — where is it genuinely getting in?
2. **Repair it** — [tuckpointing](/services/tuckpointing), [brick repair](/services/brick-repair), sills, [lintels](/services/lintel-repair), sealant joints, flashing
3. **Let new mortar cure**
4. **Then apply a breathable repellent** to sound wall

## Breathable vs. Film-Forming

| | Penetrating repellent (silane/siloxane) | Film-forming coating |
|---|---|---|
| Where it sits | Soaks into the pores | On the surface as a film |
| Liquid water | Repelled | Blocked |
| Vapor | Wall still dries | Moisture trapped inside |
| Appearance | No gloss, no colour change | Glossy, darkens brick, can cloud |
| How it fails | Wears gradually, reapplied | Peels, blisters, spalls the brick face |

Brick walls are not meant to be airtight; they are meant to shed water and dry out. Anything preventing that is working against the wall, and in Will County's freeze-thaw cycling trapped moisture is precisely what pops brick faces off. ## New Lenox: Two Kinds of Wall

**The subdivision stock.** Most of New Lenox is 1990s-through-2010s **brick and stone veneer** on open Will County lots. A veneer wall is a drainage assembly — brick, an air cavity, flashing at the base and over openings, and weep holes to let water back out. It is designed to get wet and drain. Sealing the face does not change that design, and it is never a substitute for clear weep holes and intact flashing. On exposed west elevations with no windbreak, though, a breathable repellent meaningfully reduces how much water the wall takes on during driving rain.

**The older village core.** The blocks around the original town along the Lincoln Highway corridor carry older, softer brick that needs a lighter hand — soft mortar when repointed, gentle cleaning, and a repellent chosen for a more absorbent substrate.

Both sit in the same climate: dozens of freeze-thaw cycles a winter, and the wettest masonry on any building taking the worst of it.

## Our Process

1. **Walk the whole envelope** — joints, brick faces, sills, lintels, chimney, sealant, weeps, grade, gutters and downspouts.
