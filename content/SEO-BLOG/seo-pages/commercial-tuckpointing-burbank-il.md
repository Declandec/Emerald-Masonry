## What commercial tuckpointing actually is

**Tuckpointing** — properly, repointing — is the removal of deteriorated mortar from the joints and the placement of new mortar in its place. The joint is the sacrificial, replaceable part of a masonry wall; the brick is not. When the joints go and are left, water gets past them, and the damage moves from something replaceable into something that is not.

It is not a surface treatment. Done correctly the old mortar is ground or raked out to a real depth — typically two to two and a half times the joint width — the joint is cleaned, new mortar is packed in lifts, and the joint is tooled to match the existing profile. [What repointing actually means](/blog/what-repointing-actually-means) covers the method; [how to spot a bad tuckpointing job](/blog/how-to-spot-bad-tuckpointing-job-chicago) covers what it looks like when somebody skipped the grinding and smeared a skim coat over the face.

## Your building is probably two different walls

This is the single most useful thing to know about Burbank's mid-century commercial and apartment stock.

> A great many of these buildings were built with hard face brick on the street elevation and softer, more absorptive Chicago common brick on the sides and the rear. Those two bricks have different water behaviour, need different mortar hardness, and reach the end of their service life years apart. The back of the building looking a decade older than the front is not neglect — it is two walls on one structure.

Which means a single price per square foot across the whole building is the wrong unit, and a single mortar mix across the whole building is the wrong specification. The governing rule does not change: **the mortar must be softer than the brick it holds**, so movement and moisture relieve through the joint instead of spalling the brick face. Apply a hard Portland-heavy mix to a soft common-brick rear elevation and you will be replacing brick, not joints, within a few winters. [Chicago common brick and why it crumbles](/blog/chicago-common-brick-why-it-crumbles) and [Type N versus Type S mortar](/blog/type-n-vs-type-s-mortar-chicago) cover the mechanism.

## Spot repointing or the full elevation — honestly

Every owner asks this, and most contractors answer it in whichever direction is bigger. The test is physical, not commercial.

| Finding | Honest call |
|---|---|
| Mortar solid a quarter inch in; failure in bands or patches | **Spot repointing** is legitimate and appropriate |
| Screwdriver enters easily across most of the elevation | **Full elevation** — spot work will be back next year |
| Joints sound on the street face, sandy on the rear | **Two different scopes**, priced separately |
| Cracks tracking diagonally or along a floor line | Not a mortar problem — investigate before pricing joints |

[Spot repointing versus full tuckpointing](/blog/spot-repointing-vs-full-tuckpointing) covers the decision in more depth, and [how to tell if mortar is failing with a screwdriver](/blog/how-to-tell-if-mortar-is-failing-screwdriver-test) is a test you can run yourself this afternoon.

## Signs it is time on a Burbank commercial building

- **Receding or crumbling joints** — mortar visibly set back from the brick face, sand at the base of the wall
- **Spalling or flaking brick faces**, concentrated near the bottom courses and around downspouts
- **Efflorescence** — white powdery salt bloom on the brick, which means water is moving through the wall
- **Water stains inside** top-floor units or along the rear stairwell
- **Loose or hollow-sounding brick** at the parapet or around openings
- **Rust staining below a lintel** over a storefront or window — that is steel, not mortar
- **Step cracks** through the joints — a movement or settlement question first

[Signs a commercial building needs tuckpointing](/blog/signs-commercial-building-needs-tuckpointing) covers what each one actually indicates.

## What waiting costs

Repointing is the cheapest masonry work there is, and every year of deferral moves the job toward more expensive categories. Open joints admit water; water saturates the brick; freeze-thaw pops the faces; spalled brick has to be **replaced** rather than repointed; and water that reaches the steel behind the wall starts corroding lintels and shelf angles, which is a structural repair. On an apartment building it also reaches interior finishes and generates tenant complaints, which is the version of the cost that arrives fastest.
