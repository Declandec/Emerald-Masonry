## Athens Marble: What Lemont Is Actually Built From

In the 1830s and 40s, digging the **Illinois and Michigan Canal** through the Des Plaines valley cut straight into a thick bed of **dolomitic limestone**. The stone came out of the ground as a byproduct of the canal and immediately became the building material of the settlement beside it — which, for a period, was called **Athens**. The stone was marketed as **Athens Marble**.

It is not marble. It is a hard, cream-to-buff **dolomite** — limestone with a high magnesium content — and it was good enough that it left the valley by canal boat and built a substantial share of pre-fire Chicago. The **Chicago Water Tower and Pumping Station**, which famously survived the 1871 fire, are Athens Marble. So is a great deal of the stone in Lemont's own historic downtown, its churches, and the buildings along the bluff and the canal corridor.

**And the quarries are long closed.** That single fact governs restoration here: matched replacement stone is genuinely scarce, so repairing original material is not a preference — it is usually the only real option.

## How Dolomitic Limestone Fails

Limestone is sedimentary, laid down in **bedding planes** — visible horizontal layers. It is durable, workable and handsome, and it has one vulnerability: it is **porous**, so it takes on water.

In a Chicagoland winter that absorbed water freezes inside the stone, expands, and splits it **along those natural layers**. The result is the characteristic failure you see across Lemont:

- **Delamination** — the face separating and shedding in sheets or shells following the bedding.
- **Spalling** at edges, noses and corners.
- **Cracking**, often across a sill span or radiating from a bearing point.
- **Surface erosion** — carved and tooled profiles rounding over and washing away.
- **Displacement** — units dropping or shifting because the mortar bed beneath them failed.

**Horizontal surfaces suffer worst** — sills, water tables, steps, coping — because they hold water rather than shedding it.

## The Mortar Decision That Decides Everything

This is the point the competing pages skip, and on Lemont stone it is the difference between preservation and destruction.

**A masonry wall is designed so the mortar is softer than the units it holds.** Mortar is the sacrificial element — it absorbs movement and moisture stress and wears out first, because replacing mortar is cheap and replacing 1840s quarried stone is, given the closed quarries, effectively impossible.

Canal-era walls were laid with **soft, lime-based mortar**. Repoint them with hard modern **Portland mortar** and the relationship inverts: the joints become the strongest element, and every freeze-thaw cycle drives the stress into the stone faces instead of the joints. On layered dolomite, that means accelerated **delamination** — the repair actively destroying the material it was meant to protect, typically within three to eight winters.

| | Canal-era wall (pre-1900) | Modern wall |
|---|---|---|
| **Mortar** | Lime-rich, soft, breathable | Portland-based, hard, dense |
| **Units** | Dolomitic limestone, soft historic brick | Machine-made, dense, uniform |
| **Joint prep** | Hand-raked, shallow tools | Power grinding acceptable |
| **If mismatched** | Stone delaminates, faces lost permanently | Mortar erodes early |
| **Cleaning** | Gentle only — never blasting | Wider tolerance |
| **Sealing** | Breathable products only | Wider range |

## Signs a Historic Lemont Building Needs Restoration

- **Stone faces flaking or shedding in layers** — delamination along the bedding.
- **Open, sandy or washed-out joints** between stone units.
- Mortar you can **rake out with a screwdriver**, or joints receded well behind the face.
- **Cracked, dropped or tilted** sills, lintels and trim.
- **Hard gray mortar standing proud** of softer stone — a prior inappropriate repair.
- A **band of delamination** matching where somebody repointed a few years ago.
- **Efflorescence** blooming on interior or exterior masonry.
- **Eroded carved detail** on trim, cornices and door surrounds.
- Evidence of **past sandblasting** — an unnaturally open, rough stone surface that now drinks water.
- **Step cracks** through joints at corners and around openings.

## Repair Hierarchy — Least Invasive First

| Approach | What it involves | When it is right |
|---|---|---|
| **Repointing** | Restoring joints with matched lime mortar | Stone sound, joints failed |
| **Matched patching** | Cementitious patch rebuilding lost material | Localized spalling and edge loss |
| **Dutchman repair** | Cutting out the damaged area, fitting matched new stone | Significant local damage, unit otherwise sound |
| **Repinning / resetting** | Removing, rebedding and anchoring a shifted unit | Displaced but intact stone |
| **Replacement** | New matched stone unit | Structurally failed or broken units — last resort |

## Our Restoration Process

1. **Assessment and documentation.** We walk the building, photograph conditions, evaluate the existing mortar's hardness and composition, and flag prior inappropriate repairs.
2. **Mortar analysis and matching.** A lime-based mix formulated to the original — soft enough to protect the stone, durable enough to serve.
3. **Hand joint preparation.** Raked by hand where the stone is soft; power tools only where masonry can safely take them. Aggressive grinding permanently widens joints and chews the arrises.
4. **Undoing bad repairs** where hard mortar must come out — carefully, without taking stone with it.
5. **Repointing in lifts**, packed and compacted, then tooled to the original joint profile.
6. **Stone repair** — patching, dutchman inserts or repinning, chosen unit by unit rather than applied blanket-wide.
7. **Chimneys, coping and trim** restored to shed water properly.
8. **Gentle cleaning, breathable protection.** No sandblasting, no high-pressure washing, no film-forming sealers — all three damage historic stone permanently.

## What Drives Restoration Cost in Lemont

- **Scope** — one elevation, a full facade, or the whole envelope.
- **Mortar matching complexity** — a custom lime blend takes more work than a bagged mix.
- **Hand work required** — soft stone means hand-raking, slower by design.
- **Stone sourcing** — matched dolomite is scarce, so dutchman and patching work is often preferred and priced accordingly.
- **Undoing prior hard-mortar repairs** — frequently the largest variable on a historic Lemont job.
- **Access and staging** — tall buildings, bluff sites and church elevations need real setups.
- **Review and permitting** where a designation applies.
