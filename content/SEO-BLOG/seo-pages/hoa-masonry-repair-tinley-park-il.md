## Start with a survey of every building, not a price for one

Deterioration across an association is never uniform. On a typical Tinley Park property, one or two buildings are meaningfully worse than the rest — usually the ones with the most exposed west elevations, the worst drainage, or a chimney that has been leaking quietly for a decade.

So the first deliverable is a **condition survey building by building**, ranked by risk rather than by address order. That gives a board three things a lump-sum bid cannot:

- **A defensible order of work** — which building is phase one, and why
- **A basis for the reserve conversation** — what is coming, and roughly when
- **A comparable bid document** — every contractor pricing the same quantities

## How we rank an association scope

| Priority | What it covers | Why it comes first |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Safety** | Loose or displaced brick, unstable chimney or parapet, failing lintels over entries | Anything over a walkway, entry or patio is the first dollar spent |
| **2 — Active water entry** | Open joints, failed flashing, cracked sills, blocked weeps | Water reaching a unit becomes an owner complaint and a claim |
| **3 — Advancing deterioration** | Spalling brick, eroded joints not yet leaking | Cheaper now than as unit replacement later |
| **4 — Preventive** | Sealant renewal, breathable sealing, drainage corrections | Extends everything above it |
| **5 — Cosmetic** | Cleaning, staining, appearance blending | Once the envelope is sound |

Boards get into trouble when phase one is chosen by what looks worst from the street rather than by what is actually failing. Those are frequently different buildings.

## The per-unit chimney problem

On Tinley Park's townhome stock this is the single most common masonry dispute we encounter, and the sequence that resolves it is consistent:

1. **Establish the water path and the failed element.** A damp bedroom wall is frequently fed by a cracked chimney crown two floors above, not by the wall it appears on.
2. **Name the element precisely** — field wall, parapet, a chimney serving one unit, a stoop, a patio.
3. **Apply the declaration.** Exterior structural walls are commonly common elements; a chimney serving a single unit is frequently a **limited common element**, where the association repairs it but cost may be allocated to the benefited unit.
4. **Then decide funding** — reserves, operating budget, assessment, or allocation.

Steps 1 and 2 are ours. Steps 3 and 4 are the board's, with counsel where the answer is not clean. Our guide to [who pays for masonry repair, the association or the unit owner](/blog/hoa-vs-unit-owner-who-pays-masonry-repair) covers the classification question in depth.

## What fails on Tinley Park association property

- **Chimneys and stacks** — the most exposed masonry on any building, and the most commonly deferred because no single owner looks at them
- **Lintels over garage and entry openings** — bare steel corroding and lifting the brick above it
- **Base courses and grade-level walls** — splash-back, snow, de-icing salt and foundation plantings holding moisture
- **Veneer details** — blocked or caulked weep holes, short flashing, failed sealant joints at windows and dissimilar materials
- **West and south elevations** — driving rain with little to break it up on an open suburban site
- **Party-wall and patio transitions**, where masonry meets another material and nobody owns the joint

On newer veneer buildings the brick itself is rarely the problem. The **details** are, which is why a survey has to look at flashing, weeps and sealant rather than only at mortar joints.

## Working around residents

- **Phase by building and by elevation** — one face under staging at a time
- **Written notice** before work reaches a building, with dates and what residents must clear

- **A named point of contact** so residents call us, not the board
- **Daily cleanup**, because a tidy site generates far fewer complaints
