Here is the thing that makes **HOA masonry repair in Naperville** different from association work in the older inner-ring suburbs. Most Naperville townhome and condo stock was built between roughly 1985 and 2005, and on the overwhelming majority of it the brick is **not the whole wall.** It is a veneer panel covering the first storey, the garage front, the chimney chase and maybe one gable — with vinyl, cedar, LP trim or stucco taking over above and beside it.

That means the wall has a **horizontal seam in it**, and that seam is where the water gets in. It is also, almost without exception, where a masonry bid ends, a siding bid begins, and neither one owns the detail in between.

## The transition line nobody scopes

> On partly clad Naperville association buildings, the single most common source of persistent interior water complaints is not the brick and not the siding — it is the termination where the veneer stops. If nobody flashed it, nobody is responsible for it, and every trade can truthfully say it is outside their scope.

A brick veneer wall is a drained assembly. Water is expected to pass the brick face, land on flashing, and exit through weep holes. That works only if the flashing exists and is turned up and lapped correctly — including **at the top of the veneer panel**, where the wall changes material.

What we find on this stock, repeatedly:

- **No through-wall flashing at the veneer termination** — the panel just stops, and water that gets behind it runs down the sheathing
- **Weep holes missing, mortared shut by a prior repointing crew, or buried** under raised mulch and regraded beds
- **Sealant used as the only defence** at the transition, decades past its service life
- **Kickout flashing absent** where a roof edge meets a brick wall, so roof water is delivered straight into the veneer cavity

None of those are mortar problems, and none of them are fixed by tuckpointing. Our guides to [through-wall flashing in masonry walls](/blog/through-wall-flashing-masonry-walls) and [weep holes](/blog/weep-holes-masonry-walls) explain what should be there; [solid masonry versus brick veneer repair](/blog/solid-masonry-vs-brick-veneer-repair) explains why the distinction changes everything about the repair.

## Garage fronts: the hardest-working masonry on a townhome

The second condition that defines Naperville association work is the **garage front**. A townhome garage opening is wide, the brick above it is carried on a single steel lintel, and that lintel sits at the most weather-exposed, least protected point on the building — often with a driveway that drains toward it and snow piled against it all winter.

When cracking shows above a garage door, the mortar is the symptom. The mechanism is usually the lintel: deflecting under a long span, or corroding and **rust-jacking** the course above it upward, which opens a horizontal crack that looks exactly like tired mortar from twenty feet away. Repointing it buys a season or two. Our guide to [brick veneer ties, shelf angles and hidden steel](/blog/brick-veneer-ties-shelf-angles-hidden-steel) covers how to read it.

We scope garage fronts by **count**, not by area — because on a fifty-unit townhome association the number of openings, not the square footage of wall, is what drives the price and the schedule.

## A word about reserve-study numbers

Most Naperville associations arrive at masonry through a reserve study line item. That line is a **planning placeholder**, generally derived from an assumed service life and an assumed unit cost — not from anyone opening a wall on your property. It is the right tool for funding and the wrong tool for bidding.
