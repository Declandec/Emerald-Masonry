Every restoration job starts with a question that ordinary repointing skips entirely:

**What mortar is actually in this wall right now?**

Not what mortar the era typically used. Not what mortar is on the truck. **What is in this specific wall**, how hard is it, and how hard are the units it holds. Everything else follows from the answer, and getting it wrong is how repairs destroy the buildings they were meant to save.

## Two Materials, One Rule

St. Charles has an unusual masonry inventory for a town its size, because it built itself out of what was underfoot.

**Limestone.** The Fox Valley quarries supplied dolomitic limestone for the 19th-century downtown, the churches and civic buildings, and the foundations, sills and trim of homes across the older neighborhoods on both banks of the river.

**Brick.** Layered on top of that across the following century — Victorians, Italianates, Foursquares, Queen Annes, then 1920s brick, then post-war stock, then modern veneer toward Randall Road.

**Both are governed by the same rule.** A masonry wall is designed so **the mortar is softer than the units it holds.** Mortar is the sacrificial layer — it absorbs movement and moisture stress and wears out first, because replacing mortar is cheap and replacing 150-year-old stone or handmade brick is not.

Satisfying that rule means different mixes on different walls in the same town, and sometimes on the same building.

| | Pre-1900 stone and brick | Early 1900s | Post-war |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Original mortar** | Lime-rich, soft | Lime-modified | Portland-based |
| **Correct repair mix** | Custom lime blend or **Type O** | Lime-modified or **Type N** | **Type N**, sometimes **Type S** |
| **Joint preparation** | Hand-raked | Hand-raked or light grinding | Power grinding acceptable |
| **If mismatched** | Faces spall, material lost permanently | Faces spall | Mortar erodes early |

## How Each Material Fails

**Limestone** is sedimentary, laid down in visible **bedding planes**, and porous. Absorbed water freezes and splits it along those layers — **delamination**, the face shedding in sheets. Add spalled edges, cracking across sill spans, eroded carved profiles, and units dropping when the mortar bed beneath them fails.

**Historic brick** is softer and more absorbent than modern face brick. It **spalls** — faces flaking and popping — once water has been getting in long enough to freeze inside the unit.

**Both fail worst on horizontal surfaces**: sills, water tables, steps, coping and copestones, which hold water instead of shedding it.

## Signs a Historic St. Charles Building Needs Restoration

- Mortar joints **receded well behind the face**, or raking out as loose sand.
- **Spalling brick** or **delaminating stone** — faces flaking, shelling or popping.
- Cracked, eroded or displaced **sills, lintels and water tables**.
- **Hard gray mortar standing proud** of softer original masonry — a prior inappropriate repair.
- **A band of damage** matching where somebody repointed a few years ago.
- Open joints and deteriorated crowns on original chimneys.
- **Efflorescence** blooming on interior or exterior masonry.
- **Step cracks** through joints at corners and openings.
- Evidence of **past sandblasting** — an unnaturally open, rough surface that now drinks water.

## Our Restoration Process

1. **Assessment and documentation.** Walk the building, photograph conditions, test the existing mortar's hardness, identify prior inappropriate repairs.
2. **Mortar analysis and matching.** A mix formulated to the original — soft enough to protect the masonry, durable enough to serve. Judged on **cured** samples, since mortar dries lighter than it finishes.
3. **Hand joint preparation.** Raked by hand where the units are soft; power tools only where the masonry can safely take them. Aggressive grinding permanently widens joints and chews the arrises.
4. **Undoing bad repairs** where hard mortar must come out — carefully, without taking original material with it.
5. **Repointing in lifts**, packed and compacted, tooled to the original joint profile.
6. **Unit repair** — matched patching, **dutchman** inserts and repinning on stone; salvaged matched brick where units are beyond saving. Decided unit by unit.
7. **Chimneys, sills and trim** restored to shed water properly.
8. **Gentle cleaning, breathable protection.** No sandblasting, no high-pressure washing, no film-forming sealers — all three damage historic masonry permanently.

## What Drives Cost

- **Scope** — one elevation, a full facade, or the whole envelope including chimneys.
- **Mortar matching complexity** — a custom lime blend takes formulation and test batching.
- **Hand work required** — soft masonry means hand-raking, slower by design.
- **Undoing prior hard-mortar repairs**, frequently the largest single variable on an older building.
- **Stone sourcing** for dutchman and replacement units.
- **Access and staging** — tall Victorians, church elevations and downtown storefronts each differ.
- **Carved and ornamental work**, which is skilled and slow.
- **Review and permitting** where a designation applies.

## Why Deferring Costs More on an Old Building

On a century-old wall, waiting is not just a larger repointing bill later — it is the **permanent loss of original material**. Once the face spalls off handmade brick or delaminates off quarried stone, that material is gone, and every replacement is a compromise against the original. Carved detail that erodes away cannot be reproduced convincingly.

Restoration is always cheaper, and always more authentic, when it happens before the units themselves begin failing.
