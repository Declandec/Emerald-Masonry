## Three eras in one building, failing in the wrong order

A Downers Grove school campus is rarely one building. It is typically a 1920s or 1930s original, one or two 1950s–1960s additions built during the enrolment boom, a 1990s or 2000s addition, and often a separate gym, boiler house or district office.

Facilities directors expect the oldest section to be the worst. It frequently is not.

- **The 1920s–30s original** is usually solid masonry with soft brick and lime-rich mortar, built with generous detailing. Where it has not been repointed with a hard modern mix, it often has the soundest walls on site.
- **The 1950s–60s additions** are where the trouble concentrates: harder, more absorptive brick, long unjointed runs, exposed steel lintels over classroom window bands, and shelf angles now corroding and **rust-jacking** a horizontal crack along a floor line. That crack reads as tired mortar from the parking lot and is nothing of the kind.
- **Every addition meets the older building at a cold joint**, and the two sections move independently. That vertical crack at the junction is not a failure of the mortar; it is a movement joint that was never formally provided. See [where additions meet original masonry](/blog/where-additions-meet-original-masonry-cold-joints).
- **Flat-roofed additions have parapets**, and the coping joints on top of them are the most under-inspected element on any district campus because nobody can see them from grade.

So the scope has to be written **by building and by era**, with a mortar specification for each. One mix across a whole campus is how a district ends up with spalled brick on the 1950s wing five years after a repointing.

## Prevailing wage: decided before the bid, not argued after

In Illinois, whether prevailing wage applies to a given piece of school work turns on **the funding source and how the work is procured**, not on the fact that the owner is a school district. That determination belongs to the district's business office and counsel, and it needs to be settled and stated in the bid documents.

The reason it matters so much here is simple: it materially changes every number that comes back. A package that leaves it ambiguous produces bids that cannot be compared, and a contractor who assumed wrong either loses money or asks for a change order. Our guide to [public works masonry bidding, schools and prevailing wage](/blog/public-works-masonry-bidding-schools-municipal-prevailing-wage) covers what districts typically need to resolve up front.

Bid, performance and payment bond requirements are likewise the district's to set. They appear in the bid documents, and a district should confirm bonding capacity with any bidder early rather than at award.

## What has to be in the package for bids to be comparable

Districts often send out a short description and receive three wildly different numbers. That is not a market signal; it is a scope problem. A biddable masonry package needs:

1. **Quantities by building and elevation** — linear feet of joint, unit replacement counts, coping footage, opening counts — with the measurement assumptions stated
2. **Mortar specification** per era, and the requirement for an approved mock-up panel before production work
3. **Cut-out depth** and repointing method
4. **Access and staging plan** — where pipe staging or lifts can stand, and what has to stay clear
5. **Protection requirements** around entrances, bus lanes, play areas and parking, including overhead protection where anyone walks beneath the work
6. **The completion date**, with the district's required buffer before the first day back
7. **The prevailing-wage position**, stated plainly
8. **Documentation requirements** — COI, W-9, lien waivers, pay-application format, bonding

[How to write a masonry scope for bid](/blog/how-to-write-masonry-scope-for-bid) covers the mechanics in more depth. It is the difference between three comparable bids and three different jobs.
