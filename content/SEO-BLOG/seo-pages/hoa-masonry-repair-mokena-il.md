Association masonry advice is almost always written for a different kind of association than the ones in Mokena. It assumes a professional management company, a current reserve study, a board with a treasurer who does this for a living, and a procurement process.

**Most Mokena associations have none of that.** The townhome, duplex and rowhome communities off LaPorte Road, Wolf Road, 191st and out toward the Metra station are frequently eight to thirty units, **self-managed by three volunteers**, with a reserve balance somebody maintains in a spreadsheet and no reserve study at all. Nobody on the board has bought masonry before, because nobody buys masonry twice in the same decade.

That does not make the work different. It makes **the document** different.

> A self-managed board does not need a forty-page condition report. It needs a scope it can hand to three contractors and get three comparable numbers back, a priority order it can explain to neighbours at a meeting, and a phase plan that fits the money it actually has.

## The problem a small board runs into first

Almost every self-managed association we meet in Mokena has already had the same experience: three quotes that cannot be compared.

One is a single number on a page. One is priced per building. One excludes brick replacement. The spread between them is enormous and the board has no way to tell whether the low number is efficient or incomplete.

That is not bad luck. **It is what happens when nobody supplied a scope**, so each contractor wrote their own — and three contractors reading the same walls will make different assumptions about how much of the joint is actually failing, how deep to cut, whether units get replaced, and how they will reach the second storey.

The fix is to have the scope written **once**, with quantities, before anybody prices it:

- Linear feet of joint to be repointed, **by building and elevation**
- Cut-out depth and repointing method
- The mortar specification, and a cured sample panel approved before production work
- Brick replacement count, and how units will be matched
- Chimney, lintel and sill counts
- The access and staging assumption
- What is deliberately excluded, and what is being deferred to a later phase

Now three bids are three prices for one job. ## Read your declaration before you solicit bids

Two governance points cost small boards real money when they are discovered late.

**Authority thresholds.** Most declarations and bylaws set a limit — by dollar amount, sometimes by contract duration — above which the board cannot commit without an owner vote. Finding that out after choosing a contractor means restarting.

**Common element versus limited common element.** On Mokena's townhome and duplex stock this matters most for **chimneys, patios and entry stoops** serving a single unit. A chimney serving one home is frequently a *limited* common element, which can mean the association performs the repair while cost is allocated differently. Your declaration is the authority, not us and not the neighbour with the strongest opinion. [HOA versus unit owner responsibility](/blog/hoa-vs-unit-owner-who-pays-masonry-repair) covers the distinction.

## Reserves, assessments and the honest conversation

Small associations carry a specific risk that large ones do not: **a single unbudgeted building can be the whole reserve.** With eight to thirty units there is very little room between "we can fund this" and "this is a special assessment."

That makes ranking and phasing more important here, not less. A properly ranked scope lets a board fund the elevation that is actually admitting water this year and hold the sheltered elevations with real numbers attached — which is a decision, on the record, rather than a gap.
