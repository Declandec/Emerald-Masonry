## What a parapet is, and why it is the worst-treated wall on the building

A parapet is the section of wall that continues above the roof line. On a flat-roofed commercial building it exists to conceal the roof, to carry the roof edge detail, and often to provide a place to terminate the membrane.

Structurally it is in the worst position masonry can occupy:

- **Exposed on both faces** — street side and roof side — so there is no warm, dry interior face to dry toward
- **Topped horizontally**, so water sits on it rather than running off it
- **Unrestrained at the top**, so it moves more than the wall below it
- **Invisible from grade**, so nobody looks at it until something inside gets wet

The result is that the parapet reaches the end of its service life well before the elevation beneath it, and gets inspected years later.

## The five failure points, in the order we find them

### 1. Coping joints

The coping is the cap — stone, precast, clay or metal — that sits on top of the wall. Its joints are the single most common water entry point on any flat-roofed Oak Lawn building. When the bed and head joints fail, water goes straight into the core of the wall. [Coping and roofline masonry leaks](/blog/coping-roofline-masonry-leaks) covers the mechanism.

### 2. Missing or unturned through-wall flashing

A properly built parapet has a through-wall flashing at its base, turning water that gets inside the wall back out. On a great deal of 1960s–1990s commercial construction it was omitted, installed unlapped, or is now perforated. It cannot be added without taking courses down, which is frequently why a parapet gets **rebuilt** rather than repointed. See [through-wall flashing in masonry walls](/blog/through-wall-flashing-masonry-walls).

### 3. Scuppers and roof drains

This one is specific and under-discussed. A **scupper** is a deliberate hole cut through the parapet to let roof water out. Its sleeve, its flashing and its sealant all have a shorter service life than the masonry surrounding them, and when a scupper blocks, water ponds directly against the wall it passes through. On buildings with internal drains instead, the equivalent weak point is wherever a drain line or overflow penetrates the parapet.

### 4. The roofing interface

The membrane, the cant strip and the base flashing turned up the parapet are roofing work. The reglet that receives counterflashing, and the coping above it, are masonry work. The **counterflashing** between them is routinely unassigned by both contracts — which is how a building gets a new roof, a repointed wall, and the same leak. [Masonry versus roofing sequencing at the parapet](/blog/masonry-vs-roofing-sequencing-parapet-coping-membrane-il) covers who should own which piece.

### 5. Rooftop equipment

Medical and professional buildings carry a lot of rooftop mechanical. Units, curbs, condensate lines, conduit and gas runs all crowd the roof edge, and anchors driven into the parapet to secure or support them are penetrations in the wettest masonry on the building. Half the time the original installer sealed them; the last service technician did not.
