**"My house is only twenty years old. Why is the brick falling apart?"**

The answer is almost never the brick's age. It is that a specific area of wall has been getting wet on a schedule, and brick that is repeatedly saturated will spall whether it was laid in 1925 or 2005.

## The Three Things Soaking Plainfield Brick

On newer subdivision homes, it is nearly always one of these — and they are all cheap to correct compared to the masonry they destroy.

**1. Lawn irrigation.** This is the leading cause we find on Plainfield's newer stock. A sprinkler head that clips the wall — even lightly, even only on a windy morning — hits the same three feet of brick several times a week from April to October. That brick never fully dries, then goes into winter saturated. The giveaway is a distinct band of spalling and white mineral staining at exactly the height the spray reaches. Rotate the head, adjust the arc, and the mechanism stops.

**2. Grade, mulch and landscaping.** Brick veneer sits on a foundation ledge, and there is a **weep and flashing line at its base that must stay above the soil.** Over twenty years of mulch topped up each spring, that line disappears. Now soil is holding moisture directly against brick, the weep holes are buried, and the drainage cavity behind the veneer has nowhere to discharge. Pull the beds back down below the weep line and the wall starts drying again.

**3. Downspouts and grading.** A downspout discharging at the foundation, a splash block that has drifted, or a low spot where water pools against the wall. Same result, concentrated at one corner — which is why spalling on these homes is so often localized to one elevation near a downspout.

There is a fourth, seasonal one: **de-icing salt** on walks and drives splashing onto the lowest courses. See [road salt and de-icer brick damage](/blog/road-salt-deicer-brick-damage-illinois).

## Understanding Brick Veneer — It Is a Drainage System

This is the piece most homeowners were never told, and it changes how the whole repair makes sense.

On virtually every Plainfield home built since the 1970s, the brick is **veneer**: a single wythe of brick standing in front of a wood-framed, sheathed wall, with an air cavity between them and metal ties anchoring it back.

**The brick is not holding up your house.** It is a rain screen.

And critically, **the system expects water to get behind the brick.** Wind-driven rain passes through mortar joints — that is normal and designed for. The cavity, the flashing at the base, and the **weep holes** you can see every few feet along the bottom course exist to catch that water and send it back out.

Which is why two things matter enormously:

- **Weep holes must stay open.** Mulched over, caulked shut, or filled with mortar by a well-meaning past repair, and the cavity becomes a bathtub.
- **The base flashing must stay above grade.** Buried, and the drainage path is defeated.

## Signs You Need Brick Replacement in Plainfield

- **Faces flaking, popping or sheeting off** the brick — spalling, the clearest signal.
- **A horizontal band of damage** at sprinkler height, or concentrated at the bottom two or three courses.
- **Brick that crumbles or powders** under a key.
- **Cracked units**, cracked straight through rather than just at the joint.
- **White chalky efflorescence** blooming on the surface.
- **Weep holes you cannot find** along the bottom course.
- **Mulch or soil sitting above the base of the brick.**
- **Step cracks** running diagonally through joints — often movement rather than water, and worth a look.
- **Loose or shifted brick**, especially near a corner.
- **Rust staining below a window or garage opening** — that is a lintel, and it is a different repair.

## Our Brick Replacement Process

1. **Free on-site assessment.** We map which units have actually failed, and — more importantly — we trace the water. Sprinkler pattern, mulch height, downspout discharge, grade, weep holes, flashing line.
2. **Correct or specify the water source.** Sometimes it is a five-minute sprinkler adjustment we point out; sometimes it is grading or downspout work. Either way it is identified in writing before masonry starts.
3. **Source matched brick.** Current-production match where available, or salvaged units — including harvesting sound brick from a hidden elevation and putting the new units there instead.
4. **Remove failed brick by cutting the mortar joints, never the brick.** Neighboring units and the wall ties behind stay undamaged.
5. **Inspect the cavity.** With units out, we check the ties, the flashing and the weep path — the one moment you can actually see behind the wall.
6. **Set new units** in matched mortar, in the correct bond, with joints packed in lifts.
7. **Tool the joints to match** the surrounding profile.
8. **Clear the weep holes** along the affected course so the wall drains as designed.
9. **Clean down before the mortar sets hard**, protecting the faces.
