## Veneer Fails at Points, Not Across the Field

This is the single most useful thing to understand about newer masonry.

**Solid multi-wythe masonry** — the pre-1940 stuff — is several courses thick and structural. It absorbs water and dries outward, and it deteriorates fairly evenly as its mortar ages out.

**Brick veneer** is a single decorative layer over a framed or block backup, with an air cavity behind it. It is **designed to leak**: water gets past the brick, runs down the back face, hits **through-wall flashing**, and exits through **weep holes** in the bottom course.

That system works — until one specific detail fails. So on a New Lenox home the field brick is often perfectly sound while these points are not:

| Failure point | Why it goes | What you see |
|---|---|---|
| **Chimney** | Exposed on four sides, no interior heat drying it | Staining, spalling, cracked crown |
| **Lintels over wide openings** | 1990s picture windows, sliders, 3-car garages are long spans | Rust stains, horizontal crack above opening |
| **Sills** | Horizontal surfaces that hold water once slope fails | Spalled brick streaking below the window |
| **Flashing** | Rushed or mis-detailed during a fast build | Efflorescence, damp interior walls, no visible cause |
| **Weep holes** | Mortared shut by a crew who thought they were gaps | Trapped moisture, efflorescence at the base |
| **Isolated joint runs** | Under a leaking gutter, shaded elevation | Localized receded joints |

**A crew that mortars your weep holes shut has converted a drained wall into a bucket.** We leave them open and verify they are draining before we finish.

## Signs Your New Lenox Brick Needs Repair

- **Spalling faces** — brick flaking, shelling or crumbling, worst near grade, below sills and on shaded elevations.
- **Orange rust staining** running down from a window, door or garage opening.
- **A horizontal crack** in the joint directly above an opening.
- **Cracked brick**, particularly cracks running through the units rather than following joints.
- **Loose brick** that moves under hand pressure.
- **Step cracks** climbing diagonally through the mortar joints.
- **Efflorescence** — white chalky bloom proving water is moving through the wall.
- **Blocked or missing weep holes** at the bottom course.
- **A bulge or bow** in the wall plane — structural, needing evaluation rather than a patch.

## Why Brick Fails Here

**Freeze-thaw is the engine.** Not extreme cold, but repeated crossings of the freezing point with water present — dozens each Will County winter, plus more in the shoulder seasons. Each cycle expands trapped water about nine percent inside the brick.

**Clay soils move.** Will County's heavy clay swells and shrinks seasonally, producing step cracking at corners and around openings.

**Fast-build detailing.** Rapid subdivision construction is not inherently worse work, but flashing, weep spacing and lintel detailing are exactly the items that suffer when schedules compress — and they are invisible until years later.

**And the steel is now mature.** A 1997 house has thirty-year-old lintels. Steel is entirely reliable until it gets wet, and then it expands to several times its original volume inside a rigid wall.

## What Waiting Costs

On veneer specifically there is a hidden risk worth naming. The **ties** holding the brick skin to the building are steel, and they are corroding on the same timeline as your lintels — driven by the same water. You cannot see them, which is precisely why the visible symptoms are worth acting on early.

Spalling is also progressive: once a brick loses its fired face, the porous body underneath absorbs water far faster, deteriorates more quickly, and starts wetting its neighbors.
