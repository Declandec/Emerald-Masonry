## Why parapets fail first, everywhere

A parapet is the most abused masonry on any commercial building, and the reason is geometry.

Every other wall on your building is weathered on **one** face, with conditioned interior space on the other side helping it dry. A parapet is weathered on **both faces and across the top**, with outdoor air on both sides and nothing sheltering it. It gets wet from three directions and it dries slowly.

Then the region does the rest. Chicagoland runs through dozens of freeze-thaw cycles in a typical winter — repeated crossings of 32°F rather than one long freeze — and each one expands water held inside saturated masonry. **Wet masonry plus repeated freezing is the whole mechanism**, and no element on your building stays wetter than the parapet.

Add the specific loads a retail parapet carries:

- **Coping** that has to act as the roof of the wall, with joints that open as the wall moves
- **Roof drainage** — scuppers and overflow scuppers cut straight through it
- **Rooftop equipment** anchored near or through it
- **Sign bands, brackets and lighting** penetrating it for tenant signage
- **Snow** that drifts and banks against it and stays

## The coping is the whole story

If you take one thing from this page: **the coping is the roof of the parapet, and open coping joints are the single most common cause of parapet failure in this area.**

Water entering an open coping joint does not run down the face where you can see it. It goes into the **core** of the wall — the collar joint between wythes — and stays there. Through the winter it freezes, expands, and pushes the wythes apart from inside. By the time the outer face is visibly bulging or the joints are blown, the damage is structural and the repair is a rebuild rather than a repointing.

So a parapet assessment starts on the roof, at the coping, not on the sidewalk.

What we look at up there:

- **Coping joints** — open, cracked, previously caulked over rather than repointed
- Whether the coping has **positive slope** and a **drip edge**, or sits flat and lets water sit
- **Through-wall flashing** beneath the coping and at the base of the parapet
- Where the **roofing base flashing** terminates and how the **counterflashing** is set — into a cut reglet in a mortar joint, or surface-caulked to the brick face, which fails within a few seasons
- **Scupper and overflow** condition
- Whether the parapet is **plumb**, sighted along its length

## Repair or rebuild: the honest criteria

| Condition | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| Eroded joints, a few failed units, wall plumb and true | **Repair** — repoint, replace units, address coping and flashing |
| Coping joints open but the wall behind is sound | **Repair** — reset coping, restore flashing, repoint |
| Outer wythe bulging or separating from the back-up | **Rebuild** the affected section from sound bearing |
| Leaning, bowing, or courses stepped out of line | **Rebuild** — displacement, not deterioration |
| Wall moves when pushed, or units lift by hand | **Rebuild** immediately; protect below in the meantime |
| Rebuilt once already, failing again in the same place | **Rebuild** and correct the water source that was never addressed |

The last row matters more than it looks. A parapet that has been repaired twice and failed twice almost always has a coping or flashing problem nobody ever fixed. Repointing the face of a wall that is being filled with water from above is a scheduled repeat.

## The seam between two trades

Most parapet leaks live where the mason's work meets the roofer's work — and that seam is exactly the part neither trade's proposal usually mentions.

The practical fix is to settle it **in writing before either trade mobilizes**:

- Who sets the **counterflashing**, and is it going into a **cut reglet** or surface-applied?
- Who supplies and installs the **through-wall flashing** at the parapet base?
- Who is responsible for the **coping** and its joints?
- What is the **sequence** — masonry before roofing, or after?
- Who protects the **roof membrane** during masonry work?

We put that split in our scope. When a roofer is already engaged, we coordinate the sequence with them directly so the owner is not the messenger between two trades.

## Materials and method

1. **Correct the water first** — coping, flashing, scuppers, drainage.
2. **Cut failed joints to depth**, roughly two and a half times the joint width, never skimming over failed material.
3. **Repoint with matched mortar** — **Type N** for most commercial parapet work; **Type S** where exposure genuinely warrants it. Mortar stays softer than the unit it holds.
4. **Match color, texture and profile** against a cured sample panel.
5. **Rebuild** where the wall is displaced — taken down to sound bearing, rebuilt with matched units, correct **wall ties** and **through-wall flashing** installed as it goes back up.
6. **Reset coping** with proper slope, drip edge and correctly detailed joints — not a bead of sealant across the top.
7. **Cure and protect** the work rather than letting it flash-dry.
