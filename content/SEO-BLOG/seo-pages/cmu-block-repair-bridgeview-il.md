## Block vs. Brick: What Actually Differs

| | Clay brick | Concrete block (CMU) |
|---|---|---|
| **Long-term movement** | Expands slightly over time | **Shrinks** as it cures, then moves with temperature |
| **Movement joints** | Expansion joints | **Control joints**, at closer intervals |
| **Unit** | Small, solid | Large, usually hollow, sometimes grouted and reinforced |
| **Typical failure** | Spalled faces, eroded joints | Shrinkage cracking, control-joint failure, impact damage |
| **Repair method** | Cut out and reset units | Often cut out a full section; reinforcement may be involved |
| **The classic mistake** | Mortar too hard for the brick | **Mortaring across a control joint** |

That last row matters more than any other. A control joint is a **designed crack** — a place the wall is allowed to move. Fill it with mortar and the movement does not stop; it simply reappears as a new crack a few feet away, usually somewhere less convenient.

## What We See on Bridgeview Industrial Property

That masonry takes damage weather alone never produces.

### Impact damage

Trailers back against dock walls dozens of times a week. Forklifts clip overhead-door jambs. Trucks catch building corners on tight drive aisles. Impact breaks units at the strike point and **breaks the bond well beyond it**, so units that look intact can be debonded — which is why we sound the wall rather than repairing only what is visibly broken.

### Control-joint failure

Sealant in a control joint has a service life. Once it splits or pulls away, water enters the joint and the wall behind it, and in a freeze-thaw climate that becomes cracking and spalling around the joint itself.

### Water at grade and at openings

Dock aprons and drive aisles are heavily salted. Salt-laden meltwater is absorbed by block, salts crystallise inside the pores, and the units break down from within — concentrated at exactly the height where impact damage also lives.

### Painted block hiding a water path

Paint on block traps moisture. Where a wall is repeatedly repainted rather than repaired, the failure continues behind the coating and shows up as blistering, spalling and efflorescence.

### Cracking that is actually structural

Widening, offset or stepped cracking through a bearing area — or a wall out of plane — is not a masonry patch. It needs a structural engineer's assessment first, and we will say so rather than pointing over it.

## Our Repair Process

1. **Assess and classify** — shrinkage, control-joint, impact, water or structural. The classification decides everything downstream.
2. **Sound the wall well beyond the visible damage** to find debonded units.
3. **Involve an engineer** where the wall has moved or a bearing area is affected.
4. **Shore where required**, particularly at openings and load-bearing sections.
5. **Cut out to sound, bonded masonry** and address any reinforcement or grouted cells involved.
6. **Rebuild with matched units** — size, colour and texture, including split-face and scored block where present.
7. **Restore control joints properly** — backer rod and sealant, never mortar.
8. **Repoint and seal joints**, correct the water path at grade and at openings.
9. **Add protection** — bollards, guide posts, corner guards, replacement dock bumpers — so the same strike does not repeat.
