We should be direct about something, because this is the service most often sold to people who do not need it.

**Sealing a brick wall with open mortar joints does not waterproof it. It seals the water in.**

Water gets into a masonry wall primarily through **failed mortar joints**, not through the face of the brick. Coat the surface while the joints are still open and you have changed nothing about the entry path — but you have made it harder for the wall to dry. That is worse than doing nothing.

## Breathable Sealer vs. Waterproof Coating

This distinction decides whether sealing helps your house or damages it.

| | Breathable penetrating sealer | Film-forming coating / paint |
|---|---|---|
| **How it works** | Penetrates the masonry, repels liquid water | Forms a skin on the surface |
| **Vapor** | Passes through — the wall still dries outward | Blocked — moisture is trapped inside |
| **Appearance** | Essentially invisible | Visible sheen or color change |
| **On Chicagoland masonry** | Appropriate where warranted | **Causes the spalling it was meant to prevent** |
| **Failure mode** | Gradually stops repelling; reapply | Blistering, peeling, brick faces spalling from behind |

A masonry wall in this climate has to be able to **dry outward**. Trap moisture behind a film and the water that inevitably gets in — through a failed sill, a chimney, a gap at a window — has nowhere to go. It freezes inside the brick and breaks the face off from behind.

**This is why we never apply film-forming coatings, and why painting brick is one of the most expensive mistakes a homeowner can make.**

## When Sealing Actually Helps

- **After repointing**, on a wall with sound joints, as added protection on a weather-beaten elevation.
- **On highly absorbent masonry** — soft older brick that takes on water readily.
- **On chimneys**, exposed on four sides with no interior drying them.
- **At grade and splash zones**, where road salt and splash-back keep masonry saturated.
- **On stone sills, water tables and horizontal surfaces** that hold water rather than shedding it.
- **On a south or west elevation** taking the most driving rain.

## When Sealing Is the Wrong Answer

- **When the joints have failed.** Repoint first. Always.
- **When you have efflorescence** and have not found the water source. That white bloom means water is actively moving through the wall — sealing over it treats a symptom.
- **When brick is already spalling.** Replace the failed units first.
- **When a chimney crown, flashing or sill is the actual leak.** Sealing the wall does nothing about water entering above it.
- **Blanket-sealing a modern brick veneer wall**, which is designed to drain through flashing and weep holes rather than be sealed.
- **When somebody proposes it as a substitute for repointing.** That is the clearest signal to get another opinion.

## Signs You May Benefit — and Signs You Have a Different Problem

**Sealing may help:** brick that darkens heavily and stays dark for days after rain; a weather-beaten elevation with sound joints; a chimney with fresh repointing; heavy salt splash at grade.

**You have a different problem:** efflorescence, spalling faces, interior water stains, rust staining below a window, receded joints you can rake with a screwdriver, or a cracked chimney crown. Those need [tuckpointing](/services/tuckpointing), [brick repair](/services/brick-repair), [lintel repair](/services/lintel-repair) or [chimney repair](/services/chimney-repair) — not a sealer.
