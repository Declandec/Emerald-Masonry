## What Actually Fails on Limestone

Limestone is sedimentary, deposited in visible **bedding planes** — horizontal layers that are planes of relative weakness. It is durable and handsome, and it is **porous**, so it takes on water.

In a Cook County winter that absorbed water freezes inside the stone, expands, and splits it **along those layers**. Four failure modes follow:

- **Delamination and spalling** — the face shedding in sheets or shells following the bedding.
- **Cracking** — often across a sill span, or radiating from a bearing point.
- **Surface erosion** — the slope flattening and the crisp nose profile rounding over. **The quiet one**, because the sill still looks intact while it has stopped shedding water.
- **Displacement** — a unit dropping or tilting because the mortar bed beneath it failed.

**Horizontal stone fails first**, always, because it holds water rather than shedding it. Sills, water tables, steps and caps take the worst of it.

## Signs Your Riverside Home Needs Stone Repair

- Sills **flaking, shelling or crumbling** at the face or the nose.
- **Cracks** running through a sill, lintel or trim unit.
- **Masonry below a window streaked, stained or spalling** — the strongest single indicator.
- **Open or missing mortar joints** where stone meets brick.
- **Failed or missing caulking** at the sill-to-window joint.
- Stone that has **dropped, tilted or shifted** out of plane.
- **A sill that no longer looks sloped** — check with a level; a flat sill is a failed sill.
- **Rust staining** on the stone, indicating corroding embedded metal or a steel lintel behind it.
- **Eroded carved detail** on trim, porches and door surrounds.
- **Prior hard-mortar repairs** — gray Portland pointing against soft historic stone.

## The Repair Hierarchy — Least Invasive First

In a landmark district this ordering is not conservatism. It is the standard the work is judged against.

| Approach | What it involves | When it is right |
|---|---|---|
| **Repointing and recaulking** | Restoring joints and sealant around sound stone | Stone intact, joints failed |
| **Matched patching** | Cementitious patch rebuilding lost material | Localized spalling and edge loss |
| **Dutchman repair** | Cutting out the damaged area, fitting matched new stone | Significant local damage, unit otherwise sound |
| **Repinning / resetting** | Removing, rebedding and anchoring a shifted unit | Displaced but intact stone |
| **Full replacement** | New matched limestone unit | Structurally failed or broken — last resort |

## Why the Mortar Decides the Outcome

**The governing rule: mortar must be softer than the masonry it holds.** Mortar is the sacrificial layer — it absorbs movement and moisture stress and wears out first, because replacing mortar is cheap and replacing a 150-year-old carved unit is not.

At a stone joint, a hard modern **Portland** mix concentrates stress **at the stone face** rather than in the joint. On porous, layered limestone that means accelerated delamination — **the repair destroying the material it was meant to protect.** On Riverside's pre-1900 and early-1900s stock we use lime-based mixes deliberately.

If a previous contractor pointed your stone with hard gray mortar and the faces have started shedding since, those two facts are related.

## Our Process

1. **Free on-site assessment.** Every stone element examined, slope checked on sills, and — critically — the water path identified.
2. **Correct the water path first.** Failed joints, missing caulking, bad flashing, a clogged gutter discharging onto stone.
3. **Cut back to sound material** without over-cutting. On soft historic stone this is hand work.
4. **Method selected per unit** — patching, dutchman, repinning or replacement, decided unit by unit rather than applied across the board.
5. **Matched stone and mortar.** Indiana limestone and matched stone, with soft **lime-based mortar** on pre-1920 walls.
6. **Restore the slope.** A repaired sill that still does not shed water has not been repaired.
7. **Sealant and breathable protection** — new backer rod and sealant where needed, vapor-permeable products only.
8. **Photo documentation** of conditions and completed work, for your records and any commission file.

## What Drives Cost

- **Number of units** — a few sills versus stone across a full facade.
- **Repair method** — patching is less involved than a dutchman; a dutchman less than replacement.
- **Height and access** — upper-floor sills need staging.
- **Matching difficulty** — unusual grain, color or a carved profile.
- **Hand work required** on soft historic stone.
- **Undoing prior hard-mortar repairs**, which is delicate and slow.
- **Related damage** — a rusting steel lintel behind the stone, or spalled brick below a failed sill.
- **Review and documentation** where preservation approval applies.
