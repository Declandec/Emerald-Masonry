## Block behaves differently than brick

Three properties drive everything about how CMU fails.

**It is porous.** Concrete masonry units absorb considerably more water than fired face brick. **Split-face block** — the decorative texture on a great many 1980s-and-later commercial elevations — is worse again, because splitting the unit exposes its open aggregate structure directly to weather. Water does not need a crack to get into a split-face wall. It moves through the field of the block.

**It shrinks.** Fired clay brick expands slightly over its life. Concrete block does the opposite: it shrinks as it cures and dries. That is why block walls are built with **control joints** — deliberate vertical breaks that let the wall move without cracking wherever it feels like. Brick walls need expansion joints; block walls need control joints; they are not the same thing and they are not interchangeable.

**It is often structural.** On a warehouse or a flex building the block is frequently load-bearing, not a veneer hanging on a frame. That changes what unit replacement involves and raises the stakes on anything that looks like movement.

## What actually fails on Tinley Park block buildings

### Control joint sealant

The single most common source of interior water on a block building, and the cheapest thing on the wall. When the sealant in a control joint hardens, tears, or lets go along one edge, the joint becomes a **full-height water path** straight into the wall. From inside, it presents as a damp vertical stripe that everyone blames on the roof.

### Joint deterioration

Mortar joints on block erode the same way they do on brick, and the horizontal bed joints on exposed elevations go first.

### Spalling and face loss

Freeze-thaw taking the face off units, concentrated at the base of the wall where splash and de-icing salt collect, and around openings.

### Cracking

**Vertical or stepped cracking** usually indicates movement or shrinkage that the control joints did not accommodate — often because there were too few of them, or they were placed poorly. **Long horizontal cracking** running with the coursing is a different signal entirely: it suggests the wall is being pushed, and that is a structural conversation before it is a masonry one.

### Coatings that trap moisture

A great many block buildings have been painted or coated at some point. A film-forming coating on a porous block wall blocks vapour from leaving, so moisture that gets in behind it — and it always does — has nowhere to go but into the freeze-thaw cycle. Blistering, peeling paint on a block elevation is a symptom, not a paint problem.

## How we repair it

1. **Find the water path before pricing the wall.** Control joints, bed joints, the field of the block, penetrations, coping and roof edge above, and flashing. On a block building, the entry point is frequently above the stain and never inside it.
2. **Repoint deteriorated joints** with mortar matched in type and profile.
3. **Repair and reseal control joints** — cut out the failed material entirely, clean the joint faces, install correctly sized backer rod, and tool a new bead. Caulking over an old bead does not bond and fails within a season.
4. **Replace spalled and failed units**, matched as closely as block allows.
5. **Repair cracks** appropriately to their cause — and escalate to a structural engineer where the pattern indicates movement rather than deterioration.
6. **Address coatings** that are trapping moisture, where that is what is happening.
7. **Seal the field of the wall** with a breathable penetrating product where the wall genuinely benefits — after the repairs, not instead of them.

## Materials

Mortar matched to the wall in type and joint profile. Replacement units matched as closely as commercially available block allows, with the honest caveat that a new CMU will read newer than a forty-year-old one for some time. Backer rod and elastomeric sealant selected for movement capability at control joints. Breathable penetrating sealers rather than film-forming coatings on any wall that has to dry.
