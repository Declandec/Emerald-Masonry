## Why the three proposals don't match

They almost never do, and it is usually not because anyone is gouging you. **You are looking at three different projects.**

One bidder walked two buildings and assumed the rest match. One priced spot repointing of visible failures. One priced full elevations. One included the chimneys, one excluded them. One carried staging for the rear elevations; one assumed ladder access. None of them said which.

So the fix is not to negotiate the numbers. It is to **make everyone bid the same thing.** Ask every bidder, in writing:

1. **Which buildings and which elevations** are in the scope — listed individually?
2. **What quantities** — square feet of repointing, count of units to be replaced, number of chimneys and lintels, linear feet of sealant?
3. **How deep are the joints cut?** (The answer should be roughly two and a half times the joint width, not "we grind them out.")
4. **What mortar**, and how is it matched?
5. **What staging method**, and does the price include it?
6. **How are hidden conditions priced** — is there a unit rate carried in the bid?
7. **What is excluded?**

A bidder who can answer all seven in one page has done the work. A bidder who cannot has given you a number, not a proposal — and the difference will surface as change orders after the board has already voted.

## Start with a condition assessment, not a bid

If your association has no reserve study, the single most valuable thing you can buy is not repointing. It is **an assessment of every building, documented and ranked.**

That produces the two things a board actually lacks:

- **A defensible order of work.** Not "the building the loudest owner lives in," but a risk ranking anyone can read.
- **Real numbers** to put into a budget or a reserve study, instead of a figure someone guessed at.

We walk every building, sound the masonry rather than eyeballing it, photograph conditions by building and elevation, and hand the board a document it can act on — including the parts that do **not** need doing this year, which is usually more of the property than people expect.

## Your buildings age as a cohort

Here is the structural fact that shapes association masonry, and it cuts both ways.

When eight or twelve buildings go up inside the same eighteen months, with the same brick, the same mortar, the same lintels and the same details, **they do not fail in a convenient rotation. They arrive at the end of their mortar's service life together.**

The bad news is that everything qualifies at once and the budget does not.

The good news is bigger than most boards realize:

- **One building predicts the others.** Real opened-wall quantities from a pilot building replace guesswork across the whole property.
- **One mobilization covers many buildings**, which is materially cheaper per building than separate visits in separate years.
- **One mortar and brick match** is established once and carried across the property, so building nine matches building one.

That is why we often scope one building completely, execute it, and then price the balance as **unit work** using what the first building actually revealed.

## Ranking by risk, so a board can vote

| Priority | What belongs here | Why it leads |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Loose or displaced units above entries, walks and patios; unstable chimney crowns | Falling material is the only failure that becomes an incident |
| 2 | Active water entry — failed lintels, open joints on weather elevations, chimney and flashing leaks | Water in a wall turns repointing into rebuilding, and produces unit-owner claims |
| 3 | Accelerating deterioration where joints have receded past the face of the brick | Quantities grow every winter this waits |
| 4 | Appearance — stained, mismatched or previously patched areas | Real value, no urgency |

Each phase gets its own price. A board can approve phase one at the next meeting and take phase two into the following year's budget with a number it can trust — which is frequently what prevents a special assessment rather than causing one.

## Common element or unit owner?

The question every association masonry conversation eventually reaches, and the honest answer is: **read your declaration.**

Your governing documents define **common elements**, **limited common elements** and **unit boundaries**, and those definitions decide who pays. Exterior walls are usually common elements and therefore the association's responsibility. But **chimneys, patios, balconies, stoops and privacy walls** are treated inconsistently from one declaration to the next — sometimes common, sometimes limited common with the owner responsible for maintenance, sometimes explicitly the owner's.

What a mason can contribute is precision about **what is broken and where**, in writing and by element, so the board's discussion — and its attorney's, if it gets that far — is about a documented condition rather than a memory of a phone call.

## What drives the cost

**How much wall is actually failing**, not total square footage. **The number of buildings and whether they can be done in one mobilization.** **Height and staging.** **The number of chimneys and lintels**, which are separate, slower operations. **How difficult the brick and mortar are to match.** **Access constraints** on rear elevations, courtyards and between buildings. And **what is found once the first wall is opened** — which is exactly why a pilot building and carried unit prices are worth more to an association than the lowest headline number.
