Oak Forest's association stock is a particular kind of building. The brick condominium buildings and townhome rows put up from the 1970s through the 1990s — many of them within reach of the Metra station and along the 159th Street and Cicero Avenue corridors — are typically **three-storey brick veneer over a frame or block back-up**, with steel lintels over every window, cantilevered or post-supported balconies, and in the townhome communities a chimney chase or masonry chimney per unit.

Most of these associations are **professionally managed** and already have a **reserve study**. That changes the job. The board is not asking "what is wrong with the walls" in a vacuum. It is asking whether the wall matches the spreadsheet — and what to do when it does not.

## Where reserve studies and masonry stop agreeing

A reserve study is a funding plan. It assigns each component a useful life, a remaining life and a replacement cost, usually from a visual walk-around. For masonry that typically produces lines such as *tuckpointing — 25% of façade*, *lintel replacement*, *balcony repairs* and *sealant replacement*.

Those lines are reasonable planning figures. The problem is that nobody probed a joint to write them. In our experience on 1970s–90s south-suburban condo buildings, the study tends to be wrong in a predictable way:

- **Repointing quantity is spread evenly** across the property, when in practice the south and west elevations and the parapet or top courses are a full cycle ahead of the sheltered faces
- **Lintels are carried as a future item**, when rust-jacking at the bearing ends is already cracking brick over the windows
- **Sealant is a separate line** from masonry, even though the two are done from the same staging and should be bid together
- **Balconies are a single number**, when the masonry at the balcony connections is the part that is failing

None of that means the study was bad. It means the masonry scope should be written to **talk back to it**.

## How we map HOA masonry repair in Oak Forest to your reserve lines

For a managed Oak Forest association, our survey and scope are built around the reserve study rather than beside it:

1. **Every building walked at the wall**, with joints probed, brick sounded, lintels inspected at the bearing ends and balcony connections checked
2. **Quantities by building and elevation** — linear feet of repointing, brick replacement counts, lintel counts and lengths, sealant footage
3. **Each line item tagged to the reserve component it draws from**, so the manager can see what is coming out of which line
4. **Remaining-life notes per elevation** the reserve specialist can use at the next update — "south elevation of Building C is due now, north elevation can hold three to five years"
5. **A risk-ranked phase plan** — overhead hazard and active water entry first, then accelerating deterioration, then appearance
6. **The mortar specification and brick source recorded**, so phase three matches phase one

That last point matters more than it sounds. A multi-year programme only works if the building repaired in year four looks like the one repaired in year one; our guide to [a multi-year masonry programme](/blog/multi-year-masonry-program-associations-portfolios-il) covers how to hold that specification across phases.

## Common versus limited common elements

This is where managed associations most often lose time. On Oak Forest condo and townhome stock, four items routinely sit on the boundary:

### Balconies

The balcony slab, railing and the masonry around its connection to the building are frequently treated differently in the declaration. We price **balcony-connection masonry** as its own line per unit so the manager can allocate it however your documents require. [Balcony and walkway masonry on condo buildings](/blog/balcony-walkway-masonry-condo-multifamily-chicago) covers what fails there.

### Chimneys and chimney chases

A chimney serving one townhome is often a **limited common element**. The association may perform the repair while the cost is assigned to that unit — or not. We list each chimney separately with its own condition note and price.

### Stoops and entry steps

Same pattern. A stoop serving a single entry is priced per stoop, never buried in a building total.

### Shared walls and the main façade

These are almost always common elements and are carried at the building level.

Your declaration is the authority — not us, not the manager's memory of how the last project went. [HOA versus unit owner responsibility](/blog/hoa-vs-unit-owner-who-pays-masonry-repair) walks through the distinction.

## Risks of letting the reserve date decide

If the study says masonry is due in 2029 and a lintel is cracking brick over a second-floor window today, the reserve date is not a reason to wait. Steel lintels expand as they rust and push the brick above them out of plane; open head joints at the top courses let water into the veneer cavity, where it reaches ties and sheathing. Each winter of freeze-thaw turns a repointing item into a brick-replacement item. Deferring on purpose, with numbers on the record, is good management. Deferring because the spreadsheet said so is how a planned project becomes a special assessment.

## What drives the cost

- **Real joint-failure percentage** by elevation, not the study's assumed percentage
- **Lintel count** and whether bearing-end rust has already cracked the brick above
- **Balcony connections and per-unit chimneys** — usually the swing items
- **Access** — three-storey walls, landscaping, parking lots and resident traffic
- **Phasing** — fewer mobilisations is cheaper, but the reserve balance sets the pace

## Owner communication

On a managed property, we feed the manager everything the owners will ask for: a schedule by building and elevation, written notice before work reaches a stack, what to clear from balconies and patios, where parking moves, and how windows and vehicles are protected. Progress photos go to the manager weekly, so the board can answer questions without a site visit.
