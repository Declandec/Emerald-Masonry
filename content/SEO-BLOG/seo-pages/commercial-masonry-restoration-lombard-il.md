## The failure almost every Lombard strip center shares

> A brick wall grows. Fired clay takes up moisture over its first decades and expands irreversibly — it does not shrink back. A long, unjointed run of brick has to relieve that growth somewhere, and it will choose a corner, the edge of an opening, or the point where a movement joint should have been placed and was not.

On a 1970s or 1980s strip center, movement joints were frequently spaced too far apart, placed in the wrong locations, or omitted entirely on the assumption that the control joints in the block backup would do the job. They do not — the veneer and the backup move independently.

The result is the pattern property managers here know without knowing why: **a building gets repointed every ten or twelve years and cracks again in exactly the same places.** The mortar was never the problem. Our guide to [why brick buildings need expansion and movement joints](/blog/masonry-expansion-joints-why-brick-buildings-need-them) sets out the mechanism, and it is the first thing we check on a repeat-crack building.

The second thing we check is the **coping**. A parapet built to conceal a roof is a wall exposed on both faces with a horizontal top — the worst possible condition for masonry. Water enters through open coping joints, runs down inside the parapet core where nothing can dry, and shows up as staining on the interior of the top of a tenant space, two or three bays away from where it got in. [Parapet wall failure signs on commercial buildings](/blog/parapet-wall-failure-signs-commercial-buildings) covers what to look for from the roof rather than the ground.

## Brick front, block sides — two walls, two specifications

Because the street elevation is face brick and the rest of the building is exposed CMU, a Lombard commercial building is effectively **two different walls with two different repair specifications on the same structure.** Pricing it as one square-foot number gets it wrong in both directions.

| | Brick street elevation | Exposed CMU sides and rear |
|---|---|---|
| Typical failure | Joint loss, cracking at openings and corners, spalled faces | Cracked bed and head joints along courses, spalled face shells, failed control joints |
| What drives it | Irreversible expansion, movement-joint spacing, freeze-thaw | Moisture absorption through the block, coating failure trapping water, shrinkage cracking |
| Repair | Repoint to matched mix, replace units, cut in joints | Repoint, patch or replace units, re-detail control joints, breathable coating |
| Visibility | High — ownership and tenants see it | Low — which is why it is deferred until it leaks |

The low-visibility half is where deferred maintenance accumulates. It is also where a great deal of the water gets in.
