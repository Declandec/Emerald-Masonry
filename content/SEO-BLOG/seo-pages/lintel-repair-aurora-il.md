## What a Lintel Is, and Why It Rusts

A **lintel** is the beam that carries masonry across an opening. Across most of Aurora's twentieth-century housing stock it is a bare, ungalvanized steel angle set into the wall above a window, door or garage header.

Steel in masonry survives only as long as it stays dry. Once water reaches it — through an open joint above, a failed sill, deteriorated or absent flashing, or a sealant line nobody has touched in thirty years — it corrodes. And corroding steel does not merely weaken. It **expands to several times its original volume**, and buried in a wall that expansion has nowhere to go but up and out.

That is **rust jacking**: it splits the mortar joint above the steel, lifts the brick course, pushes the face out of plane, and finally loosens brick over a doorway. It is also the single most misdiagnosed masonry defect in Chicagoland. Homeowners tuckpoint the crack; it returns in two seasons, because the crack was never the problem. The orange bleeding that often appears first is covered in our guide to [rust stains and orange streaks on brick](/blog/lintel-rust-stains-orange-streaks-brick).

## The Test That Decides Treat vs. Replace

| Finding at the steel | The proportionate answer |
|---|---|
| Light surface corrosion, full section, sound bearing, no deflection | **Treat** — clean back, treat, prime, flash and repoint above |
| Scaling rust but no measurable section loss or movement | **Treat**, with the water source corrected and the opening monitored |
| Section loss, pitting through, or visible swelling lifting the course | **Replace** |
| Any measurable deflection or sag at mid-span | **Replace** |
| Loss of bearing at one or both ends | **Replace**, and rebuild the bearing pockets |
| Masonry above already loose or displaced | **Replace**, with shoring before removal |

The honest part of that table is the top two rows. A lintel with surface corrosion and its full section is not an emergency, and telling you so costs us a bigger invoice.

## Reading the Crack

- A **horizontal crack** in the joint immediately above an opening — the signature of rust jacking
- **Orange staining** bleeding from the steel edge or running down the brick below
- Brick above the opening **lifted, stepped or bowed** outward
- A **visible sag** at mid-span on a wide garage or picture-window header
- **Step cracks** angling up from the opening corners
- Windows or doors that **stick or bind** under a masonry opening
- **Loose or drummy brick** directly over the head of the opening

## Aurora's Two Kinds of Openings

**Older stone-headed openings.** Aurora's nineteenth-century neighbourhoods on both sides of the Fox River carry openings headed with **stone lintels** rather than steel — locally quarried limestone doing the work directly. Those fail from settlement, from saturation and freeze-thaw, and from adjacent embedded steel corroding nearby. They are repaired as stone work: crack repair, Dutchman patching or replacement in matched stone, pointed in soft, lime-rich mortar. Applying a steel-lintel repair logic to a stone-headed opening is simply the wrong trade.

**Twentieth-century steel-headed openings.** The bungalows, ranches, split-levels and postwar homes across the rest of the city, plus the storefronts and multi-tenant buildings along the commercial corridors, are the bare-angle stock. Wide spans are where it shows first — **attached garage headers and mid-century picture windows** carry more brick over longer spans and deflect more, which is why they consistently fail before narrower openings on the same house.

On commercial and multi-tenant buildings the same failure runs at larger scale, and the sequencing is different — see [lintel replacement on commercial buildings](/blog/lintel-replacement-commercial-buildings).

## Our Repair Process

1. **Diagnose the opening.** Confirm the steel is the cause rather than settlement or a sill problem, check bearing at both ends, and check the rest of the elevation — lintels fail in batches, and finding that out later is expensive.
2. **Shore the masonry above** before any steel is disturbed. This is what separates a controlled replacement from a wall coming down on a garage door.
3. **Remove the brick course carefully and salvage it.** Sound units are cleaned and kept in order for reuse.
4. **Treat or replace the steel.** Where replacement is warranted, new angle is sized to the span, set with proper bearing at each end, and protected against corrosion so this repair does not become the next generation's rust jacking.
5. **Flash and drain the head.** Through-wall flashing and weeps are what keep water off the steel; skipping this is why replacements fail early. Our guide to [through-wall flashing in masonry walls](/blog/through-wall-flashing-masonry-walls) explains the assembly.
6. **Rebuild the brick, matched.** Salvaged and matched units relaid in mortar matched for color, texture and hardness, tooled to your existing joint profile.
