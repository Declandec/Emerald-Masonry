## When a Brick Has Actually Failed

Not every brick problem is a brick-replacement problem. Most masonry issues start in the **mortar joints**, and when the mortar is what's failing — receding, crumbling, or washing out — the fix is **tuckpointing** (repointing the joints), not replacing brick. Brick replacement is specifically for when the **brick unit itself** has given out:

- **Spalling** — the hard fired face has flaked, popped, or peeled off, leaving a rough, soft, exposed interior
- **Cracked through** — a crack runs clean through the brick, not just along the joints
- **Crumbling or soft** — the brick has lost its structural face and can be scratched or gouged
- **Freeze-thaw damage** — water soaked into the brick, froze, expanded, and blew the face off over successive Chicago winters

When a brick reaches that state, mortar work won't save it. The unit has to come out and a sound one has to go in. Left in place, a spalled or cracked brick is an open door for water to get behind the wall.

## Wheaton's Two Kinds of Brick Homes

Here's what makes brick replacement in Wheaton distinct: the town's housing stock splits into two building types, and matching brick works differently on each.

### Older solid-masonry homes

Near downtown Wheaton, the college, and the older historic streets, you'll find **solid masonry** — walls built of multiple structural layers (wythes) of brick. The brick on these homes is often decades old, in a size, color, and texture that's no longer made. Matching it means finding **salvaged or reclaimed brick** of the same era, or — when the match has to be exact on a prominent, street-facing elevation — sourcing the closest replacement and reserving it for a less visible spot. On solid masonry we also match the brick's **hardness**, because a too-hard replacement behaves differently than its softer neighbors.

### Newer brick-veneer homes

Across Wheaton's 1980s–2000s subdivisions, most brick is **veneer** — a single decorative layer of brick anchored to a framed, sheathed wall behind it. The challenge here isn't age, it's **discontinued colors**: the specific brick your builder used may no longer be manufactured. Matching a veneer home means sourcing the nearest current brick and **blending** it across the field so the eye doesn't land on a patch. Because veneer is only one layer with a drainage gap behind it, we also make sure water that gets behind the brick can still escape as designed.

Knowing whether you have veneer or solid masonry changes the whole repair — so we identify it before we cut a single brick.

## Our Brick Replacement Process

Replacing brick cleanly is careful, patient work. Rushing it damages the good brick around the failure. Our process:

1. **Assess and identify** — confirm which bricks have truly failed, and whether the wall is veneer or solid masonry.
2. **Source the match** — size, color, texture, and (on older homes) hardness, using salvaged brick or the closest current brick as the wall requires.
3. **Remove the failed unit** — carefully cut out the damaged brick and clear the old mortar without chipping or loosening its neighbors.
4. **Prep the cavity** — clean it out, and dampen the opening so the new mortar cures properly instead of drying out too fast.
5. **Set the replacement** — bed the matched brick in **color-matched mortar**.
6. **Tool the joint** — finish the mortar joint to match the profile of the surrounding wall so the repair disappears into the field.

## Materials and Matching

The two things that make a brick replacement look right are the **brick** and the **mortar**. We match mortar type and, crucially, **mortar color**, because a bright new joint around a replacement brick advertises the repair as loudly as a mismatched brick does. On older Wheaton homes we lean on reclaimed brick; on veneer homes we blend current brick into the field. Done well, you shouldn't be able to pick out the replaced bricks from the sidewalk.

## What Drives the Cost of Brick Replacement in Wheaton

Every wall is different, so we don't quote brick replacement by a flat per-brick price. The factors that move the cost:

- **How many bricks** have failed and whether they're clustered or scattered
- **Access and working height** — ground-level work versus an upper story that needs **scaffolding**
- **Matching difficulty** — a common older brick versus a discontinued veneer color that takes sourcing and blending
- **Scope** — replacing individual units versus a section deteriorated enough to need rebuilding

We put an exact, itemized number in front of you after a free on-site look — never a guess over the phone.
