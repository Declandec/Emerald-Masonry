## Why the sequence matters more than the scope

Masonry damage almost always has a source, and the source is almost always **above** the damage.

Repair spalled brick beneath a window and leave the cracked sill above it, and the brick spalls again. Repoint a chimney and leave the cracked crown, and the new joints wash out. Seal a wall whose joints are open, and you slow the wall's drying without stopping the water getting in.

So the order runs:

1. **Structural and overhead first** — lintels, loose brick above a walkway, a leaning chimney, a wall that has moved.
2. **Active water entry second** — crowns, sills, flashing, failed sealant, open joints on the weather elevations.
3. **Deterioration third** — repointing and unit replacement on walls losing material but not yet leaking.
4. **Appearance last** — cleaning, blending, sealing sound masonry.

An estimate that does not distinguish these is a list, not a plan.

## Burbank, specifically

Burbank is a village built almost entirely of brick, and its housing stock is considerably older than the village itself — much of it built out through the 1940s, 50s and 60s as the southwest side pushed outward along Cicero Avenue, 79th and 87th Street.

That uniformity shapes the work in three ways.

**Everything ages together.** When a whole neighbourhood goes up with the same detailing within a narrow band of years, the mortar, the lintels, and the sills all reach the end of their service lives at roughly the same time. It is why one street can look like it needs a mason all at once — and why, if one window head on your elevation is cracked, the others deserve a look.

**Chicago common brick on the sides and rear.** Pressed face brick went on the street elevation; softer, more absorbent common brick went everywhere else. It spalls readily, and it is intolerant of hard modern mortar and aggressive cleaning — two things that shorten the life of any repair applied to it. Our guide to [why Chicago common brick crumbles](/blog/chicago-common-brick-why-it-crumbles) explains the material.

**Narrow lots.** Burbank is densely platted, and side yards are tight. That affects access and staging on second-storey work more than people expect, and it is worth knowing before the schedule is set.

## What a whole-house assessment covers

- **Mortar joints on all four elevations**, noted separately, because they will not be in the same condition
- **Brick units** — spalling, cracking, loose or displaced material
- **Lintels** above every opening, including the garage
- **Sills and porch caps** — pitch, drip edge, end joints
- **The chimney**, including crown, cap, and flashing
- **Foundation masonry at grade**, where splash and road salt do their work
- **Sealant and any expansion joints**
- **Water management** — downspouts, grade, splash blocks, window wells

That last item is not masonry, and we look at it anyway, because it determines how long the masonry work lasts.

## Materials and matching

**Mortar matched to the wall, not to convenience.** Type and hardness first — mortar must stay softer than the units around it, which matters a great deal on soft common brick. Then colour, sand, and joint profile, so a repointed section reads as part of the wall.

**Brick matched** in size, colour and texture, with salvaged units reused wherever sound. Where matching is genuinely difficult — and on mid-century Chicago-area brick it often is — we will say so and discuss taking replacement units from a less visible elevation rather than pretending a mismatch will not show. Our post on [why new brick looks different](/blog/why-new-brick-looks-different-weathering-in) sets realistic expectations.

**Sealant where sealant belongs, mortar where mortar belongs**, and never the reverse.
