## Caulking Is Not Tuckpointing

This gets conflated constantly, and the confusion causes real damage.

| | Tuckpointing / repointing | Caulking / joint sealant |
|---|---|---|
| **Material** | Cementitious **mortar** — rigid | Elastomeric **sealant** — flexible |
| **Fills** | Bed and head joints between masonry units | Joints designed to move |
| **Purpose** | Structural bond and weather seal | Accommodate movement, seal water |
| **Fails as** | Erosion, recession, cracking | Adhesion loss, splitting, hardening |
| **Service life** | Decades when correctly matched | A wear item — considerably shorter |

**Rigid mortar in a joint designed to move cracks on the first thermal cycle. Sealant where mortar belongs provides no structural bond.** A building needs both, in the right places.

## Where the Joints Are on a Burr Ridge Commercial Building

- **Precast concrete panel joints** — the grid of vertical and horizontal joints between panels, the primary weather barrier on these buildings.
- **Expansion and control joints** designed to let the structure move.
- **Window, storefront and curtain wall perimeters.**
- **EIFS joints and terminations** — where the synthetic stucco skin meets openings, penetrations and other materials. EIFS has very little ability to dry outward, so failed sealant here accumulates water in the sheathing behind it.
- **Door perimeters and louvers.**
- **Coping and parapet joints** at the roof edge.
- **Material transitions** — brick to precast, panel to EIFS, masonry to metal.
- **At-grade joints** where wall meets slab or sidewalk.

## How Sealant Fails, and What Each Mode Means

- **Adhesion failure** — the bead has pulled cleanly away from one joint face. Points to preparation: contaminated substrate, missing primer, or caulking applied over old material.
- **Cohesive failure** — the bead has split down its own middle. The product was over-stretched for the joint.
- **Hardening and crazing** — lost elasticity, usually age and UV.
- **Chalking** — powdery UV-degraded surface.
- **Bubbling, sagging or wrinkling** — installation problems.
- **Missing sections** outright.
- **Staining below a joint** — water running the wall.
- **Interior water staining** on upper floors with a sound roof — the classic symptom.

**South and west elevations reliably fail first**, because they take the most sun and the widest daily temperature swings.

## Why Preparation Matters More Than Product

The failure mode we see most often is not old sealant. It is **new sealant installed badly**.

**Backer rod** is the item that decides it. A sealant bead has to be bonded to exactly **two** surfaces — the opposing joint faces — and free to deform in the middle. Backer rod sets the depth and creates the **hourglass profile** that lets the bead stretch.

Leave it out and the sealant bonds to **three sides**, including the back of the joint. A three-sided bead cannot deform, and the first cold snap that opens the joint tears it.

The other decisive step is **complete removal of the old sealant**. Applying new over failed is the most common shortcut in this trade and it fails again quickly, because the new bead is anchored to a layer that is already letting go.

## Our Process

1. **Free on-site assessment.** Joint condition, width and movement, substrate type, prior repairs, and where water is actually entering.
2. **Full removal of old sealant** — cut out, not caulked over.
3. **Substrate preparation** — joint faces cleaned to sound, clean material.
4. **Repair the substrate** where needed. Spalled precast edges and damaged joint arrises get addressed before sealing.
5. **Install correct backer rod** — right type, right diameter, right depth. Bond breaker tape where the joint is too shallow.
6. **Prime** where the product data sheet requires it for that substrate.
7. **Gun the sealant** with enough pressure to fully wet both joint faces, without voids.
8. **Tool the bead** to shape the profile and force contact with both faces.
9. **Cure and inspect**, with completed elevations documented.

## Why Deferred Sealant Gets Expensive

Sealant is inexpensive relative to nearly everything it protects. Once joints open, water enters the wall assembly and goes after what actually costs money: **corroding embedded steel, anchors and shelf angles, saturated insulation, deteriorating panel edges, interior finishes and tenant space**. On precast construction, water reaching the anchorage is a structural conversation rather than a maintenance one.

Replacing sealant on a cycle is one of the highest-return maintenance items on a commercial building. Replacing it after the leaks start means paying for the sealant plus everything the water reached.
