## Step One: The Survey

Before we price anything, we walk all four elevations, the chimney, the openings and the grade, looking for:

- **Mortar joints** receding, sandy, cracked or missing
- **Brick faces** spalling, flaking, cracked, loose or drummy
- **Limestone sills, bands and porch caps** — cracks, lost wash, worn drip edges, sugaring
- **Chimney** — crown, cap, flashing, joints and the stack above the roofline, which weathers hardest
- **Lintels** — horizontal cracking and lifted brick above windows and doors
- **Sealant joints** at windows, doors and dissimilar materials
- **Water management** — gutters, downspouts, grade, splash zones, window wells
- **Movement** — step cracks, bowing, separation, anything suggesting structure rather than weathering

You get told what we found, in plain language, with the reasoning. Not a list of line items to take on faith.

## Step Two: Ranking It Honestly

| Priority | What it covers | Why it comes first |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Safety and structure** | Loose or displaced brick, failing lintels, unstable chimney | Falling masonry, and damage that compounds |
| **2 — Active water entry** | Open joints, failed flashing, cracked sills, blocked weeps | Every wet cycle makes the next repair larger |
| **3 — Advancing deterioration** | Spalling brick, eroding joints not yet leaking | Far cheaper now than as replacement later |
| **4 — Protective and preventive** | Sealant renewal, breathable sealing, grade and downspout work | Extends everything above it |
| **5 — Cosmetic** | Staining, efflorescence cleaning, appearance blending | Do it once the wall is sound |

## What La Grange Homes Are Actually Made Of

La Grange is one of the older, better-preserved villages on the BNSF line, and its housing stock reflects that: **late-1800s and early-1900s homes** around the historic district and the blocks near the stations, brick and frame Victorians, foursquares, bungalows and 1920s brick homes, plus coach houses, garages and porch masonry that came with them.

Two technical facts govern nearly everything on these houses.

**The masonry is soft, and the mortar must be softer still.** Pre-1930s brick and local limestone were laid in lime-rich mortar because the joint is meant to be the sacrificial, flexible element. Repoint that wall with a hard modern mix and every freeze cycle and every bit of movement gets taken out of the brick faces instead. Spalled mortar can be replaced; spalled century-old brick cannot.

**Cleaning is where irreversible damage happens.** Sandblasting and high-pressure washing strip the fired face off brick and the weathered skin off limestone permanently, leaving the masonry drinking far more water than before. The gentlest method that works is always the right one — see [why sandblasting damages brick](/blog/why-sandblasting-damages-brick).

Add a mature tree canopy that keeps north and east elevations damp, decades of gutters overflowing onto the same stone, and Cook County's freeze-thaw cycling, and you have the village's characteristic pattern: sound-looking walls with quietly failing details.

## The Restoration Work Itself

1. **Fix the causes first** — water paths, drainage, flashing, blocked weep holes.
2. **Repoint what has failed** — joints cut to sound depth and repointed in mortar matched for color, sand, texture, profile and hardness.
3. **Replace failed brick and stone** with matched or salvaged units, dry-laid for your approval before setting.
4. **Restore the chimney** — crown, cap, flashing and stack, the most weather-exposed masonry on any house and the most commonly deferred.
5. **Handle lintels and sills** — corroded steel replaced and flashed; limestone sills repaired so the wash and drip edge shed water again.
6. **Renew sealant joints** with backer rod and the correct sealant for each substrate.
7. **Clean gently** — never blasting, never high pressure.
8. **Seal last**, with a breathable penetrating repellent, once mortar has cured.
