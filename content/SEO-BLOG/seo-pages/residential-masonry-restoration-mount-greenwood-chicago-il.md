## Following the Water, Top to Bottom

### 1. The chimney

The most weather-exposed masonry on the building, unheated, exposed on four sides, and on a great many Mount Greenwood houses no longer in use. A cracked crown lets water straight into the stack; failed flashing puts it into the roof; open joints in the top courses let it into the wall below. This is where restoration should start, because everything downstream is fed from here. Related: [chimney repair in Mount Greenwood](/locations/chimney-repair-mount-greenwood-chicago-il).

### 2. The roofline, parapet and front dormer

Bungalow dormers and the masonry at the roof edge take direct weather with the least protection. Coping and cap joints, dormer cheeks and the transition where masonry meets roofing are all common entry points.

### 3. Limestone banding, sills and porch caps

The decorative stone that gives these houses their character is also the part designed to get wet and throw water clear. A sill that has lost its slope or worn away its drip edge stops throwing water off and starts running it back down the wall — which is why the brick directly beneath a sill is so often the worst brick on the elevation.

### 4. Lintels above openings

A horizontal crack with lifted brick above a window or door means the steel behind it is corroding and swelling. The brick is the symptom; the [lintel](/services/lintel-repair) is the cause.

### 5. The field walls — and the front/side split

Here is the fact that explains most bungalow restorations. These houses were typically built with **hard face brick on the street elevation and softer common brick on the sides and rear.** Two different materials, two different weathering rates, and — critically — two different mortar requirements. A single hard mix applied across the whole house is wrong on the sides even when it is tolerable on the front. Chicago common brick and why it crumbles is covered in more detail in [our guide to it](/blog/chicago-common-brick-why-it-crumbles).

### 6. The gangway walls

The narrow walls between neighbouring houses stay damp, see little sun and dry slowly. They deteriorate faster than any other field wall and get inspected last, because nobody walks down there.

### 7. The bottom three courses

Splash-back off walks and drives, snow banked all winter, de-icing salt, and foundation plantings holding moisture against the wall. The lowest courses live the hardest life on the building — see [why the bottom three courses of brick fail first](/blog/why-the-bottom-three-courses-of-brick-fail-first).

### 8. The water management around it all

Gutters, downspouts, grade and window wells. On a large share of houses the single most valuable item in a restoration is extending a downspout that has been discharging at the same corner for thirty years.

## Ranking and Phasing

Once the house is surveyed, the scope gets ranked honestly: **structural and safety first, then active water entry, then advancing deterioration, then preventive, then cosmetic.** Phasing across two or three seasons is a sound plan when the order is right — and a costly mistake when cosmetic work happens while water is still getting in.

You get told what we found in plain language, with the reasoning, not a list of line items to accept on faith.

## The Restoration Work

1. **Correct the causes first** — water paths, flashing, drainage, blocked weep holes.
2. **Restore the chimney** — crown, cap, flashing and stack, rebuilt to sound masonry where the top courses have gone.
3. **Repair limestone trim** — reform the wash, re-cut the drip edge, patch or replace cracked stone, point in soft mortar, clean gently and never abrasively.
4. **Handle lintels** — corroded steel replaced and flashed, masonry above rebuilt and matched.
5. **Repoint what has failed**, in mortar matched for colour, sand, texture, profile and hardness — with the front and side elevations specified separately where the brick differs.
6. **Replace failed brick** with matched or salvaged units, dry-laid for your approval before setting.
7. **Renew sealant joints** with backer rod and the correct product for each substrate.
8. **Seal last**, with a breathable penetrating repellent, once mortar has cured.
