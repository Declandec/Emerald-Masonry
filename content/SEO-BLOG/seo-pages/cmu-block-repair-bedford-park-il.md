Most masonry advice on the internet is written about brick. **Concrete block is a genuinely different material**, and the difference is not cosmetic.

**Brick is fired clay with a hard, comparatively sealed outer skin.** Water gets into a brick wall mainly through the mortar joints, which is why brick fails at the joints first and why repointing is the dominant brick repair.

**Concrete block is porous across its whole face.** It absorbs broadly, not at the joints. So a block wall wets over its entire surface, holds far more water than an equivalent brick wall, and **spalls in patches across the field** instead of eroding along the joint lines.

Everything else about repairing block follows from that one fact.

## What Actually Fails on a Bedford Park Block Wall

### Face spalling
Absorbed water freezes and pops the face off the unit, usually in patches rather than a line. Worst on the lowest courses and on elevations that stay damp.

### Coating failure
This is the one we get called for most. **Paint on CMU is a moisture trap.** It reduces absorption modestly and reduces drying dramatically, so water that gets in — around a penetration, through a crack, from the top — cannot leave. The face spalls from behind the coating, so the first symptom is peeling paint and the real damage is under it.

### Impact damage
Forklifts, pallets, dock plates, trailer corners, snow equipment. Dock-height and drive-aisle block on an active facility takes physical damage that a wall on a house never sees, and every impact opens the face for water.

### Control joint problems
Concrete block **shrinks** as it cures and continues moving with temperature. Control joints are the deliberate vertical gaps that let it do that. When a control joint has no sealant left, was never installed, or got mortared solid, the wall cracks where it wants to instead of where it was told to.

### Deicer and dock apron damage
Truck courts get the heaviest salt application on any property. Salt-laden water is absorbed and then crystallizes inside the block as it dries, which exerts pressure inside the pores in the same way freezing does.

### Water from the top
Open coping joints, failed roof-to-wall flashing and blocked scuppers feed water into the top of a block wall, where it travels down inside the cores.

## Block vs. Brick: What Changes in the Repair

| | Brick | Concrete block (CMU) |
|---|---|---|
| **Water entry** | Mainly through joints | Across the whole face |
| **Typical failure** | Joint erosion, then spalling | Face spalling in patches |
| **Movement control** | Expansion joints (brick expands) | Control joints (block shrinks) |
| **Unit matching** | Salvaged brick, size and color | Manufacturer, texture and color vary widely |
| **Coating risk** | Real | **Much higher** — more absorbent, more to trap |
| **Correct coating** | Breathable penetrating | Breathable only, after repair |

**The practical takeaway: on block, the coating decision matters more than it does on brick, and it is the decision most often made wrong.**

## Signs Your Block Building Needs Repair

- **Peeling or blistering paint** with rough, crumbling block beneath.
- **Spalled patches** across the face rather than along joint lines.
- **Cracked or gouged units** at dock height and along drive aisles.
- **Open vertical joints** running the full height of the wall with no sealant.
- **Stepped cracking** at corners or near openings.
- **Efflorescence** — white mineral deposits — blooming across the face.
- **Damp or staining on the interior face** of an exterior wall.
- **Mortar joints receded** or washed out, particularly near grade.

## Our CMU Repair Process

1. **Survey and map** conditions by elevation, and identify whether cracking is movement, control-joint related, or settlement.
2. **Find the water.** Roof, coping, flashing, penetrations, grade and dock drainage all get checked before anything is patched.
3. **Remove failed coatings** where they are trapping moisture, using methods that do not further damage the face.
4. **Cut out and replace damaged units** with matched block, toothed into the surrounding wall.
5. **Repoint** deteriorated joints, cut to sound mortar and matched to the existing.
6. **Repair or install control joints** where movement needs somewhere to go, sealed correctly with backer rod.
7. **Patch and resurface** spalled faces where full replacement is not warranted.
8. **Address the water source** — flashing, coping, drainage, penetration sealant.
9. **Recoat only if appropriate**, breathable, and only after the wall is sound and dry.
