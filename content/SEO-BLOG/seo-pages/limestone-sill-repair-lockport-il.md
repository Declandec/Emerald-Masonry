## What Actually Fails on Limestone

Limestone is a sedimentary stone with visible **bedding planes** — the horizontal layers laid down when it formed. It is durable, workable and handsome, and it has one real weakness: it is **porous**, so it takes on water. In a Will County winter that water freezes inside the stone, expands, and pushes it apart along those natural layers.

Four distinct failure modes follow:

- **Delamination and spalling** — the face flaking off in sheets or shells, following the bedding planes.
- **Cracking** — often across the middle of a sill span, or radiating from a corner where something bears on it.
- **Surface erosion** — the crisp profiles of trim and carved detail rounding over and washing away.
- **Displacement** — a sill or lintel dropping, tilting or shifting because the mortar bed beneath it failed.

**Sills fail before anything else on nearly every building, because a sill is a horizontal stone surface whose entire job is shedding water — and once its slope or its joints fail, it starts holding water instead.** Everything around it then gets wet: the masonry below streaks and spalls, the joint above opens, and water finds the window frame and the wall cavity.

## Signs Your Lockport Building Needs Stone Repair

- Sills **flaking, shelling or crumbling** at the face or the nose.
- **Cracks** running through a sill, lintel or trim unit.
- **Open or missing mortar joints** where stone meets stone or brick.
- **Failed or missing caulking** at the sill-to-window joint.
- Stone that has **dropped, tilted or shifted** out of plane.
- **Rust staining** on the stone — corroding embedded metal or a steel lintel behind it.
- **Water staining or damage inside** below a window.
- **Masonry below a sill that is spalling or streaked** — reliable evidence the sill stopped shedding water some time ago.
- **Eroded carved detail** on trim, cornices and door surrounds.
- **Open joints in canal-era stone walls**, where the original mortar has simply reached the end of its life.

## Repair Before Replacement

There is a real difference between a contractor who restores stone and one who only knows how to remove it. Replacement is occasionally right, but it is a last resort — it destroys original material, costs more, disturbs the surrounding masonry, and never matches a hundred-and-fifty-year-old wall perfectly.

| Approach | What it involves | When it is right |
|---|---|---|
| **Repointing and recaulking** | Restoring joints and sealant around sound stone | Stone intact, joints failed |
| **Patching / composite repair** | Matched cementitious patch rebuilding lost material | Localized spalling and edge loss |
| **Dutchman repair** | Cutting out the damaged area, fitting matched new stone | Significant local damage, unit otherwise sound |
| **Repinning / resetting** | Removing, rebedding and anchoring a shifted unit | Displaced but intact stone |
| **Full replacement** | New matched limestone unit | Structurally failed or broken units |

## Why the Mortar Choice Matters on Canal-Era Stone

The rule that governs all of this: **mortar must be softer than the masonry it holds.** Mortar is the sacrificial element — it absorbs movement and moisture stress and wears out first, because replacing mortar is cheap and replacing 1840s quarried stone is not possible in any meaningful sense.

A hard modern Portland mix at a stone joint concentrates stress **at the stone face** instead of in the joint. On porous, layered limestone that means accelerated delamination — the repair actively destroying the material it was meant to protect. We use lime-based mixes on historic stone deliberately, not as a stylistic preference.
