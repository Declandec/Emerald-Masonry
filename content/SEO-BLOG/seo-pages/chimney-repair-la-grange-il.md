## The Mortar Advice That Is Almost Right

You will read, in a lot of places, that historic masonry should be repointed with **Type N mortar**. That is far better guidance than most contractors give, and for a great many early-1900s homes it is correct.

**But it is not automatically correct, and on La Grange's oldest brick it is sometimes wrong.**

Here is why. **A masonry wall is designed so the mortar is softer than the brick it holds.** Mortar is the sacrificial layer — it absorbs movement and moisture stress and wears out first, because replacing mortar is cheap and replacing 1880s brick is not. That rule does not say "use Type N." It says **use something softer than this particular brick.**

Type N still contains Portland cement. On dense, machine-made brick it is appropriately soft. On genuinely soft, low-fired 1880s and 1890s brick — the kind found on La Grange's earliest houses — Type N can still be **harder than the unit**, which inverts the relationship and drives freeze-thaw stress into the brick faces.

### The full gradient

| Mortar | Relative hardness | Where it belongs |
|---|---|---|
| **Type M** | Hardest | Heavy load-bearing, below grade — not chimney stacks |
| **Type S** | High | Below grade, structural, high-lateral-load masonry |
| **Type N** | Medium | Most above-grade brick; typical early-1900s masonry |
| **Type O** | Low | Soft historic brick, low-stress repointing |
| **Custom lime blend** | Lowest | Very soft handmade or low-fired brick; preservation work |

**The right answer is determined on site**, by the era of the stack, the hardness of the existing mortar, and how the brick itself behaves — not by a rule of thumb applied to a whole decade of construction.

If a contractor tells you the mix without ever examining your brick, that is worth noticing.

## Why These Stacks Fail First

Every chimney is the most exposed masonry on a building — above the roofline, weathered on four sides, with no heated interior drying it from behind. La Grange's Victorians add two multipliers:

**Height.** A three-story stack has far more exposed surface than a suburban ranch chimney, and all of it absorbs water.

**Corbelling.** The stepped, projecting brickwork at the top creates **dozens of horizontal ledges that hold water rather than shedding it**. That is why the decorative top deteriorates visibly ahead of the plain shaft below.

Then Cook County freeze-thaw does the work — dozens of crossings of the freezing point each winter, each one expanding water trapped in saturated masonry by roughly nine percent.

## The Three Water Paths

Water enters a chimney assembly in exactly three places. Sealing the wrong one changes nothing, which is why so many "repaired" chimneys keep leaking.

| Path | What it is | Telltale sign |
|---|---|---|
| **Flashing** | Metal sealing the chimney-to-roof joint | Ceiling or attic stains near the chase; rusted or tar-patched metal |
| **Crown** | Sloped slab across the top of the masonry | Cracking on top of the stack; staining running down from above |
| **Joints / brick** | The mortar and units of the stack | Efflorescence, flaking faces, joints you can rake with a screwdriver |

**On a stack that looks sound from the ground but leaks inside, it is usually flashing or crown — not a rebuild.**

## Signs Your La Grange Chimney Needs Work

- **Deteriorated corbelling** — missing, crumbling or displaced units in the decorative top courses.
- **White efflorescence staining** on the stack.
- **Spalling brick faces**, flaking or popping, worst on the north and west sides.
- **Mortar joints that rake out** under a screwdriver, or receded behind the brick face.
- **A cracked or crumbling crown.**
- **Rusted, lifted or tar-patched flashing** at the roofline.
- **Water stains** on ceilings or in the attic near the chimney chase.
- **A visible lean**, or a gap opening between the chimney and the house.
- **Brick or mortar debris** in gutters, on the roof or in the yard.
- **A band of spalling** matching where somebody repointed a few years ago — the signature of a mortar that was too hard.
