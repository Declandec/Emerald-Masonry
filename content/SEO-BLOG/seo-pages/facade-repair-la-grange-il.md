Stand on La Grange Road or Burlington Avenue and look up. Above the awnings, the buildings are what they were in 1905 — pressed brick, cast stone bands, jack arches over the upper windows, a bracketed cornice at the top.

Now look down. The ground floor is not from 1905. It has been remodelled two or three times — a 1950s metal-and-tile update, a 1970s or 80s refit with a lower transom, a recent build-out with new glass and a new bulkhead. Each of those remodels cut into the original masonry.

**That seam is where downtown façades fail**, and it is why a leak reported by a second-floor apartment tenant so often has nothing to do with the second-floor wall.

## Three renovations deep: where the water actually gets in

> A La Grange downtown building typically has four generations of construction stacked in one wall. Original masonry above, one or more storefront remodels below, and a sealant joint between each generation that somebody installed as a temporary measure and nobody has renewed since.

Specifically, we find water entering at:

- **The storefront head** — where a later frame was set into an original opening, often with the original lintel or arch left bearing but no flashing added
- **Infilled transoms** — a lowered ceiling and a bricked-in or panelled transom, with the infill built flush and unflashed
- **The bulkhead at grade** — where a remodel brought a new material up against soft brick that is already wicking moisture from the sidewalk
- **Sign and awning anchors** — decades of tenant signage drilled into the façade band and abandoned
- **Cast stone joints** — bands and sills where the joint between stone and brick has opened
- **The cornice**, whose concealed steel anchors have been corroding since installation

## The mortar rule on soft historic brick

La Grange's downtown commercial stock is mostly **solid masonry with soft brick and lime-rich mortar**. On that wall the joint is designed to be the sacrificial part — *softer than the brick it holds* — so moisture, salt and movement relieve through the mortar, which can be replaced, instead of through the brick face, which cannot.

Repoint it with a hard modern Portland-heavy mix and that relief path closes. Within a few freeze-thaw seasons the brick faces begin popping off around joints that still look perfect. It is the most common and most avoidable damage on this building type. [Lime versus Portland mortar](/blog/lime-vs-portland-mortar) covers the building science, and [what makes historic masonry preservation different](/blog/historic-masonry-preservation-what-makes-it-different) covers the wider discipline.

Two related rules on this stock: **never sandblast** — it removes the fired skin of the brick and makes it permanently absorbent ([why sandblasting damages brick](/blog/why-sandblasting-damages-brick)) — and treat painted brick as a case-by-case decision rather than an automatic strip.

## Mixed use changes the logistics

Most downtown La Grange buildings have retail or a restaurant at grade and offices or apartments above. That means a façade project has **two different tenancies with two different tolerances**:

- The ground-floor business needs its entry, its windows and its sidewalk presence protected during trading hours
- The upper-floor residents need notice, quiet-hours respect, and their windows accessible

And the whole elevation sits directly above a **public sidewalk**, which means overhead protection is not a line item to negotiate — it is how the job gets done at all. We resolve staging, pedestrian protection and working hours at the assessment and write them into the scope, because that is exactly where two façade bids stop being comparable.
