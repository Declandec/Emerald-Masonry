## The Two Kinds of Brick Wall in Naperville

Almost every article about tuckpointing sorts houses by age. That is the wrong first cut. Sort them by **wall type** instead.

**Solid masonry.** Two or more wythes of brick stacked and bonded together, the wall itself carrying load. You will find this downtown — the nineteenth-century commercial buildings along Main and Jefferson, the older stock near Naper Settlement and the Historic District, church and institutional buildings. In a solid wall, the mortar joints *are* the weather barrier. Water that gets past them has nowhere to go but into the wall.

**Brick veneer.** A single four-inch skin of brick standing on the foundation ledge, tied back to a wood or steel frame with corrugated metal wall ties, with a deliberate air cavity behind it. This is the overwhelming majority of Naperville — the ranches and split-levels of the 1950s through 1970s, the enormous 1980s and 1990s build-out across the 60540, 60563, 60564 and 60565 ZIP codes, and the newer east-side construction. In a veneer wall, the brick is a rain screen. It is *expected* to leak. The system works because water that gets through the brick runs down the back face, lands on through-wall flashing, and exits through **weep holes** in the bottom course.

Here is the sentence that matters:

**A veneer wall does not keep water out. It lets water back out. Repointing that seals the exits turns a working wall into a tank.**

### How to tell which one you have

- Look at a window or door jamb. Veneer usually returns only a few inches into the opening before you hit frame; a solid wall returns the full thickness of the masonry.
- Look at the bottom course, just above grade or above the foundation. Open head joints, small tubes, or rope wicks at regular intervals mean veneer with a drainage cavity.
- Check the age. In Naperville, post-1960 is veneer until proven otherwise.
- Tap the wall and look at the corners for a header course — bricks laid end-on to tie two wythes together. Headers mean solid.

## Where Naperville Joints Actually Fail First

On a veneer home, mortar failure is not random. It concentrates:

- **The bottom two or three courses**, where flashing sits and where splash-back, mulch beds and irrigation keep the brick wet.
- **Under limestone, cast stone and precast sills**, where water sheds off the sill nose and hits the joint directly.
- **Chimney chases and full-height fireplace walls**, which are exposed on three or four sides and dry from none.
- **The weather-facing elevation** — in DuPage County, generally the north and west faces, which see wind-driven rain and stay frozen longest.
- **At the shelf angle**, where a taller veneer wall is carried on steel.

On solid downtown masonry the pattern is different: parapets, chimneys, the top of the wall, and anywhere a gutter, coping or roof edge has been dumping water for decades.

## Risks of Waiting

Open joints are not cosmetic. A failed joint is an opening in the wall, and in Naperville's climate the sequence is predictable: water enters, freezes, expands, and levers the joint wider. On a veneer wall the water also reaches the **wall ties**, which are typically galvanized steel. Corroded ties expand as they rust and eventually stop holding the brick back to the structure. That is how a veneer wall starts to bow — and a bowing veneer wall is a rebuild, not a repointing.

Left long enough, open joints produce spalled brick faces, efflorescence, rust staining below sills and lintels, damp interior walls, and eventually displaced brick. Every one of those is more expensive than the mortar work that would have prevented it.

## Our Tuckpointing Process

No competitor page in this market publishes one. Here is ours.

1. **Diagnose the wall.** Solid or veneer, what the brick is, how hard the existing mortar is, where the flashing and weep holes are, and whether the ties are still doing their job.
2. **Establish the scope by elevation.** Full repointing, spot repointing, or no work — decided wall by wall, not by averaging the house.
3. **Protect the site.** Windows, landscaping, walks, neighboring property and, on multi-unit or commercial work, pedestrian paths.
4. **Cut the joints.** Grind or rake failed mortar back to sound material, typically two to two and a half times the joint width, without nicking the brick edges.
5. **Clean and dampen.** Blow the joints clear and pre-wet the brick so it does not pull the water out of fresh mortar before it can cure.
6. **Match and mix mortar.** Hardness first, then color, then sand and texture — mixed in controlled batches so the wall does not shift shade partway up.
7. **Pack in layers.** Fill in lifts rather than one deep pass, so the joint is dense the whole way back instead of hollow behind a face skin.
8. **Tool to match the original profile.** Concave, weathered, grapevine or flush — profile is most of what makes a repair read as original.
9. **Restore drainage.** Open and clear weep holes, correct anything previously sealed shut, and address flashing where it is accessible.
10. **Clean down and walk it.** Brush and wash the face, then review the finished elevation with the owner.

## Materials and Mortar Matching

The governing rule is simple and it is where most bad tuckpointing goes wrong: **mortar must be softer than the brick it sits against.** Mortar is the sacrificial component. It is supposed to fail first, because a joint can be replaced and a brick face cannot.

- **Type N** — the general-purpose mix for most Naperville veneer and mid-century brick.
- **Type S** — higher strength, appropriate for below-grade, retaining and some structural conditions, and too hard for soft older brick.
- **Type O and lime-rich mixes** — for soft pre-1900 solid masonry downtown, where a modern Portland-heavy mortar would destroy the brick faces.

Colour matching is a mix of sand selection, cement colour, and curing conditions. We match wet-to-cured, not wet-to-wet, because mortar lightens as it dries.
