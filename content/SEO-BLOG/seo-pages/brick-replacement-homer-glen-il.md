## Why Freestanding Masonry Goes First

A wall attached to a house has one exposed face, a warm side, and a roof overhead. A pillar has none of that.

- **Exposed on every face** — it wets and freezes from all directions
- **No heat behind it**, so it never dries from the inside
- **A cap doing all the work.** Once the cap cracks or its joints open, water goes straight into the top of the structure, which is the direction masonry has no answer for
- **Sitting at grade** in the splash zone, buried in plowed snow, and absorbing **de-icing salt** from the drive and the road
- **Nobody inspects it.** People look at their house; they walk past the pillar

Add the mechanism that governs everything here — water enters, freezes, expands roughly nine percent, and forces the masonry apart, dozens of times each winter — and a pillar at the end of a Homer Glen driveway can be structurally spent while the house it belongs to is fine.

## The Signs It Has Gone Past Repair

- **Spalled faces across a continuous area** rather than a few scattered units — once the fired face is off, the softer interior absorbs faster and the failure spreads sideways
- **Brick you can move by hand**, or that sounds hollow when tapped
- **A pillar, pier or wall visibly out of plumb** when sighted along its length
- **A cracked or displaced cap** on freestanding masonry — the single most common root cause we find
- **Step cracks** running diagonally through the joints, which indicate movement rather than weathering
- **White efflorescence** blooming on the face, meaning water is moving through the masonry
- **Mortar you can rake out with a key**, sandy and receded well behind the brick face
- **Debris on the ground** below a pillar or wall — the masonry is already releasing material

The first four generally mean rebuilding. The rest may still be a repointing or spot-repair scope, which is a much smaller job — and worth catching at that stage.
