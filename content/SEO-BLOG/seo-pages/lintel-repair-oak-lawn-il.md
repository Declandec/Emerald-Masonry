## What a Lintel Is and Why It Fails

Brick cannot span an opening on its own. Every window, door and garage opening in a masonry wall has something carrying the brick above it, and in Oak Lawn's post-war housing that something is almost always a **steel angle lintel** — an L-shaped steel bar bearing on the masonry at each side of the opening.

Steel is entirely reliable until it gets wet. Then it corrodes, and corroding steel does something inconvenient: **it expands to several times the volume of the original metal.** Inside a rigid brick wall there is nowhere for that expansion to go, so it goes upward and outward, pushing the brick courses above the opening apart.

That is **rust jacking**, and it is why a lintel failure presents as a horizontal crack along the joint line directly above a window rather than as anything obviously metallic.

## The Progression

1. **Water gets in** — through failed mortar joints, a failed sill, cracked caulking at the window perimeter, or a gutter overflowing above.
2. **The steel starts corroding**, hidden inside the wall.
3. **Rust staining appears** on the brick below the lintel — the first visible sign, and the cheapest moment to act.
4. **The joint above the opening cracks** horizontally as expansion begins lifting brick.
5. **Brick displaces** — courses lift, mortar fails, units loosen.
6. **Section loss in the steel** means the lintel is no longer reliably carrying load.

The whole sequence is driven by water, which is why lintel repair that does not also fix the water source is a repair with a countdown on it.

Oak Lawn's housing stock went up largely between 1950 and 1970 — **brick ranches, Georgians, Cape Cods, split-levels and raised ranches**, with a very high proportion of attached brick garages. Two features of the local stock concentrate the problem:

**Low openings.** Garage-door lintels and basement-level openings sit close to grade, where splash-back and road-salt spray keep them wetter than anything higher on the wall.

## Our Lintel Repair Process

1. **Free on-site assessment** of every opening, identifying which lintels are corroding and where water is entering.
2. **Fix the water source** — joints, sill, caulking, gutters — because otherwise the new steel starts the same clock.

## The Cost of Waiting

Lintel corrosion does not pause. Every winter the steel expands a little more, the crack opens a little wider, and more water reaches the steel through the crack the steel just made. What starts as staining on one window becomes cracked brick on three, and eventually becomes loose masonry above a doorway people walk through daily.

There is also a compounding effect worth knowing: the same water that is rusting your lintels is working on your mortar joints and brick faces at the same time. Lintel staining is often the most visible symptom of a wall-wide water problem.
