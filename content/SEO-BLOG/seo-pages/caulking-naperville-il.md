## The Rule: Sealant Goes in Joints That Are Meant to Move

| | Mortar joints | Sealant joints |
|---|---|---|
| **What they are** | Joints between brick, block and stone units | Expansion, control and perimeter joints |
| **How they behave** | Rigid — they hold the wall together | Flexible — they absorb movement |
| **Correct material** | Matched mortar, [repointed](/services/tuckpointing) | Backer rod plus elastomeric sealant |
| **How they fail** | Erosion, cracking, sandy mortar | Adhesion loss, hardening, splitting |
| **The classic mistake** | Caulked instead of repointed | Filled with no backer rod, or sealed shut when it should drain |

That distinction is not academic. Caulk in a mortar joint is flexible where the wall needs rigidity and impermeable where the wall needs to dry — and it hides deteriorating mortar rather than replacing it. When we find caulked mortar joints, and we find them constantly, the caulk has to come out before the wall can be repointed properly. Our guide to [tuckpointing versus caulking](/blog/tuckpointing-vs-caulking-masonry) covers the distinction in more depth.

### Where sealant genuinely belongs

- Window and door **perimeter joints** where the frame meets masonry
- **Expansion joints** in brick veneer, which absorb the brick's thermal movement
- **Control joints** in block walls
- **Dissimilar-material joints** — masonry to metal panel, precast, EIFS or wood trim
- **Coping and cap joints** on parapets and freestanding walls
- **Penetrations** — vents, conduit, hose bibs, light fixtures, sign anchors
- **Storefront and curtain-wall perimeters** on commercial buildings

## Why Sealant Work Fails Early

**No backer rod.** The single most common defect. Sealant must bond to the two faces of a joint and stay free at the back so it can stretch. Backer rod sets the depth and breaks the back bond. Without it, the bead bonds on three sides and tears the first time the joint moves.

**Wrong bead geometry.** A bead wants roughly twice the width of its depth. Too deep and it is stiff and tears; too shallow and there is not enough material to stretch. The backer rod depth sets this, which is why the rod is not optional.

**Dirty, damp or previously-sealed joint faces.** Sealant bonds to whatever it touches. New sealant applied over failed sealant pulls away with it, usually within a season.

**Wrong chemistry for the substrate.** Urethanes, silicones and hybrids are not interchangeable, and porous stone needs a non-staining product — the wrong one leaves a permanent dark halo along every joint. Our breakdown of [silicone, urethane and hybrid sealants](/blog/masonry-sealants-silicone-urethane-hybrid-caulk) covers the selection logic.

**Sealing before finding the leak.** Sealant is not a diagnostic tool. If water is entering through failed flashing, an open mortar joint above, a cracked sill or a rusting lintel, sealing the perimeter joint only relocates the symptom.

**Sealing what must stay open.** Weep holes are drains, not defects. Caulking them shut turns a brick-veneer wall — designed to let water in and drain it back out — into a wall that holds it.

## Our Sealant Process

1. **Diagnose the actual leak path** before choosing a joint to seal.
2. **Cut out old sealant completely** and remove residue from both joint faces.
3. **Clean and prepare** — dry, sound, dust-free substrate.
4. **Repair the masonry** where joint faces are spalled or surrounding mortar has failed.
5. **Install backer rod** at the correct depth for the joint width.
