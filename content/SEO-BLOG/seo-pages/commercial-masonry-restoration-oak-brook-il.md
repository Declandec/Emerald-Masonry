## Oak Brook Is Not a Historic Masonry Market

Most commercial masonry marketing is built around landmark restoration — terra cotta, century-old load-bearing walls, lime mortar, preservation review. That work is real. It is also almost entirely irrelevant to what stands in Oak Brook.

The village's commercial fabric is **corporate office-park construction, largely 1970s through 1990s**: low- and mid-rise office buildings along the Butterfield and 22nd Street corridors, the Oakbrook Center retail environment, medical and professional offices, and a substantial stock of condominium and townhome associations. Structurally that means:

- **Cavity-wall brick veneer** — a single wythe standing off a steel or concrete frame with an air space and backup wall behind it.
- **Shelf angles** — steel angles bolted to the structure carrying the veneer at each floor line, so the brick is not stacked 60 feet on one foundation.
- **Through-wall flashing** at those angles, at the base and over openings, to catch cavity water and drain it out.
- **Movement joints** — deliberate soft, sealant-filled joints that let the masonry expand and contract.
- **Sealant joints** at windows, panels and transitions, with a service life measured in decades, not centuries.

**Every one of those five fails on a schedule.** None of them is mortar.

## The Mechanism Nobody Explains: Brick Grows

This is the part that makes repeat cracking make sense.

Fired clay brick leaves the kiln bone dry and immediately begins reabsorbing atmospheric moisture. As it does it **expands — permanently and irreversibly.** Thermal movement rides on top of that. Concrete and steel frames, meanwhile, shrink and creep slightly. So the brick skin grows while the structure behind it does not.

That movement has to go somewhere. In a properly detailed building it goes into **movement joints** — vertical soft joints near corners and at spacing, horizontal soft joints beneath each shelf angle. Where those were overspaced, omitted, or filled with hard mortar by a previous contractor, the wall relieves the stress the only way it can: **it cracks** — in the same place every time, because the geometry has not changed.

## Reading Crack Patterns on a Cavity Wall

The location of a crack tells you more than its width does.

| What you see | What it usually means |
| --- | --- |
| Vertical crack a few feet in from a building corner | Missing or overspaced vertical movement joint |
| Horizontal crack line at the same height across an elevation | Shelf angle — corrosion, deflection, or a filled horizontal soft joint |
| Cracks angling from the corners of windows | Stress concentration at openings, often with a missing joint or a corroding [lintel](/services/lintel-repair) |
| Stepped diagonal cracking near the base | Foundation or settlement movement, not veneer movement |
| Bulging or out-of-plane brick | Corroded or failed veneer ties — this is urgent |
| Rust staining at a horizontal line | Shelf angle or lintel steel actively corroding |
| Efflorescence or staining below a floor line | Flashing failed or was omitted; water is exiting where it should not |
| Cracked, hardened or missing sealant in a soft joint | Sealant at end of service life — a maintenance item, until it is not |
| Damp interior finishes at the perimeter | Cavity drainage compromised somewhere above |

**A vertical crack near a corner and a horizontal crack at a floor line are two entirely different repairs.** Treating both as "tuckpointing" is the most expensive mistake made on this stock.

## Risks of Waiting

Water past compromised flashing or an open joint reaches the **veneer ties** and the **shelf angle steel**. Corroding steel expands to several times its volume — rust jacking — cracking masonry from behind and pushing the veneer out of plane, while corroded ties stop restraining the brick at all.

That sequence ends with displaced masonry above tenants, parking and sidewalks. Long before that it produces interior water damage, tenant complaints, and repeated repointing spend that fixes nothing.

## Our Commercial Restoration Process

Most competitors in this market publish none of this. Here is ours.

1. **Close-up survey of every elevation**, from a lift or staging where needed. Distance hides everything that matters.
2. **Map the cracking** against floor lines, corners, openings and joint locations — the pattern is the diagnosis.
3. **Probe selectively** at a suspect shelf angle or flashing line rather than guessing at what is behind the wall.
4. **Check the joints** — where movement joints are, where they should be, where they have been filled with hard mortar — and assess sealant condition and remaining life.
5. **Prioritize into three buckets** — life safety, active water intrusion, cosmetic — and say plainly which is which.
6. **Produce a written scope** by elevation and element, phaseable across reserve years.
7. **Plan access and protection** — lift, swing stage or scaffold, overhead protection, pedestrian routing, tenant and parking coordination.
8. **Execute the repair**: replace or treat corroded steel, restore through-wall flashing with end dams, cut in movement joints where the building needs them, rebuild displaced masonry, replace spalled units, repoint with matched mortar.
9. **Reseal** joints with the correct sealant for the substrate and movement capability, and **document the work** with photos so the next board inherits a record rather than a mystery.

## Materials

Mortar is matched for hardness, colour, sand and texture, and is always softer than the brick it sits against — **Type N** for most veneer conditions, **Type S** only where structural or below-grade conditions warrant it. Replacement ties and anchors are **stainless or hot-dip galvanized**, never bare steel. Through-wall flashing is durable membrane or metal detailed with end dams. Sealants are selected for joint movement capability and substrate, not one tube for the whole building. Replacement brick is matched and blended through the field.
