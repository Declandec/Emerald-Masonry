## New Lenox Is a Young Masonry Town

Most tuckpointing advice is written for century-old buildings — soft brick, lime mortar, solid double-wythe walls, joints eroded a half inch deep across an entire facade. That is real, and it describes a great deal of Chicago and the older inner suburbs.

It does not describe New Lenox.

The village's masonry is overwhelmingly **modern brick veneer** on homes built from roughly the 1980s onward, through the large subdivision growth along the Route 30 and Cedar Road corridors and out toward Lincoln-Way. That means:

- **Dense, machine-made brick** with low absorption compared with century-old common brick
- **Modern Portland-based mortar**, hard and durable by historic standards
- **A drained cavity** behind the brick, with weep holes and flashing designed to let water back out
- **Twenty to forty years of exposure**, not a hundred and twenty

Young brick in a drained wall does not fail uniformly across an elevation. **It fails at details.** And that changes what a competent repointing job actually looks like.

## Where New Lenox Joints Actually Fail

On a typical New Lenox home, mortar failure concentrates in a short, specific list:

- **Under window and door sills.** A sill collects the entire runoff of the opening above and delivers it to one narrow joint. If the sill has lost its slope or drip edge, or the sealant at its ends has failed, that joint takes several times the water the rest of the wall does.
- **The bottom two or three courses.** Splash-back off walks, drives, patios and stoops, plus mulch and grade holding moisture against the wall, plus winter salt.
- **Chimney chases and full-height fireplace walls.** Exposed on three or four sides, with no heated interior behind them and no roof over them. On most modern homes this is the worst masonry on the property.
- **The weather elevation.** In Will County, generally the north and west faces, which see wind-driven rain and stay frozen longest.
- **At the shelf angle**, on taller elevations where veneer is carried on steel.
- **Around penetrations** — hose bibs, vents, conduit, light fixtures and anything anchored into the wall.

Everywhere else, the joints on a thirty-year-old veneer wall are often genuinely fine.

**Which is why the right scope is usually targeted spot repointing, not a whole-house repoint.** A contractor who quotes every elevation without walking every elevation is quoting volume.

## Signs You Need Tuckpointing

- Joints receding noticeably behind the face of the brick
- Mortar that crumbles, powders, or lets a screwdriver sink in — the simplest self-test there is
- Sand or grit collecting on the sill, walk or foundation below a wall
- Open joints or visible gaps, especially under sills and at the chase
- Hairline cracking running through joints in a line
- Efflorescence, the white powdery bloom left when water passes through masonry and evaporates
- Spalled or flaking brick faces, which means water has been getting in for a while
- Damp interior walls, or staining at the top floor near a chimney
- Weep holes that are blocked, buried, or have been mortared shut

## Risks of Waiting

An open joint is a hole in the wall. On a veneer wall, the water that enters does not simply sit in the brick — it reaches the cavity and the **veneer ties**, the corrugated metal straps holding the brick back to the frame. Ties are typically galvanized, and once they corrode they expand, lose capacity, and eventually stop restraining the wall. A veneer wall that has begun to bow is a rebuild, not a repointing.

Before that, open joints produce spalled brick, efflorescence, rust staining below sills and lintels, damp sheathing behind the veneer, and interior finish damage. Repointing a detail early costs a fraction of what any of that costs later.

## Our Tuckpointing Process

1. **Walk every elevation** and record which details need work and which do not.
2. **Test the existing mortar** for hardness so the new mix is matched rather than guessed.
3. **Scope by elevation and detail** — full repointing, spot repointing, or none.
4. **Protect** windows, landscaping, walks and neighbouring property.
5. **Cut the joints** — grind or rake failed mortar back to sound material, roughly two to two and a half times the joint width, without nicking brick edges.
6. **Clean and dampen** so the brick does not pull water out of the fresh mortar before it cures.
7. **Match and mix** in controlled batches so colour does not shift partway up an elevation.
8. **Pack in lifts**, not one deep pass, so the joint is dense the whole way back.
9. **Tool to the existing profile** — concave, weathered or flush.
10. **Confirm drainage** — weep holes open, flashing intact, nothing sealed that should not be.
11. **Clean down and walk the finished elevation** with the owner.

## Materials

**Type N** is the general-purpose mix for most New Lenox veneer. Type S is stronger and appropriate for below-grade and some structural conditions, but it is harder than many brick and is not a default. The governing rule holds regardless of the building's age: **mortar must be softer than the brick it sits against**, because mortar is the sacrificial component and a joint can be replaced while a brick face cannot.

On spot repointing, matching matters more than on a full job, because the new work sits directly beside the old. We match colour against cured mortar, match sand and texture, and match the tooled profile exactly.
