## A sill has two jobs, and it quietly stops doing both

A stone sill under a window is not decoration. It is a **water-management device** with two functions:

1. **Pitch** — the top surface, the *wash*, slopes outward so runoff leaves rather than pools.
2. **Drip edge** — a groove or projecting lip on the underside so water falling off the front is thrown clear instead of curling back along the bottom face and into the wall.

Both fail slowly and invisibly.

**Pitch is lost** when the sill settles, when a joint beneath it fails, when the stone erodes flat over a century, or when a previous "repair" troweled mortar across the wash to fill a crack and levelled it in the process.

**The drip edge is lost** almost every time someone patches a sill without knowing what the groove is for. A bead of caulk or a smear of mortar across the underside fills the drip, and from that day forward water runs along the bottom of the stone and straight into the masonry.

Here is the consequence, and it is the single most useful diagnostic on a La Grange house:

> **When the brick directly beneath a window is visibly worse than the brick anywhere else on the wall, the sill above it is almost always the cause — even when the sill itself looks fine.**

The sill stopped shedding water and started delivering it. Everything below is now on the receiving end of every rainfall that hits that window.

## What we find on La Grange stone

**Spalling and delamination.** The face of the stone flaking off in sheets or crumbling to a sugary surface. Usually moisture plus salts plus freeze-thaw, and very often accelerated by a previous hard-mortar repointing or an abrasive cleaning.

**Cracked sills.** Often straight through at mid-span, frequently over a corroded steel lintel below that has expanded and lifted.

**Open and eroded joints** at the stone perimeter, where water enters directly behind the unit.

**Hard mortar in a soft wall.** The most damaging thing a well-meaning contractor does to historic La Grange masonry. **Mortar must always be softer than the unit it holds.** Portland-heavy modern mortar in a soft limestone or soft old brick wall makes the joint stronger than the stone, so movement and freeze-thaw stress that used to relieve itself harmlessly in the joint now goes through the face of the stone instead.

**Abrasive-cleaning damage.** Sandblasting and high-pressure washing strip the weathered outer skin off limestone permanently. What is left is the soft, open interior of the stone, exposed directly to weather from then on. The building looks brighter for a season and deteriorates faster for the rest of its life. There is no putting that skin back.

**Failed patches.** Cement patches on limestone are harder and less permeable than the stone, so they trap moisture behind them and pop off, taking sound stone with them.

## Repair before replace

Good stone is worth saving, and most of it can be.

| Method | When it is right | What it involves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Re-pitch and restore the drip** | The stone is sound but no longer sheds water | Correcting the wash, re-establishing a drip groove or edge, repointing the perimeter |
| **Patch with matched material** | Localized spalling or a small failed area on a sound unit | Cutting back to sound stone, applying a compatible matched repair material |
| **Dutchman repair** | A defined failed area on an otherwise good stone | Cutting a clean square pocket and setting a matched piece of stone into it, so the original unit stays |
| **Reset** | The stone is intact but has shifted or lost bearing | Removing, correcting the bed and any steel beneath, resetting to correct pitch |
| **Replace** | The stone has genuinely lost structure or bearing | New matched limestone or cast stone, cut to the original profile |

The order matters. A contractor whose first answer to a cracked sill is replacement has skipped four cheaper, more appropriate options and thrown away a piece of the building.

## Check the steel underneath

On a great many La Grange buildings the sill or the stone lintel sits on, over, or next to **steel** — and corroding steel expands with enormous force, which is what cracks stone across the middle and lifts it out of plane.

If a sill is cracked at mid-span, if there is **rust staining** running down the masonry, or if a horizontal crack has opened in the courses above the opening, the stone is the symptom. Repairing it without addressing the steel means doing it again. That is why sill work and [lintel repair](/services/lintel-repair) are so often scoped together.

## Our process

1. **Assess every opening on the elevation**, not just the one that prompted the call — sills fail in batches because they are the same age, the same stone and the same exposure.
2. **Check pitch and drip** on each, by eye and by hand.
3. **Look for the steel** and for rust staining above and below.
4. **Establish the match** — stone, repair material, and a soft lime-based mortar matched for color, texture and profile, on a physical sample.
5. **Choose the least invasive method** that genuinely restores the element.
6. **Correct the water path first** — pitch, drip, joints, and anything overhead delivering water to the opening.
7. **Repoint the surrounding masonry** where the stone's failure has opened joints.
8. **Cure and protect** the work rather than letting it flash-dry.
