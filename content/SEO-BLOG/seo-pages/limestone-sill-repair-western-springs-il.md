Here is the thing about a stone sill: **it has exactly one job, and when it stops doing that job the damage shows up somewhere else.**

A sill exists to catch water running down the window and throw it clear of the wall below. It is sloped for that reason, and it projects past the wall face for that reason. When the slope flattens through erosion, the joints open, or the unit cracks, it stops throwing water clear and starts **delivering it straight down the brick**.

Which is why homeowners in Western Springs call us about spalling brick under a window and end up needing sill work. **The brick is the symptom. The sill is the cause.**

## How Limestone Fails

Limestone is sedimentary, laid down in visible **bedding planes** — horizontal layers that are planes of relative weakness. It is durable and handsome, and it is **porous**, so it takes on water.

In a Cook County winter that absorbed water freezes inside the stone, expands, and splits it **along those layers**. Four failure modes follow:

- **Delamination and spalling** — the face shedding in sheets or shells following the bedding.
- **Cracking** — often across the middle of a sill span, or radiating from a bearing point.
- **Surface erosion** — the slope flattening and the crisp nose profile rounding over. **This is the quiet one**, because the sill still looks intact while it has stopped shedding water.
- **Displacement** — a sill dropping or tilting because the mortar bed beneath it failed.

**Horizontal stone always fails first**, because it holds water rather than shedding it. Sills, water tables, steps and caps take the worst of it.

## Signs Your Western Springs Home Needs Stone Repair

- Sills **flaking, shelling or crumbling** at the face or the nose.
- **Cracks** running through a sill, lintel or trim unit.
- **Brick below a window streaked, stained or spalling** — the strongest single indicator.
- **Open or missing mortar joints** where stone meets brick.
- **Failed or missing caulking** at the sill-to-window joint.
- Stone that has **dropped, tilted or shifted** out of plane.
- **A sill that no longer looks sloped** — check with a level; a flat sill is a failed sill.
- **Rust staining** on the stone, indicating corroding embedded metal or a steel lintel behind it.
- **Water staining or damage inside** below a window.
- **Eroded carved detail** on trim and porch stonework.

## Repair Before Replacement

Replacement is occasionally right, but it is a last resort — it destroys original material, costs more, disturbs the surrounding masonry, and never matches a century-old wall perfectly.

| Approach | What it involves | When it is right |
|---|---|---|
| **Repointing and recaulking** | Restoring joints and sealant around sound stone | Stone intact, joints failed |
| **Patching / composite repair** | Matched cementitious patch rebuilding lost material | Localized spalling and edge loss |
| **Dutchman repair** | Cutting out the damaged area, fitting matched new stone | Significant local damage, unit otherwise sound |
| **Repinning / resetting** | Removing, rebedding and anchoring a shifted unit | Displaced but intact stone |
| **Full replacement** | New matched limestone unit | Structurally failed or broken units |

## Why the Mortar Matters at a Stone Joint

**The governing rule: mortar must be softer than the masonry it holds.** At a stone joint, a hard modern Portland mix concentrates stress **at the stone face** rather than in the joint — and on porous, layered limestone that means accelerated delamination. On Western Springs's pre-1920 homes we use lime-based mixes deliberately, not as a stylistic preference.
