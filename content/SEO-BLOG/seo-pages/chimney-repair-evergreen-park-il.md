Here is the single most useful thing we can tell an Evergreen Park homeowner: **when water shows up near a chimney, the chimney itself is often not the problem.** It is very frequently the **flashing** — the metal that seals the joint where the stack passes through the roof.

That matters here more than in most places, because Evergreen Park's housing is dominated by 1940s–50s brick bungalows, ranches, Georgians and Cape Cods with comparatively **low-pitch roofs**. A low-pitch roof drains slowly, holds snow and ice longer, and drives water sideways into the chimney-to-roof joint rather than shedding it fast. Flashing on those roofs works harder and fails sooner than the masonry above it.

We have been called out to quote chimney rebuilds and found perfectly sound stacks with sixty-year-old flashing that somebody tar-patched twice.

## The Three Water Paths — and Which One Is Yours

Water gets into a chimney assembly in exactly three places. Sealing the wrong one changes nothing, which is why so many "repaired" chimneys keep leaking.

| Path | What it is | Telltale sign |
|---|---|---|
| **Flashing** | Metal sealing the chimney-to-roof joint | Ceiling or attic stains near the chase; rusted, lifted or tar-patched metal at the roofline |
| **Crown** | Sloped concrete slab across the top of the masonry | Visible cracking on top of the stack; staining running down from the top |
| **Joints / brick** | The mortar and units of the stack itself | White efflorescence, flaking faces, joints you can rake with a screwdriver |

A proper inspection determines which is active — often more than one — before anything is scoped.

## What Each Part Actually Does

Most pages list these as service bullets without ever explaining them. They are three different components with three different jobs.

- **Flashing** is metal, not masonry. Step flashing tucks under the shingles course by course; counter flashing laps over it and into a mortar joint in the chimney. Together they bridge a joint between two materials that move independently. When it fails, water enters *below* the chimney top entirely.
- **The crown** is the sloped concrete slab covering the top of the brickwork, with an overhang and a drip edge so runoff throws clear of the stack. Hairline cracks open under freeze-thaw, water gets into the masonry from above, and the crack widens every winter.
- **The cap** is the metal cover over the flue opening. It stops rain, snow and animals dropping straight down the flue. On unused or decorative chimneys, an open flue is simply a funnel.

A chimney needs all three intact. Fixing one while another is failing buys a season.

## Signs Your Evergreen Park Chimney Needs Work

- **Ceiling or attic water stains** near the chimney chase — start with flashing.
- **Rusted, lifted or tar-patched flashing** at the roofline. Tar is somebody's temporary fix from years ago, not a repair.
- **A cracked or crumbling crown** on top of the stack.
- **White efflorescence staining** on the brick — water is moving through the masonry.
- **Spalling brick faces**, flaking or popping, worst on the north and west sides.
- **Mortar joints that rake out** under a screwdriver or have receded behind the brick.
- **A visible lean**, or a gap opening between the chimney and the house.
- **Brick or mortar debris** in the gutters, on the roof, or in the gangway.
- **Daylight visible** through the stack from the attic.

## Mortar Matching on Post-War Brick

Evergreen Park's chimneys were largely laid with **Portland-based mortar** and denser machine-made brick. The governing rule never changes — **mortar must be softer than the brick it holds**, so the joints take the stress instead of the brick faces — but the mix that satisfies it on a 1952 bungalow is not the mix for a pre-1930 stack. Too soft and it erodes out early; too hard and it spalls the faces. We match hardness first, then color, sand and joint profile, using **Type N** and lime-modified mixes as the stack requires.
