> On a Chicago Ridge apartment or condo building, a lintel is never one lintel. It is the first visible failure in a set of identical steel members installed the same week, and the right response is a survey of every opening, not a patch over one.

## What a lintel is, and why steel ones fail

A **lintel** is the beam that carries the masonry over an opening. On buildings of this era it is almost always a **steel angle** — an L-shaped piece of steel with its horizontal leg tucked under the brick and its ends bearing on the masonry either side.

Steel is fine as long as it stays dry. It rarely does. Water running down the face of the wall, and water travelling inside the cavity behind the brick, collects at the head of each opening. Without **through-wall flashing** and open **weep holes** to send it back out, it sits on the steel.

Steel that rusts expands — corrosion occupies several times the volume of the metal it replaces. That growth, called **rust jacking**, lifts the brick above the lintel, opens the bed joint, and starts the familiar crack.

## Signs your building needs lintel replacement

From the parking lot, a property manager or board member can usually spot it:

- **Step cracks** climbing diagonally from the corners of window and patio door heads.
- A **horizontal crack or open joint** along the course directly above the steel.
- **Orange-brown rust streaks** running down the brick from window heads.
- Steel visibly **swollen, layered or flaking**, or bowed downward in the middle of a long span.
- Brick above the opening that is **displaced, bulging or loose**.
- Windows or sliders that have started to **bind** at the head.
- **Interior water** at window heads after rain, especially on south and west elevations.

Any one of these on several openings is a reason to have every lintel on the building looked at.

## Risks of waiting on lintel replacement in Chicago Ridge

Rust jacking does not pause. Each winter, water in the open joint above the steel freezes and widens it, the steel keeps corroding, and the crack grows. What starts as two or three courses to relay becomes a section of wall over the opening that has to come down.

The other risk is overhead. Over a patio, a building entrance or a storefront door on Ridgeland Avenue, loosened brick above a failed lintel is a falling-masonry hazard to residents and customers — and that liability sits with the owner or association, not the contractor who has not yet been called.

## Our lintel replacement process

1. **Survey every opening.** We record each lintel by elevation and unit, grade its condition, and photograph it, so the board or owner sees the whole picture rather than one complaint.
2. **Written scope.** Openings are grouped into replace now, replace next phase, and clean/prime/re-flash, with quantities and an access plan.
3. **Resident and tenant notice.** We give the property manager a schedule by stack and date so units can be told which day their window is affected.
4. **Shore.** Where the brick above needs temporary support, it is shored before anything is removed.
5. **Remove brick and old steel.** Masonry above the opening is taken down carefully, brick salvaged for reuse where it is sound.
6. **Install new steel.** New hot-dip galvanized steel angle, correctly sized, with proper bearing at each end.
7. **Flash and weep.** Through-wall flashing with end dams over the new lintel, and open weep holes at the head so the cavity can drain.
8. **Rebuild and match.** Brick relaid in matched mortar, toothed into the surrounding wall, the opening closed up and weathertight at the end of each day.
9. **Seal the perimeter.** New sealant where the window frame meets the masonry, so the joint we just rebuilt is not fed by a failed caulk line.
