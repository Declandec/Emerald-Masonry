**Spalling almost never starts where you happen to notice it.** It starts where the water goes.

That single idea changes how a brick repair should be scoped. A homeowner points at a patch of flaking brick on the front of the house; a mason looks up and sideways for the gutter seam, the failed sill, the downspout, the shaded elevation that never dries. The damaged patch is the **output**. The water path is the **input**, and repairing the output while the input keeps running is how people pay for the same repair twice.

## Reading Where the Water Goes

Damage clusters in predictable places, and each pattern names its own cause.

| Where the spalling is | What is almost certainly causing it |
|---|---|
| **A vertical band down one section of wall** | A leaking or overflowing gutter seam directly above |
| **Directly under a window** | A failed sill that has lost its slope or its joints |
| **The bottom two or three courses** | Splash-back at grade, plus road salt in winter |
| **Around a corner, in a diagonal line** | Step cracking from soil movement or a lintel below |
| **Streaks below an opening, orange-tinged** | A corroding steel lintel bleeding rust |
| **A whole shaded elevation, evenly** | Slow drying — the wall never gets out of the wet |
| **A band matching a past repointing** | Mortar that was too hard for the brick |

**Walk your house during a heavy rain once.** You will learn more in ten minutes about why your brick is failing than from any amount of looking at the brick.

## Mortar Failure vs. Brick Failure

The distinction that determines the whole scope:

**Mortar failure is repointing. Brick failure is replacement.**

Mortar is the sacrificial layer — deliberately softer than the brick, so it absorbs movement and moisture stress and wears out first. Replacing mortar is cheap; replacing brick is not. When only the joints have gone, [tuckpointing](/services/tuckpointing) restores the wall.

Once water has been entering long enough to saturate the **brick units themselves**, freezing inside them breaks the face off in layers. That is **spalling**, and a spalled brick cannot be brought back — it has to be cut out and replaced with a match.

## Glen Ellyn's Three Eras

The village spans an unusually wide range for its size, and each era needs a different brick and a different mortar.

**Pre-1930** — the streets around the downtown core, Lake Ellyn and the historic neighborhoods, with Victorians, Foursquares, Tudors and 1920s brick. **Soft brick, lime mortar.** These walls need lime-based or **Type O** mixes; a hard modern mortar spalls the faces within a few winters.

**Post-war** — the mid-century blocks, with denser machine-made brick and Portland-based mortar. **Type N** is usually correct.

**Modern veneer** — later subdivisions, where the failure points are ties, flashing and **weep holes** rather than the field brick.

## Matching: Four Things, Not Two

A replacement brick has to match on size, color and texture — and also on **absorption and hardness**. Get the first three right and the last two wrong, and the repair looks perfect on day one, then behaves differently from the wall around it through every wet-freeze cycle.

**On pre-1940 Glen Ellyn masonry, salvaged brick is usually the better answer than new** — not for authenticity but for physics. Salvaged units from the same era share the original's softness and absorption.

## Signs Your Glen Ellyn Brick Needs Repair

- **Spalling faces** — flaking, shelling or crumbling.
- **Cracked brick**, particularly cracks running through units rather than following joints.
- **Loose brick** that moves under hand pressure.
- **Step cracks** climbing diagonally through the mortar joints.
- **Rust staining** below a window, door or garage opening.
- **Receded or sandy mortar joints** that rake out under a screwdriver.
- **Efflorescence** — white chalky bloom proving water is moving through.
- **A bulge or bow** in the wall plane — structural, needing evaluation rather than a patch.
- **Brick debris** collecting at the base of the wall.

## What Waiting Costs

Spalling is progressive. Once a brick loses its fired face, the porous body underneath absorbs water far faster, so that unit deteriorates more quickly and starts wetting its neighbors. A dozen brick becomes fifty; fifty becomes a section rebuild. And because spalling means water is already inside the wall, the steel lintels and ties in there are corroding on the same timeline.
